Unit 1 · Overview

Introduction to Psychology

What psychology is, where its main schools of thought came from, what psychologists study today, and the careers a psychology degree leads to.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

Three puzzling cases

The chapter opens with three real people whose experiences raise the questions psychology tries to answer.

Memory

Clive Wearing

A skilled musician who lost the ability to form new memories after falling ill at 46. He can still play the piano perfectly but cannot recall a meal from an hour earlier (Sacks, 2007).

Perception

James Wannerton

Tastes the sounds of words. His former girlfriend’s name tastes like rhubarb (Mundasad, 2013).

Psychological disorder

John Nash

Nobel Prize-winning mathematician who, while at MIT, believed the New York Times held coded messages for him, heard voices and was later diagnosed with schizophrenia (O’Connor & Robertson, 2002).

The guiding questions: why do people have experiences like these, how does the brain work, and how do the brain’s internal processes connect to outward behavior?

Unit 1 · Section 1.1

What Is Psychology?

Psychology is a science: it builds knowledge about the mind and behavior from measurable evidence.

Learning objectives

  • Define psychology
  • Understand the merits of an education in psychology

Definition

Psychology is the scientific study of the mind and behavior. It asks questions such as what creativity is, what prejudice and discrimination are, and what consciousness is. The suffix -ology means “scientific study of.”

The scientific method

Psychologists gain knowledge with the scientific method. A researcher who wonders how or why something happens proposes a hypothesis, a tentative explanation of it. The hypothesis should fit within a theory: a broad explanation of some part of the natural world that evidence has supported consistently over time. A theory is our best current understanding of that part of the world.

The researcher then tests the hypothesis through observation or an experiment, and publishes or presents the results so that others can replicate them or build on them.

Measurable evidence

Science deals with what can be perceived and objectively measured, so psychological science is empirical. An empirical method acquires knowledge through observation, including experiments, rather than through logical argument or past authorities alone.

The text’s example: you cannot test whether a bird sings because it is happy, because a bird’s happiness cannot be measured. You could study the bird’s brain state instead. People, however, can tell you whether they sing because they are happy.

Since no one can read another person’s mind, psychologists study mental processes indirectly and draw inferences from three kinds of data:

  • Observable behavior
  • Biological indicators
  • Self-reports

A young discipline

Psychology became an academic discipline of its own only in the late 1800s. Before that, the workings of the mind belonged to philosophy. Today psychology is both a natural science, because all behavior is biological at its roots, and a social science, because behavior is shaped by our interactions with others.

Why study psychology?

Psychology is one of the most popular majors on U.S. campuses (Johnson & Lubin, 2011). About 6 percent of all U.S. bachelor’s degrees are in psychology (U.S. Department of Education, 2016). Former psychology majors include Mark Zuckerberg, Jon Stewart, Natalie Portman and Wes Craven (Halonen, 2011).

A psychology education builds:

  • Critical thinking: actively applying a set of skills to understand and evaluate information, including its reliability and usefulness. It involves skepticism, recognizing your own biases, logical thinking, asking appropriate questions and making observations.
  • Training in the scientific method.
  • Communication skills (American Psychological Association, 2011).

Together these raise scientific literacy, the ability to judge the many sources of information we meet. Students also learn how biology, environment and experience interact to shape behavior, and come to appreciate the diversity among individuals and across cultures.

Unit 1 · Section 1.2

History of Psychology

Psychology’s experimental roots date to the 19th century. Wilhelm Wundt and William James are generally credited with founding it as a science separate from philosophy; later schools of thought each shifted what the field studied.

Learning objectives

  • Understand the importance of Wundt and James in the development of psychology
  • Appreciate Freud’s influence on psychology
  • Understand the basic tenets of Gestalt psychology
  • Appreciate the role behaviorism played in psychology’s history
  • Understand the basic tenets of humanism
  • Understand how the cognitive revolution shifted the focus back to the mind

Wundt and structuralism

Wilhelm Wundt (1832–1920), a German scientist, was the first person called a psychologist. He saw psychology as the scientific study of conscious experience: identifying its components and how they combine.

  • Introspection (his “internal perception”): examining your own conscious experience as objectively as possible. Wundt used instruments such as reaction-time devices and called his approach experimental introspection.
  • Voluntarism: people have free will and should know the intentions of an experiment they take part in.
  • In his Leipzig lab (1879), a subject reacted to a light, image or sound by pushing a button, and Wundt timed the response to one-thousandth of a second.

His student Edward Titchener developed structuralism, which focused on the contents of mental processes rather than their function. The weakness: introspection stayed highly subjective, and different people seldom agreed.

Functionalism

William James, the first American psychologist, together with John Dewey and Charles Sanders Peirce, built functional psychology on Darwin’s theory of evolution by natural selection. Natural selection produces adaptation: a trait, including behavior, exists because it served survival and reproduction.

Functionalism therefore asked how mental activities help an organism fit its environment. It also studied the whole mind rather than its separate parts. James used introspection but added objective measures: recording devices, the concrete products of mental activity, and anatomy and physiology.

Freud and psychoanalytic theory

Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), an Austrian neurologist, studied patients with “hysteria” (an old diagnosis, mostly given to women, for physical and emotional symptoms with no apparent physical cause) and neurosis. He traced their problems to the unconscious mind, a store of feelings and urges we are unaware of, reachable through dream analysis, the first words that come to mind, and slips of the tongue.

Psychoanalytic theory stresses the unconscious and early childhood experience; it dominated clinical psychology for decades. Freud popularized (but did not invent) psychoanalysis, the talking method still in use. Many of his ideas are controversial, but Drew Westen (1998) argues later research supports his broad ideas, such as the importance of childhood experience and of unconscious motives. Modern versions of his approach have been shown to be effective (Knekt et al., 2008; Shedler, 2010).

Gestalt psychology

Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler fled Nazi Germany for the United States and introduced Gestalt principles there. Gestalt roughly means “the form in its whole”: a sensory experience can be broken into parts, but we respond to how the parts relate as a whole. A song is made of notes, yet we perceive its melody, rhythm and harmony. This view contradicted Wundt’s structuralism.

Forced to abandon much of their work, and overshadowed by behaviorism, they had less influence in the U.S. than in Germany. Their ideas still shape research on sensation and perception and later fed into humanism’s focus on the whole person.

Behaviorism

Some researchers doubted that inner experience could be studied scientifically and studied only behavior, the observable outcome of mental processes.

  • Ivan Pavlov (1849–1936), a Russian physiologist, studied the conditioned reflex. Dogs salivate to food; after a sound is repeatedly paired with food, the sound alone triggers salivation. This is classical conditioning.
  • John B. Watson (1878–1958), the father of behaviorism, worked at Johns Hopkins. He considered the study of consciousness flawed and shifted psychology toward observing and controlling behavior: behaviorism. Behaviorists often used animal models, assuming the findings partly apply to humans.
  • B. F. Skinner (1904–1990) studied how consequences shape behavior through reinforcement and punishment. His operant conditioning chamber (Skinner box) isolates an animal with a lever or button that can deliver food or a noise.

Behaviorism dominated experimental psychology for decades and helped establish psychology as a science through objective experimentation. It lives on in behavioral and cognitive-behavioral therapy and classroom behavior modification.

Humanism

Some psychologists objected to Freud’s pessimism and determinism (all actions driven by the unconscious) and to behaviorism’s reductionism (oversimplifying) and its own determinism (behavior fixed by genes and environment). Humanism instead stresses personal control, intentionality and the potential for good innate in all humans.

Abraham Maslow (1908–1970) proposed a hierarchy of needs: once basic survival needs are met, higher needs motivate behavior, ending in self-actualization, reaching your full potential.

Carl Rogers (1902–1987) developed client-centered therapy, in which the client, not the therapist, leads the session. The therapist provides three things:

  • Unconditional positive regard
  • Genuineness
  • Empathy

Unconditional positive regard means accepting clients as they are, whatever they say. Humanistic research has been largely qualitative, though quantitative work exists on happiness, self-concept, meditation and therapy outcomes (Friedman, 2008).

The cognitive revolution

By the 1950s, linguistics, neuroscience and computer science renewed scientific interest in the mind (Miller, 2003). Noam Chomsky (1928–), an American linguist, was especially influential: he argued psychology had to study mental functioning again to explain behavior. In 1967 Ulric Neisser published the first textbook called Cognitive Psychology.

The revolution reconnected American and European psychologists (Europe had been less behaviorist) and joined psychology with anthropology, linguistics, computer science and neuroscience in the interdisciplinary cognitive sciences.

Feminist, multicultural and cross-cultural psychology

Early psychology was dominated by Western, White, male academics, and their biases shaped the field. Until the 1960s it was largely “womanless” (Crawford & Marecek, 1989): few women practiced and most research subjects were men.

Feminist psychology

Naomi Weisstein’s 1968 article criticized male psychologists for building a psychology of women from cultural bias without careful experiments. Feminist approaches since then rediscover women’s contributions, study gender differences and question male bias in science.

Results from WEIRD societies (Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic) have been wrongly applied to everyone else; people differ by culture in perception, cooperation and moral reasoning (Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan, 2010). Multicultural psychologists study diverse groups within one country; cross-cultural psychologists compare countries.

  • Francis Cecil Sumner: first African American psychology PhD in the U.S. (1920); founded Howard University’s psychology program.
  • George I. Sanchez: showed that language and cultural barriers in intelligence tests denied Mexican American children equal opportunity; challenged segregated schooling.
  • Mamie Phipps Clark and Kenneth Clark: their doll-preference studies with African American children were instrumental in Brown v. Board of Education; they opened the first child guidance center in Harlem.

Women in psychology

  • Margaret Floy Washburn: first woman awarded a psychology doctorate (1894); wrote The Animal Mind, the field’s standard text for over 20 years.
  • Mary Whiton Calkins: mentored by William James; Harvard refused her the PhD because she was a woman. Studied memory (primacy and recency).
  • Mary Cover Jones: removed a fear of rabbits in “Little Peter,” a sequel to Watson’s Little Albert study (Jones, 1924).
  • Martha Bernal: first Latina psychology doctorate (1962); researched Mexican American children.
  • Inez Beverly Prosser: first African American woman awarded the PhD (1933, University of Cincinnati).

Unit 1 · Section 1.3

Contemporary Psychology

Today’s psychology draws on every historical perspective and spans many specialties, from the nervous system to the courtroom.

Learning objectives

  • Appreciate the diversity of interests and foci within psychology
  • Understand basic interests and applications in each described area
  • Know some major concepts and important figures in each area

Professional organizations

The American Psychological Association (APA) represents psychologists in the United States and is the largest organization of psychologists in the world. Its mission is to advance and spread psychological knowledge for the betterment of people. It has 54 divisions, ranging from the psychology of religion and spirituality to exercise and sport psychology to behavioral neuroscience. Its first president, G. Stanley Hall, taught at Wilberforce University (an HBCU), earned his PhD under William James and became the first president of Clark University.

The Association for Psychological Science (APS) was founded in 1988 to advance psychology’s scientific orientation, after disagreements between the scientific and clinical branches of the APA. It publishes five research journals.

Groups for psychologists of particular ethnic or racial backgrounds include the National Latina/o Psychological Association (NLPA), the Asian American Psychological Association (AAPA), the Association of Black Psychologists (ABPsi) and the Society of Indian Psychologists (SIP).

Biological and evolutionary psychology

Biopsychology studies how biology influences behavior, especially how the nervous system’s structure and function generate it. Researchers combine methods from psychology and physiology, and topics range from sensory and motor systems to sleep, ingestive and reproductive behavior, plasticity and the biology of psychological disorders. This interdisciplinary field is neuroscience, of which biopsychology is one part (Carlson, 2013).

Biopsychology looks at immediate causes; evolutionary psychology seeks the ultimate biological causes of behavior. It traces back to Darwin’s The Descent of Man (1871) and The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals (1872). To evolve by natural selection, a behavior must have a significant genetic cause, so it should appear in all human cultures. Researchers predict a behavior from evolutionary theory, then test the prediction. Buss (1989), surveying 37 cultures, found women valued earning potential more and men valued youth and attractiveness more, broadly as predicted.

Limits of the evolutionary approach

  • Matching a prediction is not strong evidence of adaptation unless the behavior is shown to be partly genetic and not entirely cultural (Endler, 1986).
  • Traits evolved under ancient conditions we understand poorly, and need not be adaptive today.

Sensation, perception and cognition

Sensation and perception research is interdisciplinary, covering both the physiology of the senses and our experience of what they deliver. Perception is more than the sum of sensations: the same input can look like a duck or a rabbit, and what we perceive depends on attention, past experience and culture.

Cognitive psychology, spurred by the cognitive revolution, studies cognitions (thoughts) and how they relate to experience and action. Its breadth and many collaborators led to the term cognitive science (Miller, 2003).

Developmental psychology

Developmental psychology studies development across the lifespan: physical maturation, but also cognitive skills, moral reasoning and social behavior. Jean Piaget showed that very young infants lack object permanence, the understanding that objects still exist when hidden. The age at which it appears is debated (Munakata et al., 1997).

Early researchers studied change up to adulthood. Interest is now growing in later life, reflecting aging populations.

Personality psychology

Personality psychology studies the patterns of thought and behavior that make each person unique. Early theorists included Freud, Maslow and Gordon Allport. Freud proposed psychosexual stages, in which erogenous zones migrate from the oral to the anal to the phallic to the genital; adult personality results from resolving the conflicts of each stage. The idea was controversial and hard to test (Person, 1980).

Modern research is more quantitative: it identifies and measures traits and asks how they interact with situations. A personality trait is a relatively consistent pattern of thought and behavior. The Big Five (Five Factor model) dimensions are each fairly stable across the lifespan and influenced by genetics.

Social psychology

Social psychology studies how we interact with and relate to others: how we explain our own behavior versus others’, prejudice, attraction, resolving conflict, and how being among people changes our behavior and thinking.

Milgram’s obedience study

After the 1961 trial of Nazi war criminal Adolf Eichmann, most psychologists assumed few people would inflict severe pain simply because they were ordered to. Stanley Milgram tested this: nearly two-thirds of participants delivered what they believed were lethal shocks when instructed by an authority figure in a lab coat, even though they had been paid just for showing up and could have withdrawn. No one was actually hurt; the “victim” was a research confederate, someone working for the researcher while posing as a participant.

Studies like this, involving deception and possible emotional harm, led to ethical guidelines that discourage deception unless it can be shown harmless and generally require informed consent.

Applied areas

Industrial-organizational (I-O) psychology

Applies psychological theory and research to work settings: personnel management, organizational structure and workplace environment, such as helping businesses hire well and raise productivity. I-O psychologists also research behavior in these settings (Riggio, 2013).

Health psychology

Studies how health is affected by the interaction of biological, psychological and sociocultural factors, the biopsychosocial model. Health psychologists work through public policy, education, intervention and research (MacDonald, 2013).

Sport and exercise psychology

Studies motivation, performance anxiety and the effects of sport and exercise on mental and emotional wellbeing, plus mental and physical performance under demanding conditions such as firefighting, military operations, artistic performance and surgery.

Clinical and counseling psychology

Clinical psychology diagnoses and treats psychological disorders and other problematic behavior; it is mainly applied, though some clinicians do research. Counseling psychology focuses on emotional, social, vocational and health outcomes in psychologically healthy people. Freud (psychodynamic therapists) and especially Rogers (client-centered therapy) shaped clinical practice, as did behaviorism and the cognitive revolution through behavioral, cognitive and cognitive-behavioral therapy. Clinical psychology gets the most media attention, so people often wrongly assume all psychology is clinical.

Forensic psychology

Forensic psychology applies psychology to the justice system: assessing competency to stand trial and a defendant’s state of mind, consulting on child custody, sentencing and treatment, and advising on eyewitness and children’s testimony. Forensic psychologists usually serve as expert witnesses and must understand the law. They also help with jury selection and witness preparation. Criminal profilers are a small fraction of the field.

Unit 1 · Section 1.4

Careers in Psychology

The degree you earn largely decides where you can work: doctorates for university faculty and licensed clinical practice, master’s and bachelor’s degrees for a wide range of other careers.

Learning objectives

  • Understand educational requirements for careers in academic settings
  • Understand the demands of a career in an academic setting
  • Understand career options outside of academic settings

Academic careers

A career at a 4-year college or university requires a doctorate for some specialties and at least a master’s degree for others. In most areas this means a PhD, a doctor of philosophy. “Philosophy” here does not mean the subject; it refers to the many disciplines housed in a traditional college of liberal arts and sciences.

PhD requirements vary by country and school, but candidates usually complete a dissertation, a long research paper (or set of published articles) on their doctoral research. In the U.S. it is generally defended before a committee of expert reviewers before the degree is conferred.

Faculty divide their time among teaching, research and service to the institution and profession; the balance differs greatly between schools.

Other academic roles

  • Adjunct faculty and instructors teach extra courses. They usually hold an advanced degree and often have a primary career outside academia, or lack the doctorate most 4-year schools require and want teaching experience.
  • 2-year colleges: many instructors have master’s degrees; some PhDs teach there too.
  • Research-only positions: for PhDs who prefer research to teaching, mostly at large research universities.
  • Postdoctoral training programs: in some areas, new PhDs complete one or two before applying for faculty jobs, developing their research and skills under supervision.

Becoming a clinician

Future clinical psychologists can earn a PsyD (doctor of psychology) instead of a PhD. PsyD programs put less emphasis on research skills and more on applying psychology in clinical settings (Norcross & Castle, 2002).

To be licensed as a clinical or counseling psychologist, a graduate in most states may complete postdoctoral work under a licensed psychologist (several states have recently dropped this requirement), has their credentials evaluated, and must pass the licensure exam. Licensed psychologists work anywhere from private practice to hospitals.

Careers by degree

A doctorate usually takes 5–6 years of graduate study (DeAngelis, 2010). PhDs work in settings that match their specialty; a biopsychologist might test a new drug at a pharmaceutical company, while someone with clinical training might become a forensic psychologist advising criminal trials and parole hearings.

Undergraduate psychology coursework also helps in psychiatric social work and psychiatric nursing, where assessment and therapy can be part of the job. Employers value the knowledge and skills a psychology bachelor’s brings, so graduates spread across many occupations.
