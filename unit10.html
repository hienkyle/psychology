Unit 10 · Overview

Emotion and Motivation

Why we do what we do: the theories behind motivated behavior, two powerful biological drives (eating and sex), sexual orientation and gender identity, and what emotions are and where they come from.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening questions

The chapter opens with a photograph taken moments after the bombing at the Boston Marathon on April 15, 2013 (OpenStax Figure 10.1). Emotions can change in an instant, especially in response to an unexpected event: surprise, fear, anger and sadness are among the immediate reactions. What are emotions, and what causes them? And what motivated some bystanders to help others while some ran for safety?

The chapter asks “What makes us behave as we do?” It looks at theories of motivation, the physiology of two motivated behaviors (eating and sex), and then at emotion: how it happens, its biological basis, and whether emotions are universal.

Unit 10 · Section 10.1

Motivation

Why do we do the things we do? Motivation describes the wants or needs that direct behavior toward a goal.

Learning objectives

  • Define intrinsic and extrinsic motivation
  • Understand that instincts, drive reduction, self-efficacy, and social motives have all been proposed as theories of motivation
  • Explain the basic concepts associated with Maslow’s hierarchy of needs
Read Section 10.1 on OpenStax ↗

Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation

Motives can be biological (hunger, thirst) or come from two other sources. Intrinsic motivation arises from internal factors: we do the behavior for the sense of personal satisfaction it brings. Extrinsic motivation arises from external factors: we do the behavior to receive something from others.

Think about why you are in school. If you are there because you enjoy learning, that is intrinsic motivation. If you want a degree to get a high-paying career, or to satisfy your parents, that is extrinsic. In reality, our motivations are often a mix of both, and the mix can change over time, sometimes in counterintuitive ways.

The overjustification effect

“Choose a job that you love, and you will never have to work a day in your life” is a familiar adage. Research challenges it (Daniel & Esser, 1980; Deci, 1972; Deci, Koestner, & Ryan, 1999): receiving extrinsic reinforcement, such as getting paid, for something you already enjoy can make it feel like work, and you may do it less when the reward is not around.

Other research finds intrinsic motivation less vulnerable, and verbal praise can even increase it (Arnold, 1976; Cameron & Pierce, 1994). If Odessa’s customers praise her work, her motivation may stay high. Two factors explain the mixed findings:

Culture and the classroom

Culture matters too. In collectivistic cultures, people often act for the welfare of family and group rather than for individual benefit, which encourages attention to both situational and cultural influences on behavior (Nisbett, Peng, Choi, & Norenzayan, 2001). In education, students are more intrinsically motivated when they feel a sense of belonging and respect, when evaluation is de-emphasized, when they have some control over their learning, and when tasks are challenging but doable with a clear rationale (Niemiec & Ryan, 2009).

Theories of motivation: instincts

William James (1842–1910), often called the “father of psychology” in the United States, proposed that behavior is driven by instincts, which aid survival (OpenStax Figure 10.4). An instinct is a species-specific pattern of behavior that is not learned, such as an infant’s rooting and sucking reflex.

James and his contemporaries disagreed on what counted as an instinct. James proposed several dozen human instincts, including a mother’s protection of her baby, the urge to lick sugar, and hunting prey. Critics pointed to the major role of learning: as early as the 1900s some “instinctive” behaviors were shown to result from associative learning (Faris, 1921), as in Watson’s conditioning of fear in “Little Albert” (Unit 6). Instincts alone cannot explain most human behavior.

Drive theory and habits

Drive theory builds on homeostasis, the tendency to maintain a balance, or optimal level, within a biological system. A control center (the brain) receives input from receptors and directs effectors to correct any imbalance. Deviations from homeostasis create physiological needs; needs produce psychological drive states; drives direct behavior toward restoring balance.

Drive theory also emphasizes habits, patterns of behavior in which we regularly engage. Once a behavior successfully reduces a drive, we are more likely to repeat it the next time we face that drive (Graham & Weiner, 1996).

Arousal theory and the Yerkes-Dodson law

Extensions of drive theory propose an optimal level of arousal that we try to maintain (Berlyne, 1960). When underaroused, we become bored and seek stimulation; when overaroused, we try to reduce arousal. The chapter’s example: students’ stress peaks at the end of the spring semester, boredom sets in not long after the semester ends, and by fall they are eager to return to school.

Yerkes and Dodson (1908) found that, in general, moderate arousal leads to the best performance. A softball team that thinks it will easily beat a weak opponent may be underaroused and play poorly. But the best level also depends on the task. The Yerkes-Dodson law holds that simple tasks are performed best at higher arousal, and complex tasks at lower arousal.

Self-efficacy and social motives

Self-efficacy is an individual’s belief in their own capability to complete a task, which may include past success at the same or a similar task. Albert Bandura (1994) theorized that self-efficacy plays a pivotal role in motivating behavior: our expectations about what a behavior will achieve, and our confidence that we can do it, shape what we try and the goals we set. People high in self-efficacy see difficult goals as challenges to master and persist through setbacks.

Several theorists have focused on social motives (McAdams & Constantian, 1983; McClelland & Liberman, 1949; Murray et al., 1938).

Murray et al. (1938) also grouped needs into broader domains: ambition (achievement and recognition), human power (dominance and aggression) and interpersonal affection (which includes play).

Maslow’s hierarchy of needs

Abraham Maslow (1943) proposed a hierarchy of needs spanning biological, individual and social motives, usually drawn as a pyramid. Needs at the base must be largely met before the needs above them motivate behavior: someone struggling to find food is unlikely to worry much about what others think of them. Select a level.

Self-transcendence goes beyond self-actualization: seeking meaning and purpose beyond the self (Koltko-Rivera, 2006). It helps explain why people sometimes sacrifice their own well-being for a greater cause, as Mohandas Gandhi did when he used hunger strikes as nonviolent protest.

Criticism: Maslow’s theory is considered subjective, and it does not fully account for real-world phenomena (Leonard, 1982). Still, it is a useful way to connect biological, individual and social motives in one model.

Hunger and Eating

Eating is essential for survival, so it is no surprise that hunger drives us toward food. Social, cultural and economic factors matter too, but this section focuses on the physiology.

Learning objectives

  • Describe how hunger and eating are regulated
  • Differentiate between levels of overweight and obesity and the associated health consequences
  • Explain the health consequences resulting from anorexia and bulimia nervosa
Read Section 10.2 on OpenStax ↗

Physiological mechanisms: hunger and satiety

Hunger starts the meal; satiation, a sense of fullness and satisfaction, ends it. Signals from the gut, the pancreas and liver, and fat cells (which release the satiety hormone leptin) reach the brain, where several areas of the hypothalamus and hindbrain integrate them (Ahima & Antwi, 2008; Woods & D’Alessio, 2008). Brain activity then determines whether feeding starts or stops. Switch between the two states.

Metabolism and body weight

Body weight depends on gene-environment interactions and on the balance between calories consumed and calories burned. Eat more than you burn and the excess is stored as fat; burn more than you eat and stored fat is used. Metabolic rate, the amount of energy expended in a given period, varies widely between people.

The idea

Set point theory: each of us has an ideal body weight, or set point, that is genetically predetermined and resistant to change. Moving away from it triggers compensatory changes in energy intake or expenditure (Speakman et al., 2011).

The problems

Weight-loss studies found no metabolic changes compared with controls (Weinsier et al., 2000), and the theory ignores social and environmental factors (Martin-Gronert & Ozanne, 2013; Speakman et al., 2011). It remains a common, intuitive explanation.

Obesity and body mass index

Body mass index (BMI) is calculated from height and weight. The CDC classifies an adult as overweight with a BMI of 25–29.9, obese with a BMI of 30 or more, and with severe obesity above 40, where the risk of death is high. Try the calculator.

BMI is most useful for studying populations, not individuals. Because of its limitations, the American Medical Association recommended in 2023 that BMI be removed as a standard measure of health.

Health risks and contributing factors

Being overweight or obese raises the risk of:

  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Stroke
  • Type 2 diabetes
  • Liver disease
  • Sleep apnea
  • Colon cancer
  • Breast cancer
  • Infertility
  • Arthritis

Socioeconomic status and the physical environment contribute: an unsafe neighborhood limits outdoor exercise, and cost and availability can push people toward inexpensive, high-calorie fast food.

Treatment

Diet and exercise are the first recommendation. For people who have failed repeated attempts to lose weight, or whose weight carries a high risk of death, a doctor may recommend bariatric surgery, which modifies the gastrointestinal system to reduce how much food can be eaten and/or how much of the digested food is absorbed (Mayo Clinic, 2013). Gastric banding, for example, creates a small pouch at the top of the stomach (OpenStax Figure 10.11). A meta-analysis found surgery more effective than non-surgical treatment for up to two years afterward, but no long-term studies yet exist (Gloy et al., 2013).

Dig deeper: Prader-Willi syndrome

Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS) is a genetic disorder that causes intense, insatiable hunger and reduced metabolism. Many children with PWS need around-the-clock supervision. It is the leading genetic cause of severe childhood obesity and is linked to cognitive deficits and emotional problems. OpenStax Figure 10.12 shows a 1680 painting of Eugenia Martínez Vallejo, who may have had PWS; at eight years old she weighed about 120 pounds.

The likely cause is abnormal hypothalamic function. Because the hypothalamus also regulates sexual behavior, many people with PWS do not reach sexual maturity in adolescence. There is no cure, but controlling weight greatly extends life expectancy (historically short), and psychoactive medications and growth hormones improve quality of life (Cassidy & Driscoll, 2009; Prader-Willi Syndrome Association, 2012).

Eating disorders

About 5% of the U.S. population experiences an eating disorder, which the APA (2023c) defines as an abnormal disturbance in attitudes and behaviors related to food. Like other psychological disorders, eating disorders are illnesses, yet they bring shame, blame and bias that can keep people from seeking care (Levinson et al., 2024; Lubieniecki et al., 2024). There is no single cause: research has focused on environmental and societal factors such as family, culture, peers, media and trauma (Mitchison & Hay, 2014), and also on biological predisposition (Donato et al., 2022; Bulk, Blake & Austin, 2018). Eating disorders are covered further in the psychological disorders chapter (Unit 15).

Who is affected

Eating disorders are rising globally in nearly every population. Constant exposure to extremely thin, sometimes digitally altered models feeds beauty standards linked to eating disorders (OpenStax Figure 10.13).

Increased rates have also been seen in midlife women (Salamon, 2022). Men may be harder to diagnose because excessive exercise and “athletic” diets can mask a disorder (Arnow et al., 2017), and many face a “double stigma” (Lehe et al., 2025).

Sexual Behavior, Sexuality, and Gender Identity

Sex matters to many people for evolutionary reasons, since reproduction depends on it, but human sexuality involves far more than reproduction.

Learning objectives

  • Understand basic biological mechanisms regulating sexual behavior and motivation
  • Appreciate the importance of Alfred Kinsey’s research on human sexuality
  • Recognize the contributions that William Masters and Virginia Johnson’s research made to our understanding of the sexual response cycle
  • Define sexual orientation and gender identity
Read Section 10.3 on OpenStax ↗

Physiological mechanisms of sexual behavior and motivation

Much of what we know here comes from animal research. As with other motivated behaviors, the hypothalamus plays a key role. The central finding is that sexual ability and sexual motivation depend on separate neural systems. Select a structure.

Humans show the same split: the worldwide popularity of drugs for erectile dysfunction shows that motivation and ability can be separated (Conrad, 2005). Many animals need the right sex hormones circulating to show sexual motivation, and in humans, sexual motivation across genders varies with circulating testosterone (Bhasin et al., 2007; Carter, 1992; Sherwin, 1988).

Kinsey’s research

Before the late 1940s, reliable, empirically based information on sex was scarce. Physicians were treated as authorities on sex despite little training, and most people learned from their own experiences and peers. Alfred Kinsey of Indiana University began large-scale survey research to encourage open discussion.

The reports were sensational: they sold well and got people talking, but some countries banned them, and Kinsey lost his Rockefeller Foundation funding (Bancroft, 2004). His work has been criticized for sampling and statistical errors (Jenkins, 2010), yet it shaped all later research by showing that behaviors once thought rare or problematic were more common, and more innocuous, than assumed (Bancroft, 2004; Bullough, 1998). Among his findings:

  • Women are as interested in and experienced with sex as men.
  • Masturbation has no adverse health consequences for either sex.
  • Same-sex relationships and sexual acts are fairly common.

He also developed the Kinsey scale, a continuum still used to categorize sexual orientation (Jenkins, 2010).

Masters and Johnson’s research

In 1966 William Masters and Virginia Johnson published findings from nearly 700 participants. Unlike Kinsey’s surveys, they directly observed sexual activity in the laboratory, nearly 10,000 sexual acts, while recording physiological measures such as blood pressure, respiration rate, vaginal lubrication and penile tumescence (Hock, 2008). They described a sexual response cycle with four phases, fairly similar across most people. Select a phase.

Masters and Johnson also documented that females can have multiple orgasms, reported average penis sizes (about 3 inches flaccid and 6 inches erect), found that penis size was unrelated to a partner’s pleasure, and showed that the vagina is elastic enough to accommodate a range of sizes (Hock, 2008). Like Kinsey’s, their work has been criticized, built upon and in places proven wrong: it is historically important, but not considered entirely accurate today.

Sexual orientation

Sexual orientation is a person’s emotional, romantic and/or erotic attraction to others, or to no one. Identities include lesbian, gay, bisexual, pansexual, asexual, heterosexual (straight) and others. Orientations exist on a spectrum; some do not reference gender at all, and some people have no specific term for theirs. Terminology keeps evolving and is self-defined (UC Davis LGBTQIA Resource Center, 2020).

Between 3% and 10% of the adult population identifies as gay or lesbian (OpenStax Figure 10.18).

Where does orientation come from?

A long-held belief was that socialization and family experiences determine orientation, but the family backgrounds of straight and LGBTQ people are very similar (Bell, Weinberg, & Hammersmith, 1981; Ross & Arrindell, 1988). Instead, the evidence points to biology:

  • Genes contribute (Bailey & Pillard, 1991; Hamer et al., 1993; Rodriguez-Larralde & Paradisi, 2009); some estimates say genes account for at least half of the variability (Pillard & Bailey, 1998).
  • Brain structure and function differ (e.g., Allen & Gorski, 1992; LeVay, 1991; Swaab & Hofman, 1990).
  • Some basic body structure and function differ (e.g., Hall & Kimura, 1994; Lippa, 2003).

The chapter’s conclusion: sexual orientation is largely something we are born with.

Misunderstanding: orientation is a choice

Sexual orientation is not a choice; it is relatively stable and cannot be changed. Claims that “conversion therapy” works have been widely criticized for poor research design, recruitment of participants and interpretation of data, and there is no credible evidence that orientation can be changed (Jenkins, 2010). Robert Spitzer, author of a widely cited study supporting conversion therapy, publicly apologized in 2012 and recanted the paper in a letter to Archives of Sexual Behavior, acknowledging that the major critiques were largely correct (Carey, 2012; Becker, 2012). Evidence of harm has led to laws against conversion therapy in parts of the U.S., and many professional organizations oppose it (Human Rights Campaign, n.d.).

Gender identity

Gender identity is a person’s sense of being male, female, neither, both, or another gender. It is distinct from sexual orientation, though the two are often confused.

Sexual orientation

Who you are attracted to: emotional, romantic and/or erotic attraction to others, or to no one.

Gender identity

Who you are: your own sense of your gender. It usually matches chromosomal and phenotypic sex, but not always.

Changing attitudes

Most millennials (ages 18–34) see gender as a spectrum rather than a strict binary; about 12% identify as transgender or gender non-conforming, and more people now know someone who uses gender-neutral pronouns such as they/them (Kennedy, 2017). Some retailers have dropped “pink and blue” gendered aisles. Even so, people who vary from gender norms still face discrimination and violence. Laverne Cox, the first transgender actress to play a transgender character on a regular TV series, advocates for LGBTQ+ issues, including with her “Ain’t I a Woman?” speaking tour (OpenStax Figure 10.19).

Cultural factors

Definitions of orientation and gender vary by culture. The U.S. has historically been heteronormative (heterosexuality seen as the norm) and cisnormative (the sex assigned at birth assumed to determine gender). Other cultures differ:

  • In parts of New Guinea, young boys engage in same-sex behavior for a set period as part of maturing (Baldwin & Baldwin, 1989).
  • The Bugis of Indonesia have recognized at least five gender identities for centuries; the bissu are spiritual figures who take part in ceremonies such as weddings and coronations (Davies, 2018).

Such groups can still face persecution as cultural, religious or political conditions change (Ibrahim, 2019). Many non-binary identities around the world predate modern definitions of gender.

Dig deeper: the case of David Reimer

Dr. John Money of Johns Hopkins University proposed the theory of psychosexual neutrality: we are gender-neutral at birth, develop a gender identity only once we master language, and socialization matters more than biology (Money, 1962; Colapinto, 2000). The Reimer case put that idea to a tragic test.

Emotion

What comes first, the pounding heart or the fear? Psychologists have argued about how emotions arise for more than a century, and the brain’s limbic system is at the center of the story.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the major theories of emotion
  • Describe the role that limbic structures play in emotional processing
  • Understand the ubiquitous nature of producing and recognizing emotions

What is emotion?

An emotion is a subjective state of being, often described as a feeling. Emotions arise from a mix of four parts, though their order is unclear and some may happen at the same time (Levenson et al., 1991; Gross, 1999; Roseman & Smith, 2001; Soussignan, 2002). An emotion can start with an internal stimulus too: imagining a war can make you sad even if you have never lived through one. Emotions can also change in an instant, as when a laughing toddler starts crying (OpenStax Figure 10.20).

Three classic theories of emotion

The theories disagree mainly about order: does bodily arousal cause the feeling, happen alongside it, or need a label first? The chapter’s example is seeing a snake. Pick a theory to see its sequence.

Evidence for the role of the body

The facial feedback hypothesis says our facial expressions can shape our emotions, in line with the James-Lange idea that the body drives the feeling:

  • Suppressing facial expressions lowers the intensity of some emotions (Davis, Senghas, & Ochsner, 2009).
  • Depressed participants whose frowning muscles were paralyzed with Botox reported less depression (Havas et al., 2010).
  • The strength of a facial expression affects the strength of the emotional reaction (Soussignan, 2002).

The two-factor experiment

Before Schachter, the Spanish physician G. Marañón studied the psychological effects of adrenaline, and his model of emotion came first (Cornelius, 1991). Schachter and Singer (1962) tested the two-factor idea directly. Men were injected with epinephrine, which produces fight-or-flight arousal, and then waited with a confederate who acted out either euphoria or anger.

Application: the polygraph

A polygraph (“lie detector”) measures physiological arousal while a person answers questions. No specific pattern of arousal reliably signals lying, so its validity is questioned (Saxe & Ben-Shakhar, 1999). A polygraph would only work well if, as James-Lange suggests, each emotion had its own distinct pattern of arousal.

Appraisal and beyond

Magda Arnold introduced the concept of appraisal (Roseman & Smith, 2001): our emotions depend on how we evaluate an event, so two people can react differently to the same situation. Asked to give a lecture, one person feels happy and another feels anxious. Later theories build on, or push back against, this idea.

Facial expression and recognition of emotions

Are facial expressions learned or innate? Paul Ekman (1972) asked a man from an isolated, preliterate community in New Guinea, who had never met outsiders, to show how he would look if friends came to visit, if his child died, if he were about to fight, or if he stepped on a smelly dead pig. His expressions matched the emotions Ekman had described. People who are congenitally blind also produce the same expressions without ever having seen them. Both findings support the idea, first proposed by Darwin (1872) in The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals, that these expressions are universal.

Cultural display rules

Expressions may be universal, but cultural display rules govern which emotions are acceptable to show, when, and how often (Malatesta & Haviland, 1982).

United States

Participants expressed negative emotions such as fear, anger and disgust both alone and in the presence of others (Matsumoto, 1990).

Japan

Participants expressed these emotions only when alone. Cultures that value social harmony tend to encourage suppressing emotion (Matsumoto, Yoo, & Nakagawa, 2008).

Evidence on gender differences in emotional expression is mixed, though men and women may differ in how they regulate emotion (McRae et al., 2008). Beyond the face, we read emotion from tone of voice and body language, the posture and movement of the body, often without being consciously aware of it (de Gelder, 2006; Tamietto et al., 2009).

Connect the concepts

Emotion and autism spectrum disorder

Autistic children often have difficulty recognizing the emotional states of others, which may stem from difficulty telling different facial expressions apart (Hobson, 1986). Autistic individuals may also have difficulty expressing emotion through tone of voice and facial expression (Macdonald et al., 1989). Educational curricula, cognitive-behavioral therapies and medications have shown some promise in helping autistic people process emotional information (Bauminger, 2002; Golan & Baron-Cohen, 2006; Guastella et al., 2010).

Emotion regulation

Covert strategies

Happen within the individual.

Overt strategies

Involve other people or actions, such as seeking advice or drinking alcohol.

In a survey of 218 undergraduates, overt strategies predicted psychopathology better than covert ones (Aldao & Dixon, 2014). Pederson (2016) found a relationship between pregaming (heavy drinking before a social event) and emotion regulation strategies, but a complicated one. More research is needed on which strategies are adaptive and which are maladaptive (Aldao & Dixon-Gordon, 2014).
