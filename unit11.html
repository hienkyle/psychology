Unit 11 · Overview

Personality

What makes each of us consistently ourselves? Nine ways of answering: early temperament theories, Freud and his followers, learning, humanistic, biological and trait approaches, culture, and how personality is measured.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening question

Three months before William Jefferson Blythe III was born, his father died in a car accident. He was raised by his mother and grandparents in Hope, Arkansas. When he was 4, his mother married Roger Clinton, Jr., an alcoholic who was physically abusive to her, and six years later she had another son, Roger. William took his stepfather’s surname and became Bill Clinton, the 42nd president of the United States. His half-brother Roger was arrested many times on drug charges and served time in jail (OpenStax Figure 11.1 opens the chapter with a photograph of two children).

Two brothers, raised by the same people, took radically different paths. Why did they make the choices they did, and what internal forces shaped their decisions? Personality psychology tries to answer questions like these.

Unit 11 · Section 11.1

What Is Personality?

Personality is the set of long-standing traits and patterns that lead us to consistently think, feel and behave in specific ways. People have tried to explain it for over 2,000 years.

Learning objectives

  • Define personality
  • Describe early theories about personality development
Read Section 11.1 on OpenStax ↗

Defining personality

Personality is what makes us unique: each person has an idiosyncratic pattern of enduring, long-term characteristics and a typical way of interacting with other people and the world. Personalities are thought to be long term, stable and not easily changed.

The word comes from the Latin persona, the mask an actor wore in the ancient world. The theatrical mask was not meant to hide identity: it represented or projected a specific personality trait of the character (OpenStax Figure 11.2 shows three such masks).

A short history

Ideas about personality built on one another for centuries before Freud offered the first comprehensive theory.

Hippocrates, Galen and the four temperaments

Hippocrates (370 BCE) theorized that traits and behavior come from four temperaments, each associated with a fluid of the body. Centuries later the Greek physician and philosopher Galen suggested that imbalances in these humors explain both diseases and personality differences, and that each person exhibits one of the four temperaments.

Gall and phrenology

In 1780 the German physician Franz Gall proposed that the distances between bumps on the skull reveal personality traits, character and mental abilities. Measuring them supposedly showed the size of the brain areas underneath, and so whether a person was friendly, prideful, murderous, kind, good with languages and so on. Gall drew a chart linking skull areas to traits (OpenStax Figure 11.3 shows a phrenology journal and an 1825 lithograph of Gall examining a skull).

Phrenology was very popular at first but was soon discredited for lack of empirical support, and has long been considered a pseudoscience.

Kant and Wundt

After Galen, others developed the four types further, most prominently Immanuel Kant (18th century) and Wilhelm Wundt (19th century). Kant agreed that everyone could be sorted into exactly one temperament, with no overlap, and listed traits describing each. Wundt suggested a better description used two major axes:

  • Emotional / nonemotional: separates strong emotions (melancholic, choleric) from weak ones (phlegmatic, sanguine).
  • Changeable / unchangeable: separates the changeable temperaments (choleric, sanguine) from the unchangeable ones (melancholic, phlegmatic).

Freud and the perspectives that followed

Sigmund Freud’s psychodynamic perspective was the first comprehensive theory of personality, explaining a wide variety of both normal and abnormal behavior. For Freud, unconscious drives influenced by sex and aggression, along with childhood sexuality, shape personality.

His followers, the neo-Freudians, agreed that childhood experiences matter but reduced the emphasis on sex and focused more on the social environment and culture. This view dominated the first half of the 20th century. Other major theories then emerged: the learning, humanistic, biological, evolutionary, trait and cultural perspectives, each covered below.

Freud and the Psychodynamic Perspective

Freud was the first to systematically study the unconscious mind. His model of a mind in conflict, and of personality formed in early childhood, is controversial but hugely influential.

Freud and the “talking cure”

Sigmund Freud (1856–1939) is probably the most controversial and misunderstood psychological theorist. He was a medical doctor, not a psychologist: no psychology degree existed when he trained, which helps explain some of the controversy. Still, he was the first to systematically study and theorize the workings of the unconscious mind in the way we associate with modern psychology.

Early on Freud worked with Josef Breuer, a Viennese physician. Breuer’s patient Bertha Pappenheim, known as Anna O., developed partial paralysis, headaches, blurred vision, amnesia and hallucinations while caring for her dying father; such symptoms were then called hysteria. Breuer treated her from 1880 to 1882 and found that letting her talk about her experiences relieved her symptoms. She called it the “talking cure.”

Freud never met Anna O., but her story was the basis of Studies on Hysteria (1895), which he co-wrote with Breuer. Freud concluded that hysteria resulted from childhood sexual abuse hidden from consciousness; Breuer disagreed, which ended their work together. Freud went on refining talk therapy and his theory of personality.

Levels of consciousness

Freud compared the mind to an iceberg: only about one-tenth is conscious, mental activity we can access at any time. The rest is unconscious: mental activity we are unaware of and unable to access. Unacceptable urges and desires are kept there through repression.

A Freudian slip, unintentionally substituting one word for another, was for Freud a sexual or aggressive urge slipping out of the unconscious. Linguists today find instead that slips of the tongue tend to happen when we are tired, nervous or not at our optimal level of cognitive functioning.

Id, ego and superego

For Freud, personality develops from a conflict between two forces: our biological aggressive and pleasure-seeking drives, and our internal, socialized control over them. Personality is our effort to balance the two, through three interacting systems.

IdMost primitive drives or urges (hunger, thirst, sex); seeks immediate gratification regardless of consequencesPleasure principlePresent from birth; unconscious
SuperegoConscience, our moral compass; strives for perfection and judges us, bringing pride or guiltLearned social rules of right and wrongDevelops as the child interacts with others
EgoThe rational self, the part of personality others see; satisfies the id realistically without guiltReality principleDevelops through social interaction

Because the id and superego are in constant conflict, the ego’s job is to find the middle ground. A person with a strong ego that balances the two has a healthy personality. Imbalances can lead to neurosis (a tendency to experience negative emotions), anxiety disorders or unhealthy behavior:

  • Dominated by the id: narcissistic and impulsive.
  • Dominant superego: controlled by guilt, denying even socially acceptable pleasures. An overly dominant superego may also show up as an over-controlled person so rational they are unaware of their emotional needs, or as a neurotic who overuses defense mechanisms.
  • Weak or absent superego: the person might become a psychopath.

Defense mechanisms

Anxiety arises when the ego cannot mediate between id and superego. The ego then restores balance with defense mechanisms: unconscious protective behaviors that reduce anxiety. We are unaware that we use them, they distort reality, and according to Freud we all use them; it is overuse that may be a problem.

Freud’s example: Joe, a high school football player, is unconsciously attracted to males but consciously believes being gay is immoral and would get him disowned and ostracized. To ease that anxiety he acts very “macho,” makes gay jokes and picks on a gay classmate, which submerges his unconscious impulses further. This is reaction formation.

In repression, anxiety-causing memories are blocked from consciousness, like turning up the car radio so you no longer hear a strange engine noise you cannot afford to fix; eventually you forget it. A repressed memory may still cause symptoms elsewhere. In regression, a four-year-old who resents a new sibling may go back to drinking from a bottle. In projection, a person refuses to acknowledge her own unconscious feelings and sees them in someone else instead.

Stages of psychosexual development

Freud believed personality develops during early childhood through a series of psychosexual stages of development. In each stage, the id’s pleasure-seeking urges focus on a different area of the body, an erogenous zone. Without proper nurturing and parenting during a stage, a person becomes stuck, or fixated, there, even as an adult.

Oral stage (birth to 1 year)

In the oral stage, pleasure focuses on the mouth: eating and sucking. Weaning at about 1 year can create conflict if handled poorly. An adult who smokes, drinks, overeats or bites her nails is, for Freud, fixated in this stage after being weaned too early or too late; all these behaviors ease anxiety.

Anal stage (1 to 3 years)

In the anal stage, children experience pleasure in their bowel and bladder movements, so the conflict is over toilet training as children work to master control of themselves. Success depends on how parents handle it.

Phallic stage (3 to 6 years)

In the phallic stage, children become aware of their bodies and of the differences between boys and girls; the erogenous zone is the genitals. Conflict arises when the child desires the opposite-sex parent and feels jealousy and hatred toward the same-sex parent.

  • Oedipus complex (boys): a boy desires his mother and wants to replace his father as a rival. Afraid his father will punish him, he feels castration anxiety. It is resolved when the boy identifies with his father as an indirect way to have the mother. Failure to resolve it can lead to a vain, overly ambitious personality. The name comes from the Greek myth of Oedipus, who unwittingly kills his father and marries his mother.
  • Penis envy: Freud alleged girls experience it because they do not have a penis.
  • Electra complex (girls): a girl desires her father’s attention and wishes to take her mother’s place. Often credited to Freud, it was actually proposed by Carl Jung. Freud first embraced it, then rejected it, yet it remains a cornerstone of Freudian theory.

Latency period (6 years to puberty)

The latency period is not considered a stage, because sexual feelings are dormant. Children focus on school, friendships, hobbies and sports, mostly with same-sex peers, which consolidates gender-role identity.

Genital stage (puberty on)

In the genital stage, sexual urges reawaken and are redirected to socially acceptable partners, who often resemble the other-sex parent. For Freud, mature sexual interests meant a strong desire for the opposite sex. People who reach this stage with no fixations are well-balanced, healthy adults.

Context and legacy

Freud worked in Vienna at the turn of the 20th century, in a climate of sexual repression with limited understanding of human sexuality. Because sex was taboo, he assumed neuroses came from the suppression of unconscious sexual and aggressive urges, and he took his own recollections and his interpretations of patients’ experiences and dreams as enough proof that the stages were universal.

Most of Freud’s ideas have not found support in modern research. But he showed that a large part of our mental life is shaped by early childhood and happens outside conscious awareness, paving the way for others. His followers soon realized that biology alone could not account for the diversity they met as psychoanalysis spread during the Nazi Holocaust; the antisemitism of that period may have led mainstream psychoanalysts to stress the universality of the mind’s structures.

Alfred Adler

Adler, a colleague of Freud’s and first president of the Vienna Psychoanalytical Society (Freud’s inner circle), was the first major theorist to break away from Freud (OpenStax Figure 11.8 is his portrait). He founded individual psychology, which focuses on our drive to compensate for feelings of inferiority.

An inferiority complex is a person’s feeling that they lack worth and don’t measure up to others or to society. Freud thought we are driven by sexual and aggressive urges; Adler thought childhood feelings of inferiority drive us to strive for superiority, and that this striving is the force behind all our thoughts, emotions and behavior.

Adler also stressed social connection: development emerges through social development, not sexual stages, and humanity must work together for the good of all. He named three fundamental social tasks we all face:

Because these tasks are explicitly known and pursued, Adler emphasized conscious rather than unconscious motivation. He believed in unconscious processes too, but thought conscious ones more important.

Adler also proposed that birth order shapes personality: older siblings lose sole parental attention when a new child arrives and compensate by becoming overachievers; the youngest may be spoiled; the middle child has the chance to minimize the negative dynamics of both. Despite popular attention, research has not conclusively confirmed these hypotheses.

Erik Erikson

An art-school dropout, Erikson met Anna Freud, Freud’s daughter, while tutoring the children of an American couple undergoing psychoanalysis in Vienna, and she encouraged him to study psychoanalysis. He received his diploma from the Vienna Psychoanalytic Institute in 1933 and, as Nazism spread, emigrated to the United States that same year.

Erikson’s psychosocial theory (studied in Unit 9) differs from Freud in two ways: personality develops throughout the lifespan rather than being fixed in early life, and each stage centers on social relationships rather than sex. Each of eight stages is a conflict or developmental task; a healthy personality and a sense of competence depend on completing each one.

Carl Jung

Jung, a Swiss psychiatrist and Freud’s protégé, split off to develop analytical psychology (OpenStax Figure 11.9 is his photograph). It focuses on balancing the opposing forces of conscious and unconscious thought and experience within one’s personality. Becoming aware of unconscious elements and integrating them into consciousness is a continuous learning process that happens mainly in the second half of life.

Jung split from Freud over two disagreements: like Adler and Erikson, he did not accept that the sexual drive is the primary motivator; and although he agreed there is a personal unconscious, he thought the concept incomplete.

The collective unconscious and archetypes

The collective unconscious is a universal version of the personal unconscious, holding mental patterns, or memory traces, common to all of us. These ancestral memories, which Jung called archetypes, appear as universal themes in literature, art and dreams across cultures: facing death, becoming independent, striving for mastery. Jung believed that through biology everyone inherits the same themes, so the same symbols, such as the hero, the maiden, the sage and the trickster, appear in the folklore and fairy tales of every culture.

Integrating these archetypal aspects of the self is part of self-realization in the second half of life. With this future orientation Jung parted ways with Freud’s belief that personality is determined solely by past events, and anticipated the humanistic movement and its emphasis on self-actualization.

Connect the concepts: are archetypes genetically based?

Jung likened human responses to archetypes to instinctual responses in animals. A criticism is that there is no evidence that archetypes are biologically based. Babies are born with some capacities, such as the ability to acquire language, but symbolic information such as archetypes is not encoded on the genome, and babies cannot decode symbolism. More recent research suggests archetypes emerge from our experiences and reflect linguistic or cultural characteristics. Most Jungian scholars today see the collective unconscious and archetypes as shaped by both innate and environmental influences, differing on the role and degree of each.

Introversion, extroversion and the persona

Jung’s most important contribution to personality psychology is the pair of attitudes toward life, extroversion and introversion: almost all models of personality now include them. Extroverts are energized by being outgoing and socially oriented; introverts may be quiet or social, but draw their energy from inner psychic activity. Jung thought a balance of the two best serves self-realization.

Jung also proposed the persona, a mask we adopt. We create it consciously, but it draws on both conscious experience and the collective unconscious. It is a compromise between our true self and what society expects: we hide the parts of ourselves that don’t fit society’s expectations.

Karen Horney

Horney was one of the first women trained as a Freudian psychoanalyst. She moved from Germany to the United States during the Great Depression and then away from Freud’s teachings. Like Jung, she believed everyone has the potential for self-realization, and that psychoanalysis should move toward a healthy self rather than explore early childhood patterns of dysfunction.

She rejected penis envy: any jealousy is most likely cultural, reflecting the greater privileges men often have, so differences between men’s and women’s personalities are culturally, not biologically, based. She further suggested that men have womb envy, because they cannot give birth.

Horney focused on unconscious anxiety. Normal growth can be blocked by basic anxiety from unmet needs, such as childhood loneliness or isolation. She described three ways children learn to cope with it:

The behavioral perspective

Behaviorists reject biological determinism: personality traits are not inborn. Personality is shaped by the reinforcements and consequences outside the organism, and people behave consistently because of prior learning. B. F. Skinner, a strict behaviorist, believed the environment is solely responsible for all behavior, including the enduring patterns personality theorists study.

For Skinner we develop response tendencies: we increase behaviors that lead to positive consequences and decrease those that lead to negative ones. He disagreed with Freud that personality is fixed in childhood: it develops over our entire life, so we should expect more variability over time.

Example: Greta is a risk taker who drives fast and goes hang gliding and kiteboarding. After she marries and has children, her environment stops reinforcing those behaviors, so she stops doing them, and now describes herself as cautious.

Bandura’s social-cognitive theory

Albert Bandura agreed that personality develops through learning, but felt Skinner’s strict behaviorism left out thinking and reasoning. His social-cognitive theory emphasizes both learning and cognition as sources of individual differences. Three concepts play a part: reciprocal determinism, observational learning and self-efficacy.

Reciprocal determinism

Instead of the environment alone determining behavior (Skinner), Bandura (1990) proposed reciprocal determinism: cognitive processes, behavior and context all interact, each influencing and being influenced by the others at the same time.

Observational learning

Bandura’s key contribution was that much learning is vicarious: we learn by watching someone else’s behavior and its consequences, which he called observational learning. We pick up new behavior patterns from models, and whether we imitate a model depends on whether we see the model reinforced or punished. That is how we learn which behaviors our culture accepts and rewards, and learn to inhibit deviant or unacceptable ones.

Reciprocal determinism is at work here too: personal factors decide which behaviors a person imitates. Someone who finds attention reinforcing may imitate a model’s boasting after seeing it rewarded; someone who views boasting negatively, or who feels heightened attention as scrutiny, is less likely to, for different reasons.

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy is our level of confidence in our own abilities, developed through social experiences. It affects how we approach challenges and reach goals, and in observational learning it shapes which behaviors we imitate and how well we perform them.

Julian Rotter: locus of control

Julian Rotter (1966) proposed locus of control, another cognitive factor. Unlike self-efficacy, a belief about our abilities, locus of control is about the power we have over our lives. People with an internal locus of control (“internals”) believe most outcomes result from their own efforts; people with an external locus (“externals”) believe outcomes are controlled by other people, luck or chance.

Compared with externals, people with an internal locus of control perform better academically, achieve more in their careers, are more independent and healthier, cope better, and are less depressed.

Walter Mischel and the person–situation debate

Walter Mischel, a student of Rotter and a Stanford colleague of Bandura, surveyed decades of research on trait prediction of behavior. He found that the data did not support the field’s central principle, that traits are consistent across situations. His report set off a decades-long period of self-examination, the person–situation debate.

Mischel suggested we had been looking for consistency in the wrong places: behavior was inconsistent across different situations but much more consistent within situations, and consistent in equivalent situations across time.

Self-regulation and the marshmallow test

Mischel’s best-known contribution is his work on self-regulation: identifying goals and using both internal feedback (thoughts, feelings) and external feedback (responses from anything or anyone in the environment) to reach them. It is also called will power, usually thought of as the ability to delay gratification, like Bettina passing up her son’s strawberry cupcakes because she is training for a 5K race.

The debate is now mostly resolved: most psychologists consider both the situation and personal factors. For Mischel (1993), people are situation processors: each child in the marshmallow test interpreted its reward structure in their own way. People use cognitive processes to interpret a situation and then act on that interpretation.

Humanistic Approaches

The “third force” in psychology asks how healthy people develop, and stresses our capacity for self-directed change.

Learning objectives

  • Discuss the contributions of Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers to personality development
Read Section 11.5 on OpenStax ↗

Humanism, the “third force”

Humanism reacted against both the pessimistic determinism of psychoanalysis, with its emphasis on psychological disturbance, and the behaviorists’ view of humans passively reacting to the environment, criticized as making people out to be personality-less robots.

It does not call those perspectives wrong, but argues that they miss the depth and meaning of human experience and our innate capacity for self-directed change and for transforming personal experiences. Its focus is how healthy people develop.

Abraham Maslow

Maslow studied people he considered healthy, creative and productive, including Albert Einstein, Eleanor Roosevelt, Thomas Jefferson and Abraham Lincoln. He found they shared characteristics: open, creative, loving, spontaneous, compassionate, concerned for others and accepting of themselves.

His best-known theory, the hierarchy of needs (covered in Unit 10), holds that humans share certain needs that must be met in a certain order. The highest is self-actualization, achieving our fullest potential. Maslow distinguished needs that motivate us to fulfill something missing from needs that inspire us to grow, and believed many emotional and behavioral concerns arise from failing to meet these needs.

Carl Rogers

One of Rogers’s main ideas is the self-concept: our thoughts and feelings about ourselves. How would you answer “Who am I?” A mainly positive answer suggests you feel good about who you are and see the world as safe and positive; a mainly negative one suggests you may be unhappy with who you are.

Rogers divided the self into the ideal self, the person you would like to be, and the real self, the person you actually are, and stressed that we need to achieve consistency between them.

  • Congruence: our thoughts about our real and ideal selves are very similar, so our self-concept is accurate. High congruence leads to a greater sense of self-worth and a healthy, productive life.
  • Incongruence: a great discrepancy between our real and ideal selves, which can lead to maladjustment.

Parents can help children reach congruence by giving them unconditional positive regard, or unconditional love. Rogers (1980) observed that people who are accepted and prized tend to develop a more caring attitude toward themselves.

Both Rogers’s and Maslow’s theories focus on individual choices and do not see biology as deterministic.

Biological and evolutionary views

Psychologists who favor the biological approach believe inherited predispositions and physiological processes can explain differences in our personalities (Burger, 2008).

Evolutionary psychology looks at personality traits that are universal as well as differences across individuals. In this view, adaptive differences evolved because they provide a survival and reproductive advantage; some individual differences, and their heritability, are well documented. David Buss identified theories linking traits and evolution:

  • Life-history theory: how people spend their time and energy, for example on bodily growth and maintenance, reproduction or parenting.
  • Costly signaling theory: the honesty and deception in the signals people send one another about their quality as a mate or friend.

The Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart

This behavioral-genetics study ran from 1979 to 1999 and studied 350 pairs of twins: identical and fraternal pairs, reared together and apart. It found that identical twins, whether raised together or apart, have very similar personalities, which suggests some traits are heritable.

Table 11.9The four kinds of twin pairs compared, compiled from Section 11.6
Reared togetherReared apart
Identical twinsSame genes, shared homeSame genes, different homes: still very similar personalities
Fraternal twinsPartly shared genes, shared homePartly shared genes, different homes

Heritability is the proportion of difference among people that is attributed to genetics. Traits the study reported with a heritability ratio above 0.50:

  • Leadership
  • Obedience to authority
  • Sense of well-being
  • Alienation
  • Resistance to stress
  • Fearfulness

So some aspects of personality are largely controlled by genetics, but a trait is not set by a single gene: it comes from a combination of many genes, plus epigenetic factors that control whether the genes are expressed. Other research on biology and personality has studied Type A and Type B personalities, covered in the chapter on stress, lifestyle and health.

Temperament

Most contemporary psychologists believe temperament has a biological basis because it appears so early in life (Rothbart, 2011). As in Unit 9, Thomas and Chess (1977) found babies could be categorized into one of three temperaments:

  • Easy
  • Difficult
  • Slow to warm up

Environmental factors such as family interactions, and maturation, can still affect how a child’s personality is expressed. Two dimensions of temperament are important parts of adult personality:

  • Reactivity: how we respond to new or challenging environmental stimuli. One person reacts to something new with high anxiety; another barely notices it.
  • Self-regulation: our ability to control that response.

Trait Theorists

Trait theorists describe personality through traits, characteristic ways of behaving. Are you sociable or shy, passive or aggressive, optimistic or pessimistic, moody or even-tempered?

From 4,500 words to five factors

Early trait theorists tried to describe all human traits; later theorists narrowed the list to a few broad dimensions.

Gordon Allport

Allport found 4,500 words in English that describe people and organized traits into three categories.

Raymond Cattell and the 16PF

Cattell narrowed the list to about 171 traits. But saying a trait is present or absent misses what makes us unique: we all have the same traits and differ only in the degree to which each is expressed. Cattell identified 16 factors and built the 16PF assessment, which scores each factor on a continuum from low to high. A low score on warmth, for example, means you tend to be distant and cold; a high score, supportive and comforting.

Hans and Sybil Eysenck

The Eysencks (OpenStax Figure 11.12 is their photograph) focused on temperament, the inborn, genetically based personality differences studied earlier, and believed personality is largely governed by biology. They saw two specific dimensions:

  • Extroversion / introversion: extroverts are sociable and outgoing and readily connect with others; introverts have a higher need to be alone, engage in solitary behaviors and limit interactions with others.
  • Neuroticism / stability: people high in neuroticism tend to be anxious, with an overactive sympathetic nervous system that sends them into a fight-or-flight reaction even under low stress. People high in stability need more stimulation to trigger that reaction and are more emotionally stable.

Later the Eysencks added a third dimension, psychoticism versus superego control. People high in psychoticism tend to be independent thinkers, cold, nonconformist, impulsive, antisocial and hostile; people high in superego control have high impulse control and are more altruistic, empathetic, cooperative and conventional.

The Five Factor Model (Big Five)

Cattell’s 16 factors may be too broad and the Eysencks’ two-factor system too narrow. The Five Factor Model hits a middle ground; it is the most popular theory in personality psychology today and the most accurate approximation of the basic personality dimensions (Funder, 2001). Remember the five factors with OCEAN. Everyone has every factor, each along a spectrum.

Each factor is a range between two extremes, and most of us fall somewhere in the middle. The factors are relatively stable over the lifespan, with small changes:

  • Conscientiousness increases from young adulthood into middle age, as we get better at managing relationships and careers.
  • Agreeableness increases with age, peaking between 50 and 70 years.
  • Neuroticism and extroversion tend to decline slightly with age.

The Big Five have been shown to exist across ethnicities, cultures and ages, and may have substantial biological and genetic components.

Cultural Understandings of Personality

Personality is shaped by genes and environment, and one of the most important environmental factors is culture: all of the beliefs, customs, art and traditions of a particular society.

Learning objectives

  • Discuss personality differences of people from collectivist and individualist cultures
  • Discuss the three approaches to studying personality in a cultural context
Read Section 11.8 on OpenStax ↗

Culture and personality traits

Culture is transmitted through language and through the modeling of culturally acceptable and unacceptable behaviors that are rewarded or punished. Personality appears to have both universal and culture-specific aspects, and Western ideas about personality may not apply to other cultures. The strength of traits varies across cultures:

  • Asian cultures, which are more collectivist: people tend to be less extroverted.
  • Central and South American cultures: people tend to score higher on openness to experience.
  • Europeans: score higher on neuroticism.

Regional differences in the United States

Rentfrow and colleagues (2013) analyzed responses from over 1.5 million people in the United States and found three distinct regional personality clusters (OpenStax Figure 11.15 maps them).

Table 11.14U.S. regional personality clusters (Rentfrow et al., 2013), compiled from Section 11.8
ClusterRegionDominant personality
1Upper Midwest and Deep SouthFriendly and conventional
2The WestRelaxed, emotionally stable, calm, creative
3The NortheastStressed, irritable, depressed

People in clusters 2 and 3 are also generally more open.

One explanation is selective migration: people choose to move to places compatible with their personalities and needs. Someone high on agreeableness would likely want to live near family and friends; someone high on openness would prefer a place known as diverse and innovative, such as California.

Rentfrow, Jost, Gosling and Potter (2009) also found that a region’s psychological profile is closely related to its residents’, beyond the usual explanations of religion, racial diversity and education. A state’s levels of openness and conscientiousness may predict voting patterns, and regional personality correlates with political views, economic vitality and entrepreneurial rates.

Tests developed in one culture can mislead in another. Frewer and Bleus (1991) gave the Eysenck Personality Inventory to Papua New Guinean university students and found the results relevant only when analyzed within the context of a collectivist society. Dana (1986) noted that personality assessment of Native Americans often ignores culture-specific responses and a tribe-specific frame of reference; assessors need knowledge of history, tribal differences, contemporary reservation culture and levels of acculturation to interpret responses with minimal bias.

Unit 11 · Section 11.9

Personality Assessment

Personality tests are used to screen job applicants, in criminal cases and custody battles, and to assess psychological disorders. They come in two broad kinds: objective self-report inventories and projective tests.

Learning objectives

  • Discuss the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory
  • Recognize and describe common projective tests used in personality assessment
Read Section 11.9 on OpenStax ↗

Why assess personality?

Roberto, Mikhail and Nat all want to be police officers. Roberto is quiet, shy, kind and lacks motivation; Mikhail is a loud, hardworking leader who is impulsive and drinks too much on weekends; Nat is thoughtful, well liked and trustworthy but so methodical that quick decisions are hard. Who would make the best officer?

A police officer’s job is very high in stress, so law enforcement agencies use personality testing to help hire the right people. Testing is also used for job training, in criminal cases and custody battles, and to assess psychological disorders.

Self-report inventories

Self-report inventories are an objective kind of test. They usually use multiple-choice items or numbered scales, from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), called Likert scales after their developer, Rensis Likert (1932).

They are generally easy to administer and cost effective. But test takers may answer, intentionally or not, in ways that are more socially desirable, exaggerated, biased or misleading; a job applicant will likely try to look even better than they are.

The Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory

The Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI) is one of the most widely used personality inventories.

Table 11.16Versions of the MMPI described in Section 11.9
VersionYearItemsNotes

Update since the textbook

In 2020 the University of Minnesota Press released the MMPI-3, a 335-question true/false test and the first full revision of the assessment, with new norms, in over 30 years (University of Minnesota, Office of the Vice President for Research).

Responses on the MMPI-2 produce a clinical profile of 10 scales: hypochondriasis, depression, hysteria, psychopathic deviance (social deviance), masculinity versus femininity, paranoia, psychasthenia (obsessive/compulsive qualities), schizophrenia, hypomania and social introversion, plus a scale for risk factors for alcohol abuse. Despite the newer test’s advantages, the MMPI-2 is more established and still more widely used. The tests are typically given by computer.

Originally built to help diagnose psychological disorders, the MMPI is now also used for occupational screening, such as in law enforcement, and in college, career and marital counseling.

Validity and reliability scales

  • Validity scales check whether answers can be trusted. The Lie Scale (“L” Scale), 15 items, detects “faking good”: underreporting problems to appear healthier, for example answering “yes” to many unrealistically positive items such as “I have never told a lie.”
  • Reliability scales test consistency over time: taking the test today and again 5 years later should give similar scores.

Beutler, Nussbaum and Meredith (1988) gave the MMPI to newly recruited police officers and again 2 years later. After 2 years on the job, the officers’ responses showed increased vulnerability to alcoholism, somatic symptoms (vague, unexplained physical complaints) and anxiety; after 4 years, the results suggested a high risk of alcohol-related difficulties.

Projective tests

A projective test relies on Freud’s defense mechanism of projection to assess unconscious processes. The person is shown a series of ambiguous stimuli and encouraged to project their feelings, impulses and desires onto them by telling a story, interpreting an image or completing a sentence. Many have been standardized and can show whether someone has unusual thoughts or a high level of anxiety, or is likely to become volatile.

The Rorschach Inkblot Test

Hermann Rorschach (pronounced “ROAR-shock”) developed the test in 1921. A psychologist presents symmetrical inkblot cards one at a time and asks, “What might this be?” What the test taker sees is thought to reveal unconscious feelings and struggles. Standardized with the Exner system, it is effective in measuring depression, psychosis and anxiety.

The Thematic Apperception Test

A person taking the TAT is shown 8–12 ambiguous pictures (OpenStax Figure 11.18 reproduces one: two people on a bench winding thread) and tells a story about each. The stories give insight into their social world, revealing hopes, fears, interests and goals, and the storytelling format lowers resistance to sharing unconscious details. The TAT has been used clinically to evaluate psychological difficulties, but standardization among clinicians is virtually nonexistent and validity and reliability are modest to low. Even so, it has been one of the most widely used projective tests.

The Rotter Incomplete Sentence Blank

Julian Rotter, of locus-of-control fame, developed the RISB in 1950, with school, college and adult forms. It has 40 incomplete sentences to complete as quickly as possible, in 1–2 words each, taking about 20 minutes. Like a word association test, the responses are presumed to reveal desires, fears and struggles. The RISB is used to screen college students for adjustment problems and in career counseling.

Culturally relevant projective tests

For decades the traditional projective tests were used in cross-cultural assessment, but test bias limited their usefulness: it is hard to assess people from widely different ethnic or cultural groups with instruments based on data from a single culture or race. Used with African-American test takers, for example, the TAT often produced short stories and low cultural identification.

So new tests explored race, language and acculturation. Robert Williams developed the first culturally specific projective test reflecting the everyday experiences of African Americans; its updated version is the C-TCB. Compared with the TAT, it led African-American test takers to tell longer stories with more positive feelings and stronger identification. The TEMAS, standing for “Tell-Me-A-Story” and a play on the Spanish word temas (themes), uses images and storytelling cues that relate to minority culture.
