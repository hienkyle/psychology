Unit 12 · Overview

Social Psychology

How does the presence of other people change what we think, feel and do? Seven sections: how we explain behavior, the roles we play, attitudes and persuasion, group pressure, prejudice, aggression, and helping and love.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening question

On the night of February 26, 2012, Trayvon Martin, a 17-year-old African American high school student, was shot by neighborhood watch volunteer George Zimmerman in a predominantly White neighborhood. Zimmerman saw the teen in a hoodie, followed him and called the police to report a person acting suspiciously. The 911 operator told him not to follow the teen, as did the neighborhood watch guidelines he had been given. A physical altercation ended with Zimmerman fatally shooting Martin, who was unarmed. Zimmerman claimed self-defense, and a Florida jury found him not guilty of second-degree murder and of manslaughter. The death led to a nationwide outcry (OpenStax Figure 12.1 shows rallies on each side of the debate).

In 2019 a neighbor called a non-emergency police line because Atatiana Jefferson’s front door was open late at night. Jefferson, not knowing police had been called, took out her legally owned gun; an officer in the back yard perceived a threat and fatally shot her while her 8-year-old nephew watched.

Why did each of these nights end so tragically, what dynamics contributed, and how can such deaths be prevented? Social psychologists hold that a person’s behavior is influenced by who else is present in a situation and by the makeup of social groups.

Unit 12 · Section 12.1

What Is Social Psychology?

Social psychology examines how people affect one another, with a particular focus on the power of the situation. Our explanations for behavior, our own and other people’s, are often biased.

Learning objectives

  • Define social psychology
  • Describe situational versus dispositional influences on behavior
  • Describe the fundamental attribution error
  • Explain actor-observer bias
  • Describe self-serving bias
  • Explain the just-world hypothesis
Read Section 12.1 on OpenStax ↗

The power of the situation

According to the American Psychological Association, social psychologists “are interested in all aspects of personality and social interaction, exploring the influence of interpersonal and group relationships on human behavior.” People change their behavior to align with the social situation, and when we are unsure how to behave, we take our cues from others. Social psychologists focus on how people interpret situations and how those interpretations shape thoughts, feelings and behavior (Ross & Nisbett, 1991).

Intrapersonal: the individual
  • Emotions and attitudes
  • The self
  • Social cognition: how we think about ourselves and others
Interpersonal: pairs and groups
  • Helping behavior and aggression
  • Prejudice and discrimination
  • Attraction and close relationships
  • Group processes and intergroup relationships

Situational and dispositional influences

Behavior is a product of both the situation (cultural influences, social roles, the presence of bystanders) and the person (personality characteristics).

  • Situationism: behavior is determined by our immediate environment and surroundings. Social psychologists have tended to take this view.
  • Dispositionism: behavior is determined by internal factors (Heider, 1958). An internal factor is an attribute of a person, such as personality traits and temperament. Personality psychologists have promoted this view.

Modern social psychology takes both into account (Fiske, Gilbert, & Lindzey, 2010), and the field of social-personality psychology studies how internal and situational factors interact.

The fundamental attribution error

An attribution is an explanation for someone’s behavior. People tend to overemphasize internal factors when explaining others’ behavior and to underestimate the power of the situation: they assume a behavior reflects the person’s trait and miss when it reflects their state. This is the fundamental attribution error (Ross, 1977). The predominant culture in the United States tends to favor this dispositional approach.

The quizmaster study

The error is strong enough that people overlook obvious situational advantages (Ross, Amabile, & Steinmetz, 1977). Students were randomly assigned to be a questioner or a contestant. Questioners wrote difficult questions they knew the answers to, and contestants answered only 4 out of 10 correctly. Questioners did not rate their own general knowledge higher than the contestants did theirs, but contestants rated the questioners as more knowledgeable than themselves, and so did outside observers. Both ignored the obvious cause: the questioners wrote the questions (OpenStax Figure 12.3 shows a game show).

The halo effect

The halo effect is letting an overall impression of someone color how we judge their character: for example, assuming physically attractive people, or outgoing and friendly people, have better moral character.

Some researchers suggest the error may not be as powerful as often portrayed: a review of more than 173 studies found that several factors affect how strong it is (Malle, 2006).

Is the fundamental attribution error universal?

No. People from an individualistic culture, one that focuses on individual achievement and autonomy (common in western countries such as the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom), are most likely to commit it. People from a collectivist culture, one that focuses on communal relationships such as family, friends and community (common in east Asian, Latin American and African countries), are less likely to (Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Triandis, 2001). A focus on others gives a broader view that takes situational and cultural influences into account.

Actor-observer bias

An observer of Jamie would not know about the layoff, so they would blame Jamie’s disposition. We usually have only observable information about others, but much more information about ourselves: if you yelled at a loved one, you would likely say you were tired or unwell, a situational explanation. The actor-observer bias is attributing other people’s behavior to internal factors while attributing our own to situational forces (Jones & Nisbett, 1971).

In one study, men explained why they liked their own girlfriend mostly through her qualities (her pleasant personality), which are outside causes of their own behavior, and rarely through causes inside themselves (“I need companionship”). Explaining why a friend liked his girlfriend, they gave dispositional and situational reasons about equally (Nisbett et al., 1973).

Self-serving bias

One model of attribution (Weiner, 1979) has three dimensions: locus of control (internal or external), stability (stable if the circumstances are unlikely to change) and controllability (whether we have the power to control the circumstances).

Self-serving bias is explaining our successes by internal characteristics and our failures by external factors: “I’m smart,” not “The exam was easy.” It protects self-esteem, and like the other biases it depends on culture. Sports fans show it clearly (Grove, Hanrahan, & McInman, 1991).

The just-world hypothesis

The just-world hypothesis is the belief, a common ideology in the United States, that people get the outcomes they deserve (Lerner & Miller, 1978). It makes the world feel fair, predictable and controllable: work hard and you will get ahead.

Its downside is victim blame, a consequence of westerners’ preference for dispositional explanations. People blame those living in poverty (“the poor are lazy”), a clear fundamental attribution error that ignores unemployment, recession, poor educational opportunities and the family cycle of poverty. Just-world believers also hold negative attitudes toward people who are unemployed and people living with AIDS (Sutton & Douglas, 2005), and victims of sexual assault may be blamed for their abuse. Advocacy groups such as Domestic Violence Ended (DOVE) attend court so blame is directed at perpetrators.

Self-presentation

We use the social environment as a source of cues about how to behave. Three situational forces, social roles, social norms and scripts, have a strong influence on what we do.

Learning objectives

  • Describe social roles and how they influence behavior
  • Explain what social norms are and how they influence behavior
  • Define script
  • Describe the findings and criticisms of Zimbardo’s Stanford prison experiment
Read Section 12.2 on OpenStax ↗

Social roles

A social role is a pattern of behavior expected of a person in a given setting or group (Hare, 2003). Each of us holds several at once: student, parent, son or daughter, spouse, lifeguard. Roles are defined by culturally shared knowledge, so nearly everyone knows what a student is expected to do: take notes, listen, read and attend class.

Our behavior changes with the role. You may be relaxed and joking at a family function but professional and focused at work. Roles can shape behavior so much that identity and personality can vary with context (Malloy et al., 1997).

Social norms

The shared knowledge behind roles comes from social norms: a group’s expectations of what is appropriate and acceptable behavior and thought for its members (Deutsch & Gerard, 1955). Norms tell us how to act, what to talk about and what to wear, and they exist everywhere: in families, workplaces, gangs and on social media.

Connect the concepts: tweens, teens and social norms

An 11-year-old asks for clothes from a store popular with preteens. Asked whether the brand will make her popular, she says no: “It is what the popular kids wear. It will make me feel happier.” Young people struggle to become independent while trying hard to fit in with their peers (OpenStax Figure 12.9).

Scripts

A script is a person’s knowledge about the sequence of events expected in a specific setting (Schank & Abelson, 1977): the first day of school, an elevator, a restaurant. Scripts differ by culture. To get a server’s attention in the United States you try to make eye contact; in Brazil you would say “psst”, which might seem rude to an American. Without a script, an unfamiliar situation can feel uncomfortable and confusing.

Zimbardo’s Stanford prison experiment

The Stanford prison experiment, run by Philip Zimbardo and colleagues, has been used to demonstrate the power of social roles, norms and scripts.

Zimbardo (2013) explained that “we had created an overwhelmingly powerful situation”: prisoners were withdrawing and behaving in pathological ways, some guards behaved sadistically, and even the “good” guards felt helpless to intervene. No guard quit, came late, called in sick or asked for overtime pay.

Criticisms, and Abu Ghraib

The study has been criticized since its inception, on ethical grounds and for its generalizability (Bartels et al., 2016; Griggs, 2014; Le Texier, 2019):

  • Recruitment: the way students were recruited may have affected the outcome (Carnahan & McFarland, 2007).
  • Expectations: Zimbardo appears to have given guards specific guidelines on expected behavior (Zimbardo, 2007), which likely created the expectation of the behaviors reported; the guards may simply have acted as they thought they were expected to (Bartels, 2019).
  • Replication: attempts to replicate aspects of the study have not succeeded. When guards got no guidelines, researchers documented different outcomes (Reicher & Haslam, 2006).

The study has parallels with the abuse of prisoners of war by U.S. Army troops and CIA personnel at Abu Ghraib prison in 2003–2004, during the Iraq War, documented in photographs some abusers took themselves.

Unit 12 · Section 12.3

Attitudes and Persuasion

Situations shape attitudes as well as behavior. Attitudes change from the inside, when we resolve cognitive dissonance, and from the outside, through persuasion.

Learning objectives

  • Define attitude
  • Describe how people’s attitudes are internally changed through cognitive dissonance
  • Explain how people’s attitudes are externally changed through persuasion
  • Describe the peripheral and central routes to persuasion
Read Section 12.3 on OpenStax ↗

Attitudes

An attitude is our evaluation of a person, an idea or an object, typically positive or negative (Eagly & Chaiken, 1993). Attitudes have three components (Rosenberg & Hovland, 1960). Attitudes can change through situational pressure, but we can also change them consciously, by our own free will.

Cognitive dissonance

Maintaining positive self-esteem is a powerful motivator (Tavris & Aronson, 2008). Leon Festinger (1957) defined cognitive dissonance as psychological discomfort from holding two or more inconsistent attitudes, behaviors or cognitions (thoughts, beliefs or opinions) that run counter to our positive self-perception.

  • Only conflicts that threaten a positive self-image cause dissonance (Greenwald & Ronis, 1978).
  • Dissonance can cause physiological arousal (Croyle & Cooper, 1983) and activates brain regions important in emotion and cognition (van Veen et al., 2009).
  • Because it is uncomfortable, we are motivated to reduce it by bringing cognitions, attitudes and behaviors back into harmony.

Joaquin, 20, enlists in the military. Boot camp means 5:00 a.m. wake-ups, chronic sleep deprivation, being yelled at, sand flea bites and exhaustion; recruits who reach week 11 do 54 hours of continuous training. He signed on for four years and cannot legally leave, so changing his behavior is not an option. Instead he can change his beliefs: “I am becoming stronger, healthier and sharper… What I am doing is really important.” He then feels better and no longer experiences dissonance.

The effect of initiation: justification of effort

A difficult initiation into a group makes us like the group more. Justification of effort is the idea that we value goals and achievements more when we have put more effort into them: a dresser you spent hours assembling yourself feels more valuable than a fancier one someone bought you.

Aronson and Mills (1959) randomly assigned students joining a discussion group to no, easy or difficult initiation. After a deliberately boring first discussion, those with the difficult initiation rated the group most favorably.

Similarly, 463 undergraduates rated higher-effort courses as more valuable and said they learned more in them, regardless of their grades (Heckert et al., 2006).

Think about it: Maria and Marco pay more than $3,000 a month for a very small house in expensive Fairfield County, Connecticut, and complain they never have money. Maria telecommutes, yet they won’t move, saying the area is beautiful and they love the beaches. How does cognitive dissonance apply to their choice?

Persuasion and the Yale attitude change approach

Persuasion is the process of changing our attitude toward something based on some kind of communication; much of it comes from outside forces such as advertising. During World War II, Carl Hovland researched persuasion for the U.S. Army, then continued at Yale. The resulting Yale attitude change approach describes when people change their attitudes: it depends on the source, the message and the audience (Hovland, Janis, & Kelley, 1953).

The elaboration likelihood model

The elaboration likelihood model (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986) uses the same source, message and audience variables to predict when attitudes change, through one of two routes.

  • Central route persuasion is logic driven and uses data and facts. It works best when the audience is analytical and willing to process the information; the argument must be strong, and if it succeeds the attitude change lasts.
  • Peripheral route persuasion is indirect: it associates positivity with the message through cues such as positive emotions and celebrity endorsement. It needs little effort, the audience need not be motivated, and it may not even be noticed, but change is less permanent.

The foot-in-the-door technique

With the foot-in-the-door technique, a persuader gets a person to agree to a small favor or purchase, then asks for a larger one (Cialdini, 2001). It relies on the principle of consistency: once we commit to a behavior, we want to stay consistent with it.

Everyday examples: a teen who gets curfew extended by half an hour asks for something bigger; a phone salesperson who sells you the best data plan then suggests a three-year extended warranty; a car buyer talked into paying for most of the available options.

Conformity, Compliance, and Obedience

Groups and authority figures can change what we say, do and decide, sometimes against our own judgment or values.

Learning objectives

  • Describe Stanley Milgram’s experiment and its implications
  • Define groupthink, social facilitation, and social loafing
Read Section 12.4 on OpenStax ↗

Asch’s line study

In the 1950s Solomon Asch showed groups a target line, x, and three comparison lines, a, b and c, and asked which matched x. Each group had only one real, naïve participant; the rest were confederates, people who work for the researcher and know about the experiment but act as participants. The confederates gave an obviously wrong answer, then the real participant answered aloud.

Asch (1955) found that 76% of participants conformed at least once. Conformity is changing your behavior to go along with the group, even if you do not agree with it, and the Asch effect is the group majority’s influence on an individual’s judgment, even when that judgment is inaccurate.

Changing your answer when it is public but not when it is private is compliance: going along with a request or demand even if you do not agree, a form of conformity. Asch’s participants complied by giving wrong answers, but privately did not accept them as correct. Children voting aloud in class show the same effect.

What makes conformity more likely?

  • Size of the majority: the bigger the majority, the more conformity, up to a limit. In Asch’s study conformity rose with majorities up to seven, then leveled off and decreased slightly.
  • Another dissenter: with at least one dissenter, conformity dropped to near zero.
  • Public or private responses: conformity is more likely in public and less likely when answers are written privately (Deutsch & Gerard, 1955). This is why government elections use secret ballots.

Why people conform: normative and informational influence

Deutsch and Gerard (1955) described two motives.

Stanley Milgram’s experiment

Obedience is changing your behavior to comply with an authority figure, often out of concern for the consequences of not complying. Stanley Milgram, a Yale professor, was influenced by the trial of Nazi war criminal Adolf Eichmann, whose defense was that he was “just following orders.”

Milgram (1963) recruited 40 men for what they were told was a study of learning and memory. As “teachers,” they were to shock a “learner” for each wrong answer, in 15-volt steps up to 450 volts. The learners were confederates and received no shocks, but they cried out, begged to stop and complained of heart trouble. When the researcher told them to continue, 65% of participants went to the maximum voltage, past the point where the learner became unresponsive.

The variations show that when the learner’s humanity was increased, or the experimenter’s authority decreased, obedience fell. The findings still apply: junior midwives obeyed a senior supervisor’s request to do something they had privately opposed (Martin & Bull, 2008), and replications by Burger (2009) and in Poland by Doliński et al. (2017) found obedience levels consistent with Milgram’s.

Groupthink

Groupthink is group members modifying their opinions to align with what they believe is the group consensus (Janis, 1972). Groups make more extreme decisions than individuals, and eliminating diverse opinions leads to faulty decisions.

Dig deeper: groupthink in the U.S. government

The chapter discusses the March 2003 invasion of Iraq, led by the United States with a small coalition. A small group of advisors and President George W. Bush reached a consensus that Iraq had weapons of mass destruction and was a significant threat, although some may have had doubts about the information. Iraq did not have such weapons, which came to light only after the invasion was underway. The chapter links an interview in which Colin Powell discusses the information behind his United Nations speech and asks whether you see evidence of groupthink.

Group polarization and social traps

Group polarization is the strengthening of a group’s original attitude after discussion (Teger & Pruitt, 1967): a group that initially favors a view favors it more strongly afterward, and a group that opposes it opposes it more strongly.

Polarization explains why people at political conventions back platforms they would not support alone. Some theorists argue it partly drives today’s extreme partisanship: people choose media that match their views, rarely meet opposing ones, and grow more hostile, with political discrimination on par with, or perhaps exceeding, racial discrimination (Iyengar & Westwood, 2015). Everyday example: if your friends vociferously agree someone is attractive, you may find them even more attractive.

Social traps are situations in which individuals or groups act against their own best interest, with negative long-term consequences, and are very hard to escape once established. Example: the post-World War II nuclear arms race between the United States and the Soviet Union.

Social loafing

Social loafing is putting in less effort on tasks where contributions are pooled, because individual performance cannot be evaluated separately from the group; performance declines on easy tasks.

  • It is less likely when work will be assessed by a manager or instructor, or when group members complete self-evaluations (Karau & Williams, 1993; Simms & Nichols, 2014).
  • It increases with group size (Shepperd & Taylor, 1999). College students were most likely to loaf; women and people from collectivistic cultures were less likely to (Karau & Williams, 1993).
  • Flocking, assigning students with similar schedules and motivations to the same group, reduced “free riding” and improved group grades compared with self-selected groups (Harding, 2018).

The opposite happens when the task is complex and difficult (Bond & Titus, 1983). When your performance can’t be evaluated there is less pressure and arousal, which leaves you relaxed enough to do your best if you choose (Zajonc, 1965), and on a hard task many people feel the group needs their input. The chapter summary frames this group effect as facilitating performance on easy tasks and inhibiting it on difficult ones.

Deindividuation

Deindividuation is a group situation in which a person feels anonymous, with a resulting reduction in accountability and sense of self. It is often pointed to in mob or riot-like behavior (Zimbardo, 1969), but research on its role has produced inconsistent results (Granström et al., 2009).

Unit 12 · Section 12.5

Prejudice and Discrimination

Prejudice and discrimination are often root causes of human conflict, from crime to war and genocide, and they affect everyone. This section defines them, gives examples and explains why they persist.

Learning objectives

  • Define and distinguish among prejudice, stereotypes, and discrimination
  • Provide examples of prejudice, stereotypes, and discrimination
  • Explain why prejudice and discrimination exist
Read Section 12.5 on OpenStax ↗

Stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination

The social groups we belong to help form our identities (Tajfel, 1974): gender, race, ethnicity, nationality, social class, religion, sexual orientation, profession and more, often several at once.

  • A stereotype is a specific belief or assumption about individuals based solely on their membership in a group, regardless of their individual characteristics. Stereotypes are overgeneralized to all members, such as believing all older adults are slow and incompetent. They are generally well known within a culture whether or not you agree with them (Devine, 1989).
  • Prejudice is a negative attitude and feeling toward an individual based solely on membership in a social group (Allport, 1954), such as disliking people not born in the United States without knowing them. It often begins as a stereotype and is common toward unfamiliar cultural groups.
  • Discrimination is negative action toward an individual because of group membership (Allport, 1954), a form of oppression. Example: in the late 1880s, when Harvard did not accept women, Mary Whiton Calkins was given special permission to attend graduate seminars there and studied with William James. She passed all the requirements for a PhD, but Harvard refused to grant it because she was a woman.

All three can also be positive, such as preferential treatment for people who share our gender, race or favorite team. Contact, education and relationships with other groups reduce prejudice; even imagining a positive interaction with someone from another group improves attitudes toward that group and reduces intergroup anxiety (Crisp & Turner, 2009).

Forms of prejudice

When we meet strangers we automatically process three things: their race, gender and age (Ito & Urland, 2003). These categories carry a wealth of information, but often information based on stereotypes.

Why modern prejudice is hard to detect. The dual attitudes model (Wilson, Lindsey, & Schooler, 2000) says we hold explicit attitudes, conscious and controllable, and implicit attitudes, unconscious and uncontrollable. Because egalitarian views are socially desirable, most people show little bias on explicit measures, but implicit measures often reveal mild to strong bias (Greenwald, McGee, & Schwartz, 1998).

Why do prejudice and discrimination exist?

They persist through social learning and conformity to social norms. Children learn prejudiced attitudes from parents, teachers, friends, the media and social media (O’Keeffe & Clarke-Pearson, 2011), and where prejudice is acceptable there may be normative pressure to share it. Example: schools remain somewhat segregated by social class, so how might classmates treat a low-income child who wins a scholarship to a private school?

Self-fulfilling prophecy and confirmation bias

A self-fulfilling prophecy is an expectation that alters behavior in a way that tends to make it true. Teachers’ expectations raised the grades of disadvantaged students they expected to do well (Rosenthal & Jacobson, 1968).

Confirmation bias is seeking out information that supports our stereotypes and ignoring information that is inconsistent with them (Wason & Johnson-Laird, 1972).

In-groups, out-groups and scapegoating

Our groups are a powerful source of identity and self-esteem (Tajfel & Turner, 1979). An in-group is a group we identify with or see ourselves as belonging to; an out-group is one we don’t belong to and view as fundamentally different. Even very young children know their gender in-group and out-group (OpenStax Figure 12.23).

One function of prejudice is to help us feel good about ourselves and our in-groups; threats are often resolved by blaming an out-group. Forces that promote reconciliation between groups include expressing empathy, acknowledging past suffering on both sides and halting destructive behaviors.

Aggression

People can help one another, as educators and healthcare workers did during the COVID-19 pandemic, or do great harm through immoral conformity and obedience. This section turns to the negative side: aggression, bullying and failing to help.

Learning objectives

  • Define aggression
  • Define cyberbullying
  • Describe the bystander effect
Read Section 12.6 on OpenStax ↗

Reducing prejudice

The section opens with two ways researchers have reduced prejudice by having groups work toward shared goals.

  • The Robbers Cave experiment (Sherif et al., 1961): when two opposing groups at a camp worked together toward a common goal, prejudice between them decreased. Focusing on superordinate goals was the key.
  • The jigsaw classroom, designed by Aronson and Bridgeman to increase success in desegregated classrooms. It reduced the potential for prejudice in Australia (Walker & Crogan, 1998).

Hostile and instrumental aggression

Aggression is seeking to cause harm or pain to another person. It takes two forms depending on the motive (Berkowitz, 1993).

Why does aggression exist?

  • Evolutionary function (Buss, 2004): men are more likely than women to show aggression (Wilson & Daly, 1985). Like that of nonhuman primates, male aggression may display dominance over other males, to protect a mate and perpetuate the male’s genes; sexual jealousy is part of it. Women also aggress, typically in more indirect forms as a means to an end, such as communication that impairs another person’s social standing (Dodge & Schwartz, 1997).
  • Frustration-aggression theory (Dollard et al., 1939): when people are prevented from achieving an important goal, they become frustrated and aggressive.

Bullying

Bullying is repeated negative treatment of another person, often an adolescent, over time (Olweus, 1993). A single incident is not bullying. It aims to inflict harm, injury or humiliation and can be physical, verbal or psychological. Boys tend toward direct, physical aggression; girls toward indirect, social aggression such as spreading rumors, ignoring or isolating others.

Bullying involves three parties, the bully, the victim and bystanders, and an imbalance of power favoring the bully. The bully may get a self-esteem boost, but victims face anxiety, depression, poorer schoolwork and, in some cases, suicide (APA, 2010), and bystanders are affected too. There is no single profile, but some children are at greater risk of being bullied (Olweus, 1993):

  • children who are emotionally reactive, because bullies get a quick reaction
  • children who are different from others: overweight, cognitively impaired, or racially or ethnically different from their peers
  • gay, lesbian, bisexual and transgender teens, who are at very high risk

Cyberbullying

Cyberbullying is repeated behavior intended to cause psychological or emotional harm, carried out online. It is typically covert and private, and the bully can remain anonymous, which gives the bully power and leaves the victim feeling helpless and unable to escape or retaliate (Spears et al., 2009).

The bystander effect

The bystander effect is when a witness does not volunteer to help a victim or person in distress and instead just watches (Latané & Darley, 1968). Social psychologists hold that this depends on the social situation, not personality.

The research was prompted by the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese, reported at the time as having no neighbor help or call police. Some facets were reported inaccurately: one man shouted at the killer, a woman said she called the police, a friend comforted her, and neighbors later helped capture the killer (Kassin, 2017). Still, the case helped identify diffusion of responsibility: no one in a group helps because the responsibility is spread across the group (Bandura, 1999).

Because the lit windows showed many witnesses, each assumed someone else had already called police. In general, the more bystanders, the less likely any one person will help, as when you pass a freeway accident and assume someone has already reported it.

Prosocial behavior and altruism

Prosocial behavior is voluntary behavior with the intent to help other people. Altruism is the desire to help others even if the costs outweigh the benefits; altruistic people may disregard the personal cost. On 9/11, an employee in the first World Trade Center tower helped co-workers to the exit stairwell, went back in to help more, and lost his life (Stewart, 2002).

Selfless (altruism)

Altruism operates on empathy, the capacity to understand another person’s perspective and feel what they feel (Batson, 1991). Feeling good afterward is a consequence of helping, not its cause.

Self-serving (egoism)

Helping is always self-serving because our egos are involved and we receive benefits from it (Cialdini et al., 1997).

True motives are hard to test experimentally, so the debate over whether pure altruism exists continues.

Forming relationships

Attraction

We look first for people who are physically attractive, though standards vary and are culturally influenced. Features research suggests are universally attractive include, in women, large eyes, high cheekbones, a narrow jaw line, a slender build (Buss, 1989) and a lower waist-to-hip ratio (Singh, 1993); in men, being tall with broad shoulders and a narrow waist (Buss, 1989). In both, facial and body symmetry is generally considered more attractive. Attractive social traits include warmth, affection and social skills in women, and achievement, leadership and job skills in men (Regan & Berscheid, 1997).

We don’t simply chase the most attractive person: the matching hypothesis says people tend to pick someone they view as their equal in physical attractiveness and social desirability, weighing attractiveness against the likelihood of success (Taylor et al., 2011).

Sternberg’s triangular theory of love

Robert Sternberg (1986) proposed three components of love: intimacy (sharing details and intimate thoughts and emotions), passion (physical attraction, “the flame in the fire”) and commitment (standing by the person, “in sickness and health”). Their combinations define the types of love in the triangular theory of love; which components are most prevalent may change across life stages.

A healthy relationship has all three components, consummate love (Sternberg, 1986). Companionate love, intimacy and commitment without passion, is characteristic of close friendships and family; romantic love has passion and intimacy but no commitment.

Social exchange theory

What decides whether we stay in a relationship? According to social exchange theory, we act as naïve economists, keeping a tally of the costs and benefits of forming and maintaining a relationship (Rusbult & Van Lange, 2003). We want to maximize benefits and minimize costs: we prefer more benefits than costs, or nearly equal ones, and most of us are dissatisfied when costs outweigh benefits.
