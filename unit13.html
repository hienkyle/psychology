Unit 13 · Overview

Industrial-Organizational Psychology

How do people affect work, and how does work affect people? Four sections: the field and its history, choosing and evaluating employees, the social side of work, and designing tools that fit the people who use them.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening question

In October 2019 the U.S. Social Security Administration announced it would end a telework program it had started 6 years earlier, serving about 12,000 employees. The agency cited an increased workload and a backlog of cases: after telework began, average wait times had temporarily increased. The change followed a negotiation with the employees’ union that broke down and had to be brokered by the Federal Services Impasse Panel, a third-party federal body that arbitrates such disputes. The panel’s May 2019 decision let managers limit or restrict telework at their discretion, and the program ended by March 2020 (Wagner, 2019a). Then came COVID-19: Congress asked for a review of the policy and whether telework should return to help slow the spread of the virus (Wagner, 2019b). OpenStax Figure 13.1 shows three home workspaces adapted to people’s lifestyles.

What are the benefits and costs of telework, and what does research say about its effects on organizations and employees? Questions like these belong to industrial and organizational psychology. (Telecommuting returns in Section 13.3.)

Unit 13 · Section 13.1

What Is Industrial and Organizational Psychology?

Industrial and organizational (I-O) psychology studies how human behavior and psychology affect work, and how work affects them. It began with selecting the right people for jobs and grew to include relationships at work and the design of tools.

Learning objectives

  • Understand the scope of study in the field of industrial and organizational psychology
  • Describe the history of industrial and organizational psychology
Read Section 13.1 on OpenStax ↗

Psychology at work

In 2019, people working in the United States spent about 42–54 hours a week working; only sleeping took more time, about 43–62 hours (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2019). Work affects workers’ and their families’ lives in positive and negative, physical and psychological ways. I-O psychologists work in four main contexts: academia, government, consulting firms and business, and most hold a master’s or doctoral degree.

Three broad areas

The field divides into three broad areas (OpenStax Figures 13.2 and 13.3):

  • Industrial psychology describes job requirements and assesses individuals for their ability to meet them; then trains, evaluates and responds to evaluations of employees. Because it judges candidates, it must also consider the legality of discrimination in hiring.
  • Organizational psychology studies how relationships among employees affect those employees and the business: satisfaction, motivation, commitment, management, leadership and culture, and how structures, norms and role expectations shape behavior. It also covers harassment and workplace violence.
  • Human factors psychology studies how workers interact with the tools of work and how to design those tools for productivity, safety and health, from the fit of a desk and chair to reading complex displays quickly and accurately. In Europe it is called ergonomics.

Occupational health and humanitarian work psychology

Occupational health psychology (OHP) deals with the stress, diseases and disorders employees can develop because of the workplace, drawing on medical, biological, psychological, organizational, human factors, human resources and industrial research. It examines how the organization affects an employee’s quality of work life, including spillover into life outside work when work–life balance is poor. Its goal is to improve the individual’s health and well-being and, through that, the organization’s (Society for Occupational Health Psychology, 2020).

Humanitarian work psychology (HWP) began in 2009 when a small group of I-O psychologists met at a conference; they formally formed the group in 2012, and it has about 300 members worldwide. It helps marginalized people, such as people with low income, find work, and helps decide how to deliver aid during major catastrophes. A key idea is KSAs: the knowledge, skills and abilities needed for gainful employment. HWP focuses on lasting skills that serve people throughout their lives (APA, 2016).

Early industrial psychology

I-O psychology began in the early 20th century. Early contributors included James Cattell, Hugo Münsterberg, Walter Dill Scott, Robert Yerkes, Walter Bingham and Lillian Gilbreth. Cattell, Münsterberg and Scott had been students of Wilhelm Wundt, the father of experimental psychology.

  • James Cattell (1860–1944) founded a psychological consulting company still operating today, the Psychological Corporation, and his Columbia students advanced industrial psychology.
  • Hugo Münsterberg (1863–1916) published Psychology and Industrial Efficiency in 1913, covering employee selection, training and effective advertising.
  • Walter Dill Scott (1869–1955) was one of the first to apply psychology to advertising, management and personnel selection. His 1903 books The Theory of Advertising and Psychology of Advertising were the first to describe psychology in business; by 1911 he added Influencing Men in Business and Increasing Human Efficiency in Business. In 1916 the Carnegie Institute of Technology hired him for applied research on employee selection (Katzell & Austin, 1992).

All of this was what we now call industrial psychology; organizational psychology developed later as an experimental science. These researchers also worked directly for businesses as consultants.

World War I

When the United States entered World War I in April 1917, the field expanded into military work. Robert Yerkes was then president of the 25-year-old American Psychological Association (APA), the U.S. professional association for clinical and research psychologists (today it holds conferences, accredits degree programs and publishes journals).

Personnel selection work continued after the war: Millicent Pond, for example, compared factory workers’ pre-employment test results with indicators of their job performance (Vinchur & Koppes, 2014).

The Hawthorne studies

From 1929 to 1932, Elton Mayo (1880–1949) and colleagues ran studies at Western Electric’s Hawthorne Works near Chicago (OpenStax Figure 13.4). They began by studying the physical environment, such as factory lighting, but found the psychological and social factors more interesting, including how supervisorial style raised or lowered productivity. These studies took the field beyond selection into interpersonal relations, motivation and organizational dynamics, and mark the origin of organizational psychology.

Later analysis gave us the Hawthorne effect: an increase in performance by people who know they are being observed by researchers or supervisors (OpenStax Figure 13.5). Any change in a variable such as lighting improved productivity, even a negative one such as a return to poor lighting, and the effect faded when the attention faded (Roethlisberg & Dickson, 1939). Today it is a factor experimenters must control for: a treatment can “work” simply because participants get more attention (McCarney et al., 2007).

Kurt Lewin and group dynamics

In the 1930s researchers began to study employees’ feelings about their jobs. Kurt Lewin, considered the founder of social psychology, studied leadership styles, team structure and team dynamics. An important early study with children tested the effect of leadership style on aggression, group dynamics and satisfaction (Lewin, Lippitt, & White, 1939). Lewin coined the term group dynamics and studied group interaction, cooperation, competition and communication.

Frederick Taylor and scientific management

Human factors psychology developed in parallel. Frederick Taylor, an engineer, saw that redesigning the workplace could raise both company output and workers’ wages. His 1911 book The Principles of Scientific Management (OpenStax Figure 13.6) covered management, personnel selection, training and the work itself. Scientific management analyzes and synthesizes workflows to improve economic efficiency, especially labor productivity. Taylor used time-motion studies: dividing operations into measurable sections to standardize work and check the efficiency of people and equipment. He held that management’s main goal is the most money for the employer along with the best outcome for the employee, reached through training and development.

Personnel selection is the process recruiters within a company use to recruit and select the best candidates; training may follow, depending on the hire’s skills. Companies often hire for a personality that fits in, since skills can be taught but personality cannot easily be changed.

Criticism. Many believed the approach would exploit workers and reduce the number needed. It assumed only a manager could find the most efficient method, and rested on the notion that workers are fundamentally lazy; it maximized productivity with little concern for worker well-being. Unions and people sympathetic to workers criticized it (Van De Water, 1997).

Lillian Gilbreth

Lillian Gilbreth (1878–1972) and her husband Frank used time and motion studies to make work more efficient by reducing the number of motions a task requires, in industry, the home, offices and shops (OpenStax Figure 13.7). She studied employee fatigue and time-management stress and found many employees were motivated by money and job satisfaction. Her 1914 book The Psychology of Management earned her the title mother of modern management. You can thank her for shelves inside refrigerator doors and the foot-pedal trash can. In 1926 she became the first woman to join the American Society of Mechanical Engineers, and in 1966 she received the Hoover Medal.

Taylor’s and Gilbreth’s work also improved the fit between technology and the human using it: the study of machine–human fit is ergonomics, or human factors psychology.

World War II to today

  • World War II: Bingham became chief psychologist for the War Department (now the Department of Defense) and developed systems for job selection, classification, training and performance review, plus team development, morale change and attitude change. Canada and the United Kingdom also saw growth in I-O psychology (McMillan, Stevens, & Kelloway, 2009).
  • After the war: both industrial and organizational psychology became major research areas. Concerns about the fairness of employment tests arose, and evaluations of ethnic and gender bias in tests had mixed results. Job satisfaction and motivation were studied heavily.
  • 1960s: Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act covered equal employment opportunity, protecting employees against discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex or national origin, or for associating with someone in one of these categories. Selection, placement and appraisal research became increasingly important.
  • Today: organizations compete globally and have diverse workforces, so they need to understand how to motivate workers. The Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology (SIOP), a division of the APA, lists 8,000 members (SIOP, 2014), and the Bureau of Labor Statistics (2013) projected the greatest growth of all job classifications in the 20 years after 2012. A master’s holder earns over $80,000 a year on average and a doctorate holder over $110,000 (Khanna, Medsker, & Ginter, 2012).

Industrial Psychology: Selecting and Evaluating Employees

Industrial psychology matches people to tasks: it describes the job, finds applicants who fit it, trains them from their first day, and appraises their performance along the way, all within the law.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the aspects of employee selection
  • Describe the kinds of job training
  • Describe the approaches to and issues surrounding performance assessment
Read Section 13.2 on OpenStax ↗

Job analysis

A job analysis determines and lists the tasks of a particular job. Job advertisements often show the results of two related approaches:

Task-oriented

What the job involves

A detailed list of the tasks, each typically rated for how frequently it is performed, how difficult it is and how important it is.

Worker-oriented

Job specification

The characteristics a worker needs to succeed: the knowledge, skills and abilities (KSAs) the job requires (Dierdorff & Wilson, 2003).

Information for both comes from observation, surveys and interviews: watching someone proficient at the job, or interviewing current job holders, their peers and supervisors for a consensus. Accuracy depends on the source: analyses built from job holders’ own descriptions were the least reliable, though the researchers did not study why (Dierdorff & Wilson, 2003).

The U.S. Department of Labor keeps a database of job analyses called O*NET (onetonline.org). Each occupation lists its tasks, knowledge, skills, abilities, work context and activities, education, interests, personality requirements and work styles, along with average earnings and projected job growth (OpenStax Figure 13.8 shows the entry for a municipal firefighter).

Candidate analysis and testing

Candidates’ KSAs are evaluated against the job description through testing, interviews, and work samples or exercises. Personality tests try to match a candidate’s traits to those that predict good performance: high agreeableness might suit customer support. But the link is not always clear: agreeing with a customer’s misperception will not resolve the complaint, so too much agreeableness may hinder. Any personality test should come with a verified assessment of which scores correlate with good performance (Arthur, Woehr, & Graziano, 2001). Other tests include IQ tests, integrity tests and physical tests such as drug or fitness tests.

What do you think? Cutoff scores

Organizations may set a cutoff score on a test: a score below which a candidate will not move forward. Robert Jordan, a 49-year-old college graduate, applied to the police force in New London, Connecticut, and took the Wonderlic Personnel Test (WPT), a measure of cognitive ability. His score was too high. The department does not interview anyone scoring over 27, believing they would be bored with police work; the average for police officers nationwide is equivalent to an IQ of 104 (Jordan v. New London, 2000; ABC News, 2000).

Jordan sued, alleging discrimination and denial of equal protection. The 2nd U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals upheld a lower court’s decision that the city did not discriminate, because the same standard was applied to everyone who took the exam (The New York Times, 1999). When might universal cutoffs make sense, and when might they screen out strong employees?

Interviews

Most jobs at mid-size to large U.S. businesses require a personal interview, and its questions are usually based on the job analysis. Interviews give a more dynamic picture than standard tests, but social factors and body language sway the outcome: how similar the applicant is to the interviewer, and nonverbal behaviors such as hand gestures, head nodding and smiling (Bye et al., 2014; Rakić, Steffens, & Mummendey, 2011).

Everyday connection: preparing for the interview

A limited amount of research addresses how candidates can improve their chances:

  • Nonverbal cues matter. Little eye contact and smiling led to lower ratings (Liden, Martin, & Parsons, 1993).
  • Self-promotion generally helps, especially when it shows person–job fit (Gilmore & Ferris, 1989), but experienced interviewers can see it overdone (Howard & Ferris, 1996). Extroverts tend to self-promote verbally; applicants high in agreeableness use smiling and eye contact.
  • First impressions during pre-interview rapport building related to job offers, and were judgments of competence, not just likability (Barrick, Swider, & Stewart, 2010).
  • In mock interviews, smiling raised likability; more eye contact made women more likable but men less, though men were interviewed by men and women by women (Levine & Feldman, 2002).
  • In a real setting, visual and vocal cues of conscientiousness and openness, and vocal cues of extroversion, raised interviewer judgments; visual cues are easier for a candidate to change than vocal ones (DeGroot & Gooty, 2009).

Take-home message: be aware of your behavior, practice with mock interviews and ask for feedback, project confidence and positive traits, and mind the first impression.

Training and mentoring

Most jobs begin with orientation: company history, policies, and protocols such as time tracking, benefits and reporting. A key goal is teaching the organizational culture: its values, visions, hierarchies, norms and how employees interact (OpenStax Figure 13.9). Job-specific training, and later training on new duties or tools, follow. Much of it is formal: software presentations or group presentations by HR or the new hire’s department.

Mentoring is informal training in which an experienced employee guides a new employee’s work; mentors may be assigned or the relationship may form on its own. Research findings:

  • For the protégé: small but significant effects on performance, motivation, satisfaction and career outcomes (Eby et al., 2008); higher compensation, more promotions, and greater career and job satisfaction (Allen et al., 2004).
  • For the mentor: greater job satisfaction and organizational commitment (Eby et al., 2006), better supervisor performance ratings (Gentry, Weber, & Sadri, 2008), higher salaries and more promotions (Allen, Lentz, & Day, 2006).
  • For women: mentoring is particularly important, creating connections to informal networks, reducing isolation and helping overcome discrimination in promotion (McKeen & Bujaki, 2007).
  • Gender and race: women and members of underrepresented groups do not lack mentors, but lack mentors of the same gender, race and ethnicity, because senior roles are mostly held by White men (MLDC, 2010). Same-group pairs tend to give better psychosocial outcomes such as feeling supported, while different-group mentors tend to bring greater career advancement and pay (Parks-Yancy, 2012). Results are mixed.

Does training work? A review of many studies found it effective on all four measures (Arthur, Bennett, Edens, & Bell, 2003): (1) employees’ immediate reaction, (2) end-of-training tests of learning, (3) supervisors’ measures of job behavior and (4) results such as productivity and profits.

Performance appraisal

A performance appraisal evaluates how well an employee performs the duties of the job. I-O psychologists design these systems to be fair and positive and to reduce subjectivity. Fair evaluation helps people do their jobs better, puts people in the right jobs, and identifies training needs. Appraisals are documented several times a year, often with an annual face-to-face meeting that should draw on the original job analysis and any goals set. The meeting covers concerns and reinforces good performance, may discuss rewards (a raise) or consequences (probation), and documents poor performance to support a decision to terminate. Appraisals are also used to motivate employees and to spot training opportunities.

In 360-degree feedback appraisal, ratings come from supervisors, peers, the people the employee supervises, the employee, and sometimes customers. It gives different perspectives to guide improvement and more information for decisions on position and pay (Tornow, 1993a). Few studies test it; one found self and peer ratings unreliable (Atkins & Wood, 2002), but the disagreement can also be a chance to learn as employee and supervisor discuss it (Tornow, 1993b).

Most employees actually ask for feedback if it is not offered (DeNisi & Kluger, 2000), yet many evaluations are disliked by organizations, employees or both (Fletcher, 2001), and few have been tested for whether they improve performance. One reason is that systems are used incorrectly or do not suit the organization’s culture (Schraeder, Becton, & Portis, 2007): how it is run, operates and makes decisions, based on its values, hierarchies and interactions.

Bias and protections in hiring

Ideally, hiring matches an accurate job analysis to candidates’ KSAs. In practice other factors intrude:

  • Similarity to the interviewer (Bye et al., 2014) and the interviewee’s regional accent (Rakić, Steffens, & Mummendey, 2011).
  • Weight: shown photos of equally qualified obese and non-obese applicants, hiring managers who scored high on a test of automatic, subconscious negative associations with overweight people favored inviting the non-obese applicant (Agerström & Rooth, 2011).
  • Attractiveness benefited hiring, promotion and performance reviews, though the benefit appeared to shrink between the late 1970s and late 1990s (Hosoda, Stone-Romero, & Coats, 2003).

Unless membership in a group directly affects potential job performance, a decision based on it is discriminatory (OpenStax Figure 13.11 shows pregnancy, religion and age). U.S. city, state and federal laws limit what an interviewer may ask:

The EEOC and anti-discrimination law

The gender pay gap

Despite the Equal Pay Act, women earned about 82 cents for every dollar men earned in their first year after college. Correcting for education, career and life choices (such as majors with lower earning potential) left an unexplained 7-cent gap attributed to gender discrimination (Corbett & Hill, 2012). This human capital model has been criticized: those choices may themselves be constrained by gender discrimination, so removing them makes the estimated gap too small (Lips, 2013).

Complaints and lawsuits

The EEOC received more than 94,000 discrimination charges in 2013, many for multiple forms of discrimination, including retaliation for making a claim, which is itself illegal. Only 148 suits were filed in federal courts that year (EEOC, 2014). In 2011 the Supreme Court denied women employees of Walmart the right to sue for gender discrimination in promotion and pay as a class action, the only practical way for individuals to sustain such a case, though the case publicized the issue.

Sexual orientation and gender identity

Before the 2020 ruling, federal law protected only federal employees, and the District of Columbia and 20 states protected public and private employees from discrimination for sexual orientation; most also covered gender identity, one’s sense of being male, female, neither, both or another gender. Evidence of discrimination comes from worker surveys, complaint filings, wage comparisons and controlled job-interview studies (Badgett et al., 2009). The ruling will be continually tested: the First Amendment protects religious organizations from some aspects of anti-discrimination law, and some recent decisions have widened these exceptions.

The Americans with Disabilities Act

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) of 1990, people may not be discriminated against because of a disability: a physical or mental impairment that limits one or more major life activities, such as hearing, walking and breathing. Employers must make reasonable accommodations, such as ramps, readers for blind personnel or more frequent breaks. The ADA has been expanded to include alcoholism, former drug use, obesity and psychiatric disabilities. Its premise: people with disabilities can contribute to an organization (O’Keefe & Bruyere, 1994).

Bona fide occupational qualifications

A bona fide occupational qualification (BFOQ) is a requirement of certain occupations for which denying someone employment would otherwise violate the law. Religion, national origin, age and sex can be BFOQs; race never is, although the First Amendment lets artistic works such as films make race a requirement of a role. Examples: a specific religion for a leadership role in a worship facility or the president of a religiously affiliated university; age for airline pilots (mandatory retirement for safety); sex for guards in male prisons.

  • Describe key elements of management and leadership

Job satisfaction

Job satisfaction is the degree to which people enjoy their job; Locke (1976) described it as the feeling that results from appraising one’s job experiences. It comes from both how we think (cognition) and how we feel (affect) about our work, but is described in terms of affect. It depends on the work itself, our personality and our culture (Saari & Judge, 2004).

It is measured after organizational changes (such as a new management model), routinely as one predictor of performance, and nationally by polling companies such as Gallup (Saad, 2012). Employees complete questionnaires, sometimes a single question on a Likert scale: typically five answers that show positive-to-negative strength of agreement or feeling.

More often a survey asks several questions to find why employees are satisfied or not. Surveys may be job-specific or general, and measure satisfaction at a global level or by specific factors:

What predicts satisfaction, and why it matters

Strongest predictor

Work content

Variety, difficulty level and role clarity predict overall satisfaction most strongly (Saari & Judge, 2004).

Weak link

Pay level

Only weakly correlated: people adapt to higher pay, so a raise stops satisfying (Judge et al., 2010).

Why should employers care? Satisfaction is (Judge & Kammeyer-Mueller, 2012, unless noted):

  • Somewhat correlated with job performance, especially organizational citizenship: discretionary behaviors that further the organization’s goals.
  • Reciprocally linked to life satisfaction: each affects the other positively (Judge & Watanabe, 1993).
  • When low, related to withdrawal such as absenteeism or leaving; the link with turnover itself is weak.
  • Related to organizational performance, suggesting that raising satisfaction improves it.

Weiss (2002) suggests measures would be more reliable if they separated the emotional and cognitive elements of satisfaction.

Dig deeper: job satisfaction in federal agencies

A 2013 study found federal job satisfaction plummeting compared with the private sector, driven mostly by pay, then training and development. The Partnership for Public Service, a nonprofit, nonpartisan group, has tracked it since 2003. Among large agencies, NASA ranked highest, followed by the Department of Commerce and the intelligence community; the Department of Homeland Security ranked lowest. The score uses three Federal Employee Viewpoint Survey items with six possible answers: “I recommend my organization as a good place to work,” and how satisfied you are, considering everything, with your job and with your organization. Would these questions capture your own satisfaction?

Job stress, downsizing and mergers

Job stress (job strain) comes from stressors in an occupation and lowers job satisfaction. Stress is the perception of and response to events judged overwhelming or threatening to well-being; the events are the stressors. It arises when employees perceive that demands exceed their ability to meet them (Gyllensten & Palmer, 2005), for example multiple roles, role ambiguity, no career progress, job insecurity, no control over outcomes, isolation, overload, discrimination, harassment and bullying (Colligan & Higgins, 2005). Stressors differ for women and men. Job stress harms employee health, job performance and family life.

Two growing threats to job security, often managed with I-O psychologists’ help:

  • Downsizing: laying off a significant percentage of employees to achieve greater overall efficiency, often after failing to meet profit goals. I-O psychologists help with how the news is delivered, support for those laid off (such as separation packages) and support for those who stay, whose intent to quit, commitment and job insecurity are all affected (Ugboro, 2006).
  • Mergers and acquisitions: a merger joins two organizations of equal power and status; in an acquisition one buys the other and is usually the dominant partner. Duplicate departments must be combined, usually with staff cuts, and employees must adjust to a changed culture and lose their connection to the old organization (van Knippenberg et al., 2002; Amiot et al., 2006).

Work–family balance

Work–family balance is juggling the demands of work life with family life, such as caring for children or an elderly parent. Conflict runs both ways: work can interfere with family, and family with work (Carlson, Kacmar, & Williams, 2000). Greenhaus and Beutell (1985) identified three sources, each in either direction:

  1. Time devoted to one makes the other’s requirements hard to fulfill.
  2. Strain from one makes the other’s requirements hard to fulfill.
  3. Specific behaviors required by one make the other’s requirements hard to fulfill.

Women often carry more family responsibility and report greater stress from work–family conflict, though U.S. men are taking on a growing share at home (Gyllensten & Palmer, 2005). Support can come from home (emotional: listening; practical: help with chores) or work: understanding supervisors, flextime (required core hours, with arrival and departure scheduled around them), paid leave and telecommuting: working from home and setting one’s own hours. Some organizations offer onsite daycare, fitness centers or health clinics.

Example policies (Posig & Kickul, 2004): IBM’s three years of job-guaranteed leave after a birth, Lucent Technologies’ one year of childbirth leave at half pay, and SC Johnson’s concierge services for daytime errands.

Theory X and Theory Y

Douglas McGregor (1960) combined scientific management and human relations into two styles of leadership behavior. Theory X assumes workers are lazy and unproductive, so managers control them with punishments; Theory Y assumes people seek inner satisfaction from work and do best when they help set goals. McGregor saw Theory X as the traditional U.S. method and argued Theory Y was needed to improve output and well-being.

Strengths-based management

Donald Clifton and colleagues interviewed 8,000 managers and concluded that organizations should focus on people’s strengths, not their weaknesses. A strength is an enduring talent that gives consistent, near-perfect performance in tasks involving it, such as public speaking or planning a successful event; strengths offer the greatest room for growth (Buckingham & Clifton, 2001). The approach is popular but its effect on performance is not well studied, and managers often neglected their weaknesses and overused their strengths, both of which hurt performance (Kaiser & Overfield, 2011).

Transactional and transformational leadership

Bass (1985) popularized two leadership styles:

Maintains the status quo

Transactional

Focuses on supervision and organizational goals, reached through rewards and punishments (transactions). Transactional leaders are managers.

Seeks to change the organization

Transformational

Has four attributes to varying degrees: charismatic (a highly liked role model), inspirational (optimistic about reaching goals), intellectually stimulating (encourages critical thinking and problem solving) and considerate (Bass, Avolio, & Atwater, 1996).

Research shows transformational approaches are more effective, though most leaders show elements of both. Gender differences are small and not absolute:

  • Women slightly tended toward an interpersonal style (morale and welfare of employees) and men toward a task-oriented style, but the difference shrank when only real organizational leaders were studied. Differences were larger and consistent for democratic versus autocratic styles (Eagly & Johnson, 1990).
  • Women tended to be more transformational and men more transactional, yet women used rewards for performance, a transactional element, more often than men (Eagly, Johannesen-Schmidt, & van Engen, 2003).

Eagly and Johnson attribute the similarities to everyone conforming to the organization’s culture, and the differences to strengths each gender brings.

Followership

Emerging followership research studies the leader–follower relationship in both directions. People differ, so there is probably no single best leader–follower dynamic: not every leadership style works with every follower.

Goals, teamwork and work teams

Changes in technology, economics, foreign competition, globalization and demographics push organizations to respond quickly, and many delegate work to work teams: groups given a specific task to achieve together, bringing diverse skills, experience and expertise. This contrasts with structures that have individuals at their base. But teams do not always deliver greater productivity (Naquin & Tynan, 2003). Teams can:

  • mask members who are not working (social loafing);
  • communicate poorly, decide poorly because of conformity, and suffer internal conflict;
  • benefit from the team halo effect: teams get credit for successes, while individuals within them are blamed for failures, which may partly explain their popularity.

Gender mix: diversity can cause communication problems but can also widen the team’s skills. In business-school project teams, gender-balanced teams outperformed mostly-male teams in sales and profits; there was not enough data on mostly-female teams, and the mechanism was not identified (Hoogendoorn, Oosterbeek, & van Praag, 2013).

There are three basic types of team (Larson & LaFasto, 1989): problem resolution teams solve a particular problem; creative teams develop innovative possibilities; tactical teams execute a well-defined plan. A fourth, the virtual team, brings together geographically scattered people through digital communication; it was growing even before the COVID-19 pandemic (Powell, Piccoli, & Ives, 2004).

Organizational culture

Organizational culture is an organization’s values, visions, hierarchies, norms and interactions among employees: how it is run, operates and makes decisions. Its industry may influence it, and departments can form their own subcultures. Ostroff, Kinicki and Tamkins (2003) describe three layers, and researchers have built surveys to measure culture.

Diversity training and intergroup contact

In a global workforce you may work with suppliers, employees and coworkers from many countries, religions, ethnic and racial backgrounds. Diversity training educates employees about cultural differences to improve teamwork. Prejudice between groups is always possible, but simply working together, under carefully set conditions, can reduce or eliminate it. A meta-analysis found contact between groups reduced prejudice, a moderate but significant effect, which was stronger under four conditions (Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006):

  • Equal standing
  • Common goals
  • Cooperation between the groups
  • Support from the institution or authorities

Dig deeper: managing generational differences

Workers’ expectations are shaped partly by the period they grew up in. Generational labels are somewhat arbitrary but can guide management as one generation leaves and another enters (Elmore, 2010):

Born 1946–1964

Baby boomers

Leaving the workforce over the next decade or more; may believe they paid their dues in their time.

Early 1960s to 1980s

Generation X

Mid-career; do not expect to stay with one employer, since they do not expect employers to be loyal. Retained by meaningful work (Gibson, Greenwood, & Murphy, 2009).

Born 1979–1994

Millennials

Early-career and technologically sophisticated; characterized as self-centered and overconfident. Value life outside work, want choices and training, and are retained with frequent rewards, praise and feedback (Myers & Sadaghiani, 2010).

One suggestion is reverse mentoring: a younger employee teaches a senior one social media or digital resources, and the senior employee can offer guidance in a less demanding role (Meister & Willyerd, 2010).

Harassment

A culture of harassment is a well-recognized negative side of organizational culture; most organizations of any size have policies and training on it. Sexual harassment is sexually based behavior that is knowingly unwanted and affects a person’s employment status, interferes with their job performance, or creates a hostile or intimidating work environment (after the EEOC’s description).

“Give something to get something”

Quid pro quo

Organizational rewards offered in exchange for sexual favors, usually by someone with more power: a kiss or touch for a promotion, positive review or raise. Threatening to withhold a reward if a request is refused is another form.

A pattern, not one event

Hostile environment

Workplace conditions that are hostile or intimidating, such as offensive language or jokes or sexually explicit images. Isolated occurrences are not harassment; a pattern of repeated occurrences is.

Both forms violate organizational policies and are illegal. Harassment need not be sexual: it can relate to any class protected by EEOC statutes, such as race, national origin, religion or age.

Violence in the workplace

Workplace violence is any act or threat of physical violence, harassment, intimidation or other threatening, disruptive behavior at the workplace, from threats and verbal abuse to assault and homicide (OSHA, 2014). It may target coworkers, supervisors or property. Warning signs include intimidating behavior, threats, sabotaging equipment and radical changes in behavior, often escalating; employees who feel unsafe should involve their immediate supervisor.

  • Murder is the second leading cause of death in the workplace and the primary cause of death for women at work; many are killed by boyfriends or husbands who choose the woman’s workplace.
  • Nearly two million workers are physically assaulted or threatened with assault each year.

A major trigger is feeling treated unfairly, unjustly or disrespectfully. This shows the importance of procedural justice: the fairness of the processes by which outcomes are decided in conflicts with or among employees.

Human Factors Psychology and Workplace Design

The third area of I-O psychology designs machines, workstations and displays that fit human requirements, for productivity, safety and satisfaction.

Fitting machines to people

Human factors psychology (or ergonomics, the term favored in Europe) integrates the human–machine interface in the workplace through design, especially machines that fit human requirements. The fit may be physical, cognitive or both. Anyone unconvinced need only try an unfamiliar TV remote or new software. The other two areas focus on the worker and the team, group or organization; human factors focuses on the individual worker’s interaction with a machine, workstation, information display or the local environment, such as lighting. In the United States it has roots in both psychology and engineering, reflected in Lillian Gilbreth (psychologist and engineer) and Frank Gilbreth (engineer).

When human factors professionals get involved

From the beginning

Design

More common in software design projects.

Toward the end

Testing and evaluation

More common in traditional industries (Howell, 2003).

They also develop regulations and best-design principles, often for safety. After the Three Mile Island nuclear accident, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission required more instrumentation to give operators critical information, plus more operator training (NRC, 2013). The American National Standards Institute (ANSI), an independent developer of industrial standards, sets ergonomic standards such as the design of control-center workstations for transportation or industrial process control.

Safety and checklists

Many human factors concerns involve workplace safety: preventing injuries to workers and those around them, and setting protocols for activities that affect the public, such as commercial driving or flying, medical procedures and law enforcement.

A checklist is a method used to reduce workplace accidents. Pilots work through a detailed checklist of aircraft parts before takeoff, as do astronauts. The World Health Organization’s surgical safety checklist (OpenStax Figure 13.15) is the basis for many checklists at medical facilities. Safety concerns also limit how long operators may work: the Federal Aviation Administration recently introduced limits on how long a pilot may fly without an overnight break.

Research example: decisions under load

Bruno and Abrahão (2012) studied an information security center at a Brazilian bank: about 45,000 decisions by 35 operators and 4 managers over 60 days. As decisions per day, and so cognitive effort, climbed, operators more often falsely identified incidents as real security breaches. The opposite mistake, dismissing real intrusions as false alarms, did not increase. That is good news for the bank: false alarms cost less than missing a genuine threat.

Studies like this combine research on attention, perception, teamwork and human–computer interaction. The same context lay behind Target’s massive data breach in fall 2013: security staff apparently received signals of the breach but did not interpret them correctly, and it continued for two weeks until the FBI told the company (Riley et al., 2014).
