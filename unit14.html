Unit 14 · Overview

Stress, Lifestyle, and Health

What stress is and where it comes from, how it can make us ill, how we cope with it, and what makes people happy. Five sections, from the body’s alarm system to positive psychology.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening question

College life brings the familiar pressures of exams and term papers (OpenStax Figure 14.1), and today’s students also face rising tuition, heavy debt and a hard job market after graduation. Many nontraditional students raise children or work full time while they study. Outside school there are money worries, conflicts with friends or neighbors, family duties and too little free time, plus minor hassles such as lost items, traffic jams and internet outages that slowly wear down well-being.

How do these pressures affect our minds and bodies, and how can we manage them rather than be overwhelmed by them? Psychology has studied stress for almost a century; this chapter looks at its psychological and physiological nature, its causes and effects, and ways to handle it.

Unit 14 · Section 14.1

What Is Stress?

Stress is not simply a bad event or a racing heart. It is a process: how we appraise an event, and how our mind and body respond to it.

Learning objectives

  • Differentiate stimulus-based and response-based definitions of stress
  • Define stress as a process
  • Differentiate good stress from bad stress
  • Describe the early contributions of Walter Cannon and Hans Selye to the stress research field
  • Understand the physiological basis of stress and describe the general adaptation syndrome
Read Section 14.1 on OpenStax ↗

Defining stress

The term stress entered the scientific literature in the 1930s and popular speech in the 1970s (Lyon, 2012). We use it loosely for almost any unpleasant feeling, which makes it hard to define. Two early approaches each captured only part of the picture:

Stimulus-based

Stress is the event

Stress is a challenging or threatening situation, such as a high-stress job, overcrowding or a long commute. Problem: people differ in how they view and react to the same event.

Response-based

Stress is the body’s reaction

Selye (1976) called stress the response of the body to any demand. Problem: pleasant events, such as an unexpected promotion or raise, can produce the same physical reactions.

Today, stress is best seen as a process: a person perceives and responds to events they appraise as overwhelming or threatening to their well-being (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). The events themselves are stressors: environmental events that may be judged threatening or demanding.

Primary and secondary appraisal

Whether an event becomes stressful depends on two judgments, made in quick succession (OpenStax Figure 14.3):

  • Primary appraisal: how much harm or threat the stressor poses. An event can be seen as a threat (possible harm or loss) or a challenge (a chance to grow). Graduation, for example, can be a threat (losing financial support from parents) or a challenge (a chance to become independent; OpenStax Figure 14.2).
  • Secondary appraisal: what coping options are available and how well they might work. A belief that we can use them effectively, self-efficacy, matters here (Lyon, 2012; Bandura, 1994).

An event appraised as threatening with no effective options produces the most stress. The text’s example: Robin and Madhuri each find a lump in the lower region of the left breast during a self-exam. Both see it as a threat, but they differ in secondary appraisal.

Appraisal still matters for events that are objectively threatening (Everly & Lating, 2002). As Selye (1976) put it, stress is largely in the eye of the beholder.

Good stress?

Not all stress is harmful. Selye (1974) described eustress, “good” stress at a moderate level, linked to positive feelings, good health and good performance. Moderate stress can even help memory: participants exposed to a mild stressor showed better recall of a scientific text, both right away and a day later (Hupbach & Fieman, 2012).

Past an optimal level, though, stress turns into distress: excessive stress that Selye linked to exhaustion, burnout and the erosion of health (Everly & Lating, 2002). Severe test anxiety, which can wreck exam performance, is an example. The relation between stress and performance therefore traces an inverted U (OpenStax Figure 14.4).

How common is stress?

Stress appears to have risen in recent years: in a survey, nearly half of U.S. adults said their stress had increased over the previous five years (Neelakantan, 2013). Stress brings physiological, cognitive and behavioral responses, and it can contribute to physical illness (Cohen & Herbert, 1996).

This link to illness is why stress is a central topic of health psychology, the subfield, which emerged in the 1970s, that studies how psychological factors influence health, illness and how people respond to illness (Taylor, 1999; Straub, 2007).

Who is most stressed? National surveys in 1983, 2006 and 2009 (Cohen & Janicki-Deverts, 2012; OpenStax Figure 14.6) found that:

  • women reported more stress than men in all three surveys;
  • the unemployed, and people with less education and income, reported high stress; retirees reported the lowest;
  • from 2006 to 2009 the largest increases were among men, Hispanic people aged 45–64, college graduates and people employed full time, plausibly because of the 2008–2009 economic downturn.

Walter Cannon and the fight-or-flight response

Early in the 20th century the Harvard physiologist Walter Cannon (OpenStax Figure 14.7) described how the body reacts to threat. Picture meeting a bear in the woods: your heart races, you breathe faster, your pupils dilate, you sweat and your stomach churns. Cannon (1932) named this the fight-or-flight response: when a threat is perceived, the sympathetic nervous system and the endocrine system raise blood pressure, heart rate, respiration and perspiration, readying the body to fight the threat or flee from it.

Cannon saw the response as adaptive: it helps the body protect itself and then return to homeostasis, its stable internal balance. Trouble starts when the response is triggered too often or for too long.

Hans Selye and the general adaptation syndrome

In the 1930s, Hans Selye (OpenStax Figure 14.9 shows a 2009 Hungarian stamp in his honor) was studying sex hormones in rats at McGill University. Whatever prolonged stressor he used, he found the same three physical changes: enlarged adrenal glands, a shrunken thymus and lymph glands (both part of the immune system), and stomach ulcers. Because the reaction was the same whatever the stressor, he called it nonspecific and named it the general adaptation syndrome (GAS): the body’s three-stage response to prolonged stress (Selye, 1936, 1976).

  • Alarm reaction: the first stage, the body’s immediate reaction to a threat, much like fight or flight.
  • Stage of resistance: the second stage, in which the body adapts to the stressor over time.
  • Stage of exhaustion: the third stage, in which resistance is depleted and illness, damage or death may follow.

Selye’s model is response-based and largely ignores psychological appraisal. Its lasting contribution was to show how stress can physically damage the body and contribute to disease, including hypertension and coronary artery disease.

Stressors

Stressors range from rare catastrophes to the daily commute. Researchers measure them with checklists of life events and study what makes some jobs and relationships so wearing.

  • Explain the importance of life changes as potential stressors
  • Describe the Social Readjustment Rating Scale
  • Understand the concepts of job strain and job burnout
Read Section 14.2 on OpenStax ↗

Kinds of stressors

For an event to be a stressor, it must be appraised as threatening or overwhelming. Stressors can be sorted by how long they last (Cohen, Janicki-Deverts, & Miller, 2007):

Chronic

Persist over time

Caring for a parent with dementia, long-term unemployment, imprisonment.

Acute

Brief, focal events

Short-lived but sometimes overwhelming, such as breaking a leg on an icy sidewalk.

They also fall into broad categories: traumatic events, significant life changes, daily hassles, and situations of regular exposure to threat, challenge or danger, such as a demanding job.

Traumatic events

Traumatic events are extremely threatening, often life-threatening, experiences: military combat, physical or sexual assault, robbery, childhood abuse, terrorist attacks, natural disasters and automobile accidents. Men, non-White people and people of lower socioeconomic status report more traumatic events than women, White people and people of higher status (Hatch & Dohrenwend, 2007).

Some people develop posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), a chronic reaction that can include intrusive memories, jumpiness, persistent negative emotions, detachment from others, angry outbursts and avoidance of reminders of the event (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). PTSD returns in Unit 15.

Life changes and the Social Readjustment Rating Scale

Most stressors are everyday events that require us to adjust: the death of a loved one, marriage, divorce, moving (OpenStax Figure 14.12). Even a move we want can change daily life enough to be stressful.

In the 1960s, Thomas Holmes and Richard Rahe proposed that life events requiring readjustment, whether desirable or undesirable, raise the risk of illness, especially when several come close together. Pleasant events such as marriage and holidays count too: they are examples of eustress. Holmes and Rahe built the Social Readjustment Rating Scale (SRRS): 43 life events, each scored by how much readjustment it requires (Holmes & Rahe, 1967).

  • How the scores were made: 394 participants estimated the readjustment each event requires; the mean ratings became life change units (LCUs) (Rahe, McKeen, & Arthur, 1967), from 11 to 100.
  • How it is used: respondents tick the events they experienced in the past 12 months and add up the LCUs.
  • Agreement: people rate the events very consistently, even across cultures (Holmes & Masuda, 1974).

SRRS research and criticism

High LCU totals have been linked to physical illness, accidents, athletic injuries and mental health problems within the following one to two years (Monat & Lazarus, 1991; Scully, Tosi, & Banning, 2000). Navy personnel’s LCU scores before a six-month voyage had small positive correlations with illness during the voyage (Rahe, 1974), and symptoms such as backache, upset stomach, diarrhea and acne appeared on days with many LCUs, such as a family member’s wedding (Holmes & Holmes, 1970).

Connect the concepts: correlation is not causation

SRRS studies are correlational (Unit 2): they show that higher LCU totals go with more symptoms, but they cannot show that stress causes illness. The section asks you to consider the reverse too: could illness cause stress?

Criticisms of the SRRS:

  • Many items are vague: “death of a close friend” could be a long-absent childhood friend, whose death needs little readjustment (Dohrenwend, 2006).
  • It assumes desirable and undesirable events are equally stressful (Derogatis & Coons, 1993), yet negative events are more strongly tied to poor outcomes such as depression (Hatch & Dohrenwend, 2007).
  • It ignores appraisal: the same event can be threatening for one person and a challenge for another (Scully et al., 2000).

Hassles

Daily hassles are the minor irritations and annoyances of everyday life: traffic, misplaced keys, difficult coworkers, bad weather, arguments (OpenStax Figure 14.13 shows two commutes). Each is small, but together they can add up to as much stress as a major life change (Kanner, Coyne, Schaefer, & Lazarus, 1981).

  • Among San Francisco residents, the frequency of daily hassles predicted physical and psychological health better than LCU totals (DeLongis, Coyne, Dakof, Folkman, & Lazarus, 1982).
  • Minor hassles, especially interpersonal conflicts, drive day-to-day distressed mood (Bolger, DeLongis, Kessler, & Schilling, 1989).
  • Cyber hassles are newer: stress from social media use has been linked to sleep loss in adolescents (van der Schuur, Baumgartner, & Sumter, 2018).

Stress at work

Jobs that are challenging, demanding or unsafe are common stressors. Some, such as police work and firefighting (OpenStax Figure 14.14), are stressful by nature; others add noise, harassment, frustration or alternating shifts (Sulsky & Smith, 2005).

Job strain

Those two themes combine in job strain: a work situation with high demands but little control or discretion over decisions (Karasek & Theorell, 1990).

Job strain is linked to hypertension (Schnall & Landsbergis, 1994), heart attacks (Theorell et al., 1998), recurrent heart disease after a first heart attack (Aboa-Éboulé et al., 2007), significant weight loss or gain (Kivimäki et al., 2006) and major depressive disorder (Stansfeld, Shipley, Head, & Fuhrer, 2012). In a study of more than 10,000 British civil servants, workers under 50 with high job strain were 68% more likely to develop heart disease later (Chandola et al., 2008).

Job burnout

Long-term job stress can lead to job burnout: a general sense of emotional exhaustion and cynicism about one’s job (Maslach & Jackson, 1981). It has three dimensions:

1

Exhaustion

Feeling emotionally drained and worn out.

2

Depersonalization

Callous, cynical or indifferent attitudes toward the people one serves.

3

Diminished accomplishment

A sense of failing or achieving little at work.

Burnout is common in human service jobs, such as those of social workers, teachers, therapists and police officers. Job strain is a major risk factor; others include heavy drinking, physical inactivity, being overweight and having a physical or lifetime mental disorder (Ahola et al., 2006). It often comes with depression: among more than 3,000 Finnish employees, about half of those with severe burnout had a depressive disorder (Ahola et al., 2005). Burnout tends to follow situations in which a person invests a lot but gets little back (Taris et al., 2001).

A clip from the 1999 comedy Office Space, linked in the section, shows a worker facing an unsupportive supervisor (see Resources).

Close relationships

The negative side of close relationships, such as arguments, lack of support and unequal give-and-take, is a potent stressor. In a study of more than 9,000 British civil servants, those with the most negative interactions in close relationships were 34% more likely to develop serious heart problems over the next 13–15 years (De Vogli, Chandola, & Marmot, 2007).

Unit 14 · Section 14.3

Stress and Illness

The stress response evolved for short physical emergencies. Switched on for months by worries about money, work and relationships, it wears the body down, from the immune system to the heart and lungs.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the immune system and how stress impacts its functioning
  • Describe how stress and emotional factors can lead to the development and exacerbation of cardiovascular disorders, asthma, and tension headaches
Read Section 14.3 on OpenStax ↗

Psychophysiological disorders

The neuroscientist Robert Sapolsky (1998) explains stress-related disease this way: the stress response serves us well in a short physical emergency, but we turn it on for months at a time over worries about money, relationships and work. Over time, this repeated or prolonged activation causes wear and tear on the body, such as high blood pressure from job strain or weakened immunity from too much cortisol.

Psychophysiological disorders are physical diseases whose symptoms are brought about or worsened by stress and emotional factors. The name joins psycho (psychological causes) and physiological (physical symptoms).

A disease-prone personality? Reviewing 101 studies, Friedman and Booth-Kewley (1987) proposed three disease-prone traits: depression, anger/hostility and anxiety. Later work agrees: in a Norwegian study of more than 61,000 people, depression was linked to many major causes of death (Mykletun et al., 2007), and neuroticism is a risk factor for chronic illness and death (Ploubidis & Grundy, 2009).

In the section’s Link to Learning, Sapolsky and Carol Shively show how a primate’s place in the social hierarchy predicts its stress, mental health and disease (videos in Resources).

Everyday connection: social status, stress and health care

People of lower social status tend to face more stress, have less control over their lives and have fewer resources to deal with it. Access to care differs too. In the United States, African American women have the highest rates of emergency room visits and of unmet health needs, and that gap widened from 2006 to 2014 (Manuel, 2018). LGBTQ youth often receive poor care because of stigma (Hafeez et al., 2017). The U.S. Healthy People 2020 initiative set a goal of eliminating such disparities in health care by race and gender.

The immune system

The immune system is the body’s surveillance system: the structures, cells and mechanisms that protect it from harmful foreign substances such as viruses and bacteria. Key players are lymphocytes, white blood cells that circulate in the body’s fluids and are central to the immune response.

The system can go wrong in two directions:

Overactive

Autoimmune disease

The immune system attacks healthy tissue: rheumatoid arthritis (joints), lupus (multiple organs, such as the heart, lungs and kidneys), Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (the thyroid gland).

Underactive

Immunosuppression

Immunosuppression is a weakening of the immune system that leaves people open to infection. HIV, which causes AIDS, destroys antibody-producing cells.

Stressors and immune function

Psychoneuroimmunology is the field that studies how psychological factors such as stress affect the immune system. A classic early study showed the mind can steer immunity: Ader and Cohen (1975) used classical conditioning (Unit 6) to suppress rats’ immune systems.

Since then, hundreds of studies have tied stressors, both brief (exams) and chronic (divorce, bereavement, caregiving, job strain), to weaker immune function. Two routes are involved: the sympathetic nervous system connects directly to immune organs such as the thymus, bone marrow, spleen and lymph nodes, and stress hormones released by the HPA axis can inhibit the production of lymphocytes.

Catching a cold. Cohen et al. (1998) paid healthy volunteers $800 to receive cold virus through nasal drops. Those who reported chronic stressors lasting at least a month, especially enduring difficulties involving work or relationships, were considerably more likely to catch a cold than those with no chronic stressors.

Other effects of stress on immunity:

  • Vaccines: people caring for a spouse with Alzheimer’s disease made a weaker antibody response to a flu vaccine than controls (Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 1996).
  • Wound healing: after small blisters were induced on their arms, more stressed participants produced less of the immune proteins needed for wound repair (Glaser et al., 1999).

Dig deeper: stress and aging, a tale of telomeres

Telomeres are protective caps at the ends of chromosomes. When telomeres become too short, they can inhibit or block cell division, which is why their shortening is linked to cellular aging. Stress appears to speed this up (Epel et al., 2004; Sapolsky, 2004).

  • Caregiving mothers: mothers of chronically ill children had shorter telomeres. Years of caregiving correlated with telomere length at r = −.40, and perceived stress at r = −.31. The most stressed mothers had telomeres like those of women 9 to 17 years older (Epel et al., 2004).
  • Early adversity: childhood exposure to violence, maltreatment, and even a mother’s stress during pregnancy are linked to telomere erosion (Shalev et al., 2013; Entringer et al., 2011; Surtees et al., 2010).

Cardiovascular disorders

The cardiovascular system is the heart and the system of blood circulation. Cardiovascular disorders are disorders of the heart and blood circulation. Heart disease covers adverse conditions of the heart’s arteries, valves or pumping ability, including heart attack and stroke; it often brings angina, chest pain or discomfort when the heart does not get enough blood. Heart disease causes about one in three U.S. deaths each year and is the leading cause of death in the developed world (CDC, 2011; Shapiro, 2005).

Hypertension, high blood pressure, is a major risk factor. It forces the heart to work harder and, untreated, can lead to heart attack, stroke, heart failure, kidney failure or blindness. It is called the silent killer because it often has no symptoms. Risk factors include aging, low income, education and employment status, poor diet, smoking, physical inactivity, alcohol, obesity and diabetes. Stressors linked to hypertension include job strain, natural disasters, marital conflict, traffic noise and, among African Americans, perceived discrimination. In the lab, stressors such as mental arithmetic, the cold pressor test (a hand in ice water), mirror tracing and public speaking all raise blood pressure.

Are you Type A or Type B?

Cardiologist Meyer Friedman noticed something odd about his waiting room: the chairs were worn only at the front edges, as if his heart patients could hardly sit still. With his colleague Ray Rosenman he went on to describe two behavior patterns (OpenStax Figure 14.18):

Type A

Driven and hostile

An excessive competitive drive, a chronic sense of time urgency, impatience, and hostility toward others, especially those who get in the way.

Type B

Relaxed

Relaxed, laid-back and easygoing.

In their first study, heart disease was over seven times more frequent among Type As than Type Bs (Friedman & Rosenman, 1959). The section’s example, Jeffrey, was intense and driven from childhood: swim team captain, honors at an Ivy League college. As an adult he works weekends, takes on extra work, never seems to have enough hours, snaps at slow coworkers and at interruptions, rages at other drivers and has strained his marriage. He had his first heart attack at 52.

Later studies failed to replicate the link between the overall Type A pattern and heart disease, so researchers turned to its components. Anger and hostility emerged as the most dangerous:

  • Suppressed hostility substantially raised heart disease risk in both men and women (Haynes et al., 1980).
  • Medical students with high anger were over six times more likely to have a heart attack by age 55 (Chang et al., 2002).
  • A review of 35 studies found anger and hostility to be serious long-term risk factors for heart disease (Chida & Steptoe, 2009).

The hostility cycle and negative affectivity

The transactional model of hostility (Vella et al., 2012; OpenStax Figure 14.19) explains how hostility keeps itself going. The section’s example, Kaitlin, is cynical and distrustful, rehearses confrontations in her head and speaks harshly. Others avoid her or snap back, which confirms her hostility and makes it worse. This cycle may set the stage for heart problems.

Negative affectivity is a tendency to experience distressing emotions such as anger, contempt, disgust, guilt, fear and nervousness. It predicts hypertension and heart disease. Among more than 3,000 people followed for 22 years, higher negative affect raised the risk of hypertension (Jonas & Lando, 2000). Among more than 10,000 London civil servants, the third with the highest scores had 32% greater risk of heart disease (Nabi et al., 2008).

Depression and the heart

The link is old: Malzberg (1937) found a death rate six times higher among institutionalized patients with melancholia (an old term for depression), and a Danish study of more than 8,000 people with bipolar disorder found nearly 50% more heart disease deaths than expected (Weeke, 1979). Today, depression is known to raise heart disease risk, and the more severe the depression, the higher the risk.

  • Depressed men were 50% and depressed women 70% more likely to die of cardiovascular causes (Ösby et al., 2001).
  • Elevated depressive symptoms carried a 64% greater risk of heart disease (Wulsin & Singal, 2003).
  • Nurses with more depressive symptoms were 49% more likely to die of heart disease over 12 years (Whang et al., 2009).
  • The American Heart Association recommends screening heart patients for depression and lists depression as a risk factor (Lichtman et al., 2008; AHA, 2014).
  • Depression in childhood predicts later obesity, smoking and physical inactivity, all risk factors for heart disease (Rottenberg et al., 2014).
  • Negative emotions together (depression, anxiety, hostility) predicted heart disease more strongly than any one alone (Boyle et al., 2006).

Asthma

Asthma is a chronic disease in which the airways become obstructed, making it difficult to exhale. During an attack the airways become inflamed and thickened, the surrounding muscles tighten and mucus builds up, causing wheezing, chest tightness, shortness of breath and coughing (OpenStax Figure 14.21).

How common: about 4,000 people a year in the United States die of asthma-related causes, and asthma contributes to about 7,000 more deaths (CDC, 2013a). It affects 18.7 million U.S. adults, is more common at lower incomes, and its rates rose 157% between 2000 and 2010 (CDC, 2013b).

Triggers include air pollution, allergens, smoke, infections, cold air and exercise. Neighborhoods with dense pollution, such as the “asthma alleys” of Long Beach, California, and the Bronx in New York, have high rates. Psychological factors are probably among the most common triggers:

  • Expectation: people with asthma can develop symptoms just by believing that an inert substance they breathe will block their airways (Sodergren & Hyland, 1999).
  • Emotional distress affects airway function; in one study 20 adults’ airway function was tied to their mood and stress (Smyth et al., 1999).
  • A case study described an 18-year-old whose attacks followed checking his ex-girlfriend’s Facebook page (D’Amato et al., 2010).
  • Conflict and loss: among more than 10,000 Finnish college students, conflicts with parents or partners raised asthma risk (Kilpeläinen et al., 2002). In 145 children, parenting difficulties in the first year of life raised the chance of asthma by 107% (Klinnert et al., 2001). Among more than 4,000 middle-aged men, ending an important partnership raised it by 124% over a decade (Loerbroks et al., 2009).

Tension headaches

A headache is pain in the head or neck. Sinus headaches are felt in the cheeks and forehead; migraines bring severe pain on one or both sides of the head, an upset stomach and sometimes visual disturbance, and are more common in women. Tension headaches, triggered by tightening or tensing of the facial and neck muscles, are the most common type, about 42% of headaches worldwide (Stovner et al., 2007). More than a third of U.S. adults have one each year, and 2–3% have chronic tension headaches.

Contributors include sleep deprivation, skipped meals, eye strain, overexertion, poor posture and stress. Stress may make people who already have lower pain thresholds even more sensitive to pain (Cathcart, Petkov, & Pritchard, 2008).

Regulation of Stress

Because stress harms health, how we deal with it matters. Coping style, a sense of control and the support of other people all shape how much damage a stressor does.

Learning objectives

  • Define coping and differentiate between problem-focused and emotion-focused coping
  • Describe the importance of perceived control in our reactions to stress
  • Explain how social support is vital in health and longevity

Coping styles

Coping is the mental and behavioral effort we use to deal with problems relating to stress, including its presumed cause and the unpleasant feelings it brings. Lazarus and Folkman (1984) described two basic kinds:

ExampleBradford, worried about a course, contacts his professor, studies two hours a day and seeks tutoringBradford instead watches a comedy, plays video games or spends hours on social media to feel better
Used more forStressors seen as controllableStressors seen as uncontrollable (Folkman & Lazarus, 1980)

Reappraisal means changing how we think about a stressor without changing its objective threat: a person sentenced to federal prison, for example, might think, “This will give me a great chance to network with others.” Most stressors are controllable to some degree, which leaves room for problem-focused coping; for stressors that truly cannot be controlled, such as a terminal illness, emotion-focused coping is the more effective choice.

Perceived control

Perceived control is our belief in our ability to influence what happens to us. People with high perceived control tend to have better physical and mental health and well-being (Diehl & Hay, 2010) and react less strongly to stressors (Neupert, Almeida, & Charles, 2007). In a diary study of 34 older widows, stress and anxiety were lower on days when the women felt more control (Ong, Bergeman, & Bisconti, 2005). As Bandura (1997) put it, how intense and lasting stress is depends largely on how much control we believe we have over life’s demands.

Dig deeper: learned helplessness

In the 1960s, Martin Seligman and colleagues placed dogs in a chamber where they received electric shocks (Seligman & Maier, 1967; OpenStax Figure 14.22). Later the dogs could escape new shocks simply by jumping over a partition.

Seligman called this learned helplessness: after experiencing events they cannot control, organisms stop trying even when control becomes possible. He proposed that something similar contributes to depression in humans. The section adds an ethics note: the model was later used, in ways Seligman never proposed, to design interrogation and torture methods after 2001; those methods are now widely considered unethical, a reminder to weigh the ethics and possible uses of research.

The reformulated model (Abramson, Seligman, & Teasdale, 1978) focuses on attributions, the way people explain bad events:

Table 14.5Attributions in the reformulated learned helplessness model, from Section 14.4
DimensionThe bad event is caused by…Depression-prone side
Internal vs. externalSomething about me, or something outside meInternal
Stable vs. unstableSomething lasting, or something temporaryStable
Global vs. specificSomething affecting many areas of life, or just this oneGlobal

People who explain bad events with internal, stable and global attributions are more likely to become depressed (Peterson & Seligman, 1984). These habits of explanation can be changed with training. The model later developed into the hopelessness theory of depression (Unit 15).

Perceived control and health

High perceived control goes with healthier behavior and a lower risk of physical illness, including heart attacks and other cardiovascular disease. Among British civil servants, workers in jobs with low control had a higher risk of heart disease (Marmot et al., 1997).

Control may also help explain why social class is linked to health. Wealthier people tend to feel more in control of their lives, and a sense of control can protect less affluent people from poorer health, depression and lower life satisfaction (Lachman & Weaver, 1998).

Social support

The need to form and keep close relationships is a basic human motive (Baumeister & Leary, 1995). Social support is the help we receive from others: advice, encouragement, emotional comfort and tangible help such as money (OpenStax Figure 14.23). Even elephants seem to sense when a group member is stressed and comfort it with a touch of the trunk or an empathetic call (Krumboltz, 2014).

  • A review of 148 studies with more than 300,000 participants found that people with strong social relationships had about a 50% greater likelihood of survival, an effect comparable to quitting smoking and larger than the risks of obesity or physical inactivity (Holt-Lunstad, Smith, & Layton, 2010).
  • Social support is linked to lower cardiovascular death, better breast cancer survival and better outcomes in HIV.
  • In a cold-virus study, the more sociable participants were, the less likely they were to become ill (Cohen et al., 2003).
  • Students who move away to college often lose their usual support and may feel anxious, depressed and lonely. Social media can help or add to loneliness; peer mentoring programs can build new networks.

How does it work? Support may boost immune function, especially under stress (Uchino et al., 2012; Baron et al., 1990), and lower blood pressure during stressful tasks, especially when a friend is present (Fontana et al., 1999; Phillips et al., 2009). It may also encourage healthier behavior.

Dig deeper: stress and discrimination

For members of marginalized groups, discrimination is a significant stressor: chronic, uncontrollable and unpredictable (Pascoe & Smart Richman, 2009). People with several marginalized identities may face compounded effects (Vines et al., 2017). Seen through the general adaptation syndrome, repeated discrimination sets off an alarm reaction, then a resistance stage of heightened reactivity, and finally exhaustion. It is linked to depression, cardiovascular disease and cancer.

Legal protections help, but much discrimination is subtle and hard to prosecute. Social support and healthy coping can buffer its effects, as in a study of African American mothers in Detroit (Ajrouch et al., 2010), but they do not erase them.

Stress reduction techniques

Beyond coping and support, several techniques reduce stress directly (OpenStax Figure 14.24):

Exercise

Both aerobic and anaerobic exercise benefit physical and mental health. Physically fit people resist stress better and recover faster (Cotton, 1990); among more than 500 Swiss police and emergency workers, higher fitness went with less stress (Gerber et al., 2010). The research also shows:

  • rats that exercised for six weeks had a less responsive HPA axis to mild stressors (Campeau et al., 2010);
  • in highly stressed people, exercise prevented telomere shortening (Puterman et al., 2010);
  • in later adulthood, exercise reduced the harm of stress to the hippocampus and memory (Head, Singh, & Bugg, 2012);
  • among cancer survivors, exercise reduced anxiety (Speck et al., 2010) and depressive symptoms (Craft et al., 2012).

The relaxation response

The relaxation response technique, developed by Herbert Benson in the 1970s, combines relaxation with transcendental meditation. It reduces sympathetic arousal and has been used to treat high blood pressure (Benson & Proctor, 1994).

Biofeedback

Biofeedback, developed by Gary Schwartz at Harvard in the early 1970s, uses electronic equipment to measure involuntary neuromuscular and autonomic activity, such as facial muscle movement, brain activity and skin temperature, and to show it as visual or auditory feedback. Watching the feedback, people learn to gain some voluntary control over these processes. It has been used for tension headaches, high blood pressure, asthma and phobias (Stein, 2001).

Stress management for students

Stress is close to universal among college students. A Penn State McNair Scholars study (Fall, 2021) reported that more than half of students felt overwhelming anxiety in 2019, and the share rose the following year. Chronic stress hurts concentration, memory and problem solving, and with them grades and relationships. About one in five U.S. undergraduates is a parent (ACE, 2023), and more than 43% of student parents report nearly constant stress (Aspen Institute, 2021).

Emotional and lifestyle

Look after mind and body

  • Regular exercise
  • Consistent sleep
  • A balanced diet
  • Social connection
  • Campus counseling, often free or low cost (Pérez-Jorge et al., 2025)
Academic and organizational

Make the work manageable

  • Break assignments into smaller parts
  • Use a planner or digital calendar
  • Set realistic daily goals
  • Use office hours and study groups
  • Avoid overcommitting (Florida Atlantic University, 2025)

Stress management is a skill that can be learned; these strategies combine problem-focused coping, perceived control and social support from earlier in the section.

The Pursuit of Happiness

Psychology has long studied what goes wrong. This section turns to what goes right: what happiness is, what predicts it, and how positive emotions, optimism and flow relate to health.

What is happiness?

Happiness means different things to different people: for some it is virtue and spirituality, for others contentment, for others engaging and rewarding activity (Martin, 2012). Seligman (2002) described three elements (Seligman et al., 2005):

A practical definition: happiness is an enduring state of mind made up of joy, contentment and other positive emotions, plus a sense that one’s life has meaning and value (Lyubomirsky, 2001). Because it is enduring, happiness is closer to subjective well-being than to a passing good mood.

How happy are people?

Surveys from 2010 to 2012 asked people in more than 150 countries to rate their lives from 0 to 10 (Helliwell et al., 2013).

  • The five happiest countries were Denmark, Norway, Switzerland, the Netherlands and Sweden; the United States ranked 17th (OpenStax Figure 14.26).
  • In a Gallup survey of more than 1,000 U.S. adults, 52% said they were “very happy” and more than 80% were “very satisfied” with their lives (Carroll, 2007).
  • A later poll found only 42% “very happy,” with the largest drops among people of color, people without a college degree, and Democrats and independents (McCarthy, 2020); economic conditions may play a part.

What goes with happiness

Most of this research is correlational, so it shows what goes with happiness, not what causes it.

Table 14.6Factors and happiness, from Section 14.5

Life events and adaptation

We are poor at affective forecasting: predicting how strongly, and for how long, future events will make us feel (Wilson & Gilbert, 2003). Newlyweds, for example, expected their marital satisfaction to stay the same or rise, but on average it fell over the next four years (Lavner et al., 2013).

One reason is adaptation: we feel the full impact of an event at first, then get used to it and drift back toward our usual baseline of happiness (Brickman & Campbell, 1971; Helson, 1964). Lottery winners, and Chicago Cubs fans after their 2016 World Series win (their first since 1908), feel elated, but the elation fades (OpenStax Figure 14.27).

Some changes last, though. People adapt to marriage, but often not fully to unemployment or severe disability (Diener, 2012). In a German sample of more than 3,000 people followed over time, about a quarter showed significant changes in happiness and 9% showed major changes (Fujita & Diener, 2005).

Increasing happiness

Well-being interventions may produce lasting gains in happiness for individuals, organizations and societies (Diener et al., 2006). Simple exercises work: writing down three good things that happened each day raised happiness for more than six months (Seligman et al., 2005).

At the national level, happiness is strongly related to six variables (Helliwell et al., 2013):

  • Per capita GDP
  • Social support
  • Freedom to make life choices
  • Healthy life expectancy
  • Freedom from perceived corruption
  • Generosity

Policy questions such as poverty, taxation, health care, housing, clean air and water, and income inequality might be better addressed with citizens’ happiness in mind.

Positive psychology

In 1998, Martin Seligman, then president of the APA, urged psychologists to study human strengths and well-being, not only illness and pathology. This helped launch positive psychology: the scientific study of the conditions and traits that let people lead happy, fulfilling lives (Compton, 2005).

Subjective level

Positive experiences

Well-being, hope, optimism, happiness.

Individual level

Positive traits

Capacity for love, courage, perseverance, forgiveness, wisdom (Seligman & Csikszentmihalyi, 2000).

Topics include altruism, creativity, forgiveness, positive emotions, immune functioning, savoring and virtue; one extension, positive peace psychology, applies the approach to global conflict (Cohrs et al., 2013).

Dig deeper: the Center for Investigating Healthy Minds

Founded in 2008 at the Waisman Center of the University of Wisconsin–Madison under neuroscientist Richard J. Davidson, the center studies kindness, forgiveness, compassion and mindfulness: kindness curricula in schools, the neural basis of prosocial behavior, Tai Chi, digital games for children, and yoga and breathing for PTSD. It grew out of a challenge from the 14th Dalai Lama to apply scientific rigor to positive qualities of mind (Center for Investigating Healthy Minds, 2013).

Positive affect and optimism

Positive psychological qualities, such as meaning, autonomy, positive emotion and life satisfaction, are linked to better health, especially cardiovascular health, both through biology and through behaviors such as diet, activity and sleep (Boehm & Kubzansky, 2012).

A feeling state

Positive affect

Positive affect is a state or trait of pleasurable engagement with the environment: happiness, joy, enthusiasm, alertness, excitement (Watson et al., 1988).

An expectation

Optimism

Optimism is a general tendency to expect positive outcomes, and to see stressors as temporary and external (Chang, 2001; Peterson & Steen, 2002).

  • Positive affect, regardless of age, gender and income, goes with social connectedness, support, coping, lower depression and longer life (Steptoe et al., 2008). In a 10-year study in Nova Scotia, each one-point rise on a five-point positive affect scale meant 22% lower heart disease (Davidson et al., 2010).
  • Optimism is linked to longer life, healthier behavior, fewer complications after surgery, better immune function in prostate cancer patients and better adherence to treatment (Rasmussen & Wallio, 2008). Optimists report fewer symptoms and less pain and are less often rehospitalized after heart surgery (Rasmussen et al., 2009).

The two are related but distinct (Pressman & Cohen, 2005): positive affect is about feeling, optimism about expecting.

Flow

Flow is a state of deep absorption in an activity so engaging that it is rewarding in itself (Csikszentmihalyi, 1997). The name came from people’s own descriptions of the experience. In flow, people lose track of time, concentrate without effort and feel in control. It happens in challenging activities that match one’s skills, and more often at work, in hobbies and in study than in passive activities such as eating. About 20% of Americans and Europeans report regular flow experiences (Csikszentmihalyi, 1997), and Csikszentmihalyi (1999) argued that creating conditions for flow, in schools and workplaces, should be a top social and political priority.
