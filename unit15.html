Unit 15 · Overview

Psychological Disorders

What counts as a psychological disorder, how disorders are diagnosed and explained, and the main DSM-5 groups: anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, trauma-related, mood, psychotic, dissociative, neurodevelopmental and personality disorders.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening case

On September 16, 2013, a gunman killed 12 people at the Washington Navy Yard in Washington, DC (OpenStax Figure 15.1 shows the memorial wreath). The shooter, 34-year-old Aaron Alexis, had a troubled history: he believed he was being controlled by radio waves, and he had called police to report hearing voices and being watched. His actions cannot be excused, but he appears to have had a mental illness, and the chapter suggests the tragedy might have been prevented had he received the help he needed.

Disorder is not the same as danger

Stuart (2003) concluded that mental disorders are neither necessary nor sufficient causes of violence. The main determinants of violence are socio-demographic and socio-economic: being young, male and of lower socioeconomic status. Section 15.8 adds that people with severe mental illness are far more likely to be victims of crime than perpetrators.

Unit 15 · Section 15.1

What Are Psychological Disorders?

Drawing the line between unusual and disordered is harder than it looks. No single criterion does it; the definitions used today combine several.

Learning objectives

  • Understand the problems inherent in defining the concept of psychological disorder
  • Describe what is meant by harmful dysfunction
  • Identify the formal criteria that thoughts, feelings and behaviors must meet to be considered abnormal and, thus, symptomatic of a psychological disorder
Read Section 15.1 on OpenStax ↗

Defining a psychological disorder

A psychological disorder is a condition characterized by abnormal thoughts, feelings and behaviors. The American Psychiatric Association (2013) describes a mental disorder as “a syndrome characterized by clinically significant disturbance” that is usually linked to significant distress in social, occupational or other important activities. Psychopathology is the study of psychological disorders: their symptoms, etiology (causes) and treatment. The term can also refer to the manifestation of a disorder.

Some cases are clear-cut: someone who washes their hands 40 times a day, or who claims to hear the voices of demons. Others are clearly normal: feeling nervous around an attractive person, or homesick during a first semester away. Many cases fall in between.

Two points run through the whole chapter. Mental illness is not a matter of choice and should not be stigmatized, and anyone with persistent symptoms should consult a qualified professional.

Atypical is not enough

A simple approach would label behavior disordered if it is atypical (different from what is normal), distressful, dysfunctional or dangerous. Feeling dejected after a romantic rejection is normal; severe depression with suicidal thoughts may signal a disorder. But being unusual alone does not make something a disorder. About 4% of people in the United States have red hair, and about 13% in Scotland (DNA Project Aims, 2012; OpenStax Figure 15.2 shows three natural redheads). Red hair is atypical, not disordered.

Context matters too. A person in a heavy winter coat yelling at strangers on a subway platform in July is behaving atypically and perhaps disturbingly, but whether that suggests a disorder depends on cultural expectations.

Cultural expectations

Each culture has norms for how to behave, and violating them is often seen as abnormal. But norms differ between cultures and change over time:

  • Smiles: in the U.S., a stranger’s smile is usually returned; people in Japan are generally unresponsive to strangers’ smiles (Patterson et al., 2007).
  • Eye contact (OpenStax Figure 15.3): in the U.S. and Europe it signals honesty and interest; in most Latin American, Asian and African cultures, direct eye contact can be rude or aggressive (Pazain, 2010).
  • Hallucinations are readily labeled disordered in Western societies, but in other cultures visions about future events may be considered normal and even valued (Bourguignon, 1970).
  • Change over time: 1980s fashions (headbands, legwarmers, big hair) would look odd today.

Dig deeper: the myth of mental illness

In the 1950s and 1960s, critics challenged the very concept of mental illness. The strongest was psychiatrist Thomas Szasz (1960), who argued that mental illness is a myth invented by society to stigmatize people who violate norms, and that their symptoms are better called “problems in living.” In The Myth of Mental Illness (1961), he held that only bodily abnormalities count as genuine illness (Oliver, 2006). The real suffering of people with disorders makes this view hard to sustain, but Szasz had lasting influence: the language of mental “problems,” his challenge to treating same-sex attraction as an illness (Szasz, 1965), and legal changes protecting the rights of people in psychiatric institutions (Buchanan-Barker & Barker, 2009).

Harmful dysfunction

There is no universally agreed definition of a psychological disorder (Zachar & Kendler, 2007). One influential definition is Wakefield’s (1992) harmful dysfunction, which has two parts:

Part 1 · Dysfunction

An internal mechanism fails

An evolved mechanism, such as learning, fails to perform the function it was designed to perform.

Part 2 · Harm

The failure causes harm

The failure causes significant harm to the person, judged by the standards of their culture.

Example: Janet has an extreme fear of spiders. Her fear reflects faulty learning (a dysfunction), and it has made her avoid many places and even quit her job (harm). Both parts are met. The model fits neurobiological views of disorder (Fabrega, 2007).

The APA’s four criteria

The American Psychiatric Association (2013) definition combines these ideas. A disorder involves:

  1. Significant disturbances in thoughts, feelings and behaviors: unusual in a negative, self-defeating way and often troubling to others.
  2. Disturbances that reflect a biological, psychological or developmental dysfunction (hallucinations in schizophrenia may reflect brain abnormalities).
  3. Disturbances that cause significant distress or impairment in daily life.
  4. Disturbances that are not expected or culturally approved responses to events (intense grief after a family member’s death is expected).

Some argue that no single criterion can separate disorder from nondisorder (Lilienfeld & Marino, 1999). Experiences such as anxiety or sadness become disordered only when they are highly disturbing, point to a dysfunction and cause significant impairment.

Diagnosing and Classifying Psychological Disorders

A shared classification system gives clinicians a common language, guides treatment and makes research possible. The two main systems are the DSM and the ICD.

Why classify?

Diagnosis is determining which disorder a set of symptoms may indicate. An accurate diagnosis lets mental health professionals use a common language with each other and with patients and the public, and it points to effective treatment. Classification systems make that possible.

The DSM-5

Most U.S. mental health professionals use the DSM-5, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (American Psychiatric Association, 2013): the APA’s authoritative index of disorders and their criteria. A text revision, the DSM-5-TR, followed in 2022. For each disorder it lists:

  • Diagnostic features
  • Diagnostic criteria
  • Prevalence
  • Risk factors

Prevalence is the percentage of a population with a disorder. The National Comorbidity Survey (2007), a national sample of 9,282 U.S. residents, estimated lifetime prevalence using DSM-IV categories (alcohol abuse now falls under the broader alcohol use disorder).

Comorbidity

Comorbidity is the co-occurrence of two disorders in the same person. It is common:

  • 41% of people with obsessive-compulsive disorder also meet criteria for major depressive disorder.
  • About 6 in 10 people with a substance use disorder also have another mental illness (NIDA, 2007, 2018). Because substances can mimic or mask symptoms, clinicians look at behavior after drug use has stopped and withdrawal has passed.
  • More than half of people with a primary depressive disorder have an anxiety disorder, and the reverse is also true (Al-Asadi, Klein, & Meyer, 2015).

Criticisms of the DSM-5

  • Overpathologizing: new diagnoses may turn ordinary human problems into disorders. Nearly half of Americans are estimated to meet criteria for a DSM disorder at some point in their lives (Kessler et al., 2012).
  • Loosened criteria: Frances (2012) warned of turning “diagnostic inflation” into “diagnostic hyperinflation.”
  • The bereavement exclusion was removed: DSM-IV excluded depressive symptoms explained by normal grief, but under DSM-5, grief after a loss can potentially count as major depressive disorder.

The ICD

The ICD (International Classification of Diseases) is the World Health Organization’s authoritative index of mental and physical diseases. It was developed in Europe shortly after World War II and is also used to monitor health and disease internationally. Its categories and criteria are similar to the DSM’s, with some differences.

A compassionate view

Psychological disorders represent extremes of experiences that everyone has. Occasional sadness or anxiety is not a disorder unless it becomes extreme and disruptive. People with disorders are more than their diagnoses and deserve compassion, understanding and dignity.

Perspectives on Psychological Disorders

Explanations have moved from spirits and witchcraft to genes and brain chemistry, and now to models in which biology and experience work together.

Supernatural perspectives

For centuries, disorders were attributed to supernatural forces, those beyond scientific understanding. Afflicted people were thought to practice black magic or to be possessed by spirits (Maher & Maher, 1985). Hieronymus Bosch’s 15th-century painting The Extraction of the Stone of Madness (OpenStax Figure 15.6) shows a supposed cure: removing a “stone of madness” from the patient’s head.

  • Convents in 16th- and 17th-century Europe reported hundreds of nuns in frenzied states: foaming at the mouth, screaming, convulsing, and confessing carnal relations with devils or Christ. Today this would suggest serious mental illness; then it was explained as possession (Waller, 2009a).
  • New England, late 17th century: fits by young girls helped set off the witch panic (Demos, 1983).
  • Today: such beliefs persist in some cultures, for example in parts of modern-day Nigeria (Aghukwa, 2012).

Dig deeper: dancing mania

Between the 11th and 17th centuries, outbreaks of compulsive dancing, sometimes involving thousands of people, swept Western Europe (OpenStax Figure 15.7). Accounts describe people dancing on bruised, bloody feet for days or weeks, screaming of terrible visions and begging priests and monks to save their souls (Waller, 2009b). Spider venom and ergot poisoning have been proposed, but the cause is unknown. Historian John Waller argues that three factors combined: psychological distress worsened by famine, plague and floods; social contagion; and belief in supernatural forces. People are more likely to enter a trance when they expect it, and entranced people act out their culture’s spiritual beliefs.

The biological perspective

The biological perspective, widely accepted in recent decades (Wyatt & Midkiff, 2006), links disorders to genetic factors, chemical imbalances and brain abnormalities:

  • Genes: most disorders have a genetic component, and some are largely genetic. Research now looks for specific genes and mutations.
  • Brain imaging shows how abnormalities in brain structure and function may be involved in many disorders.
  • Neurotransmitters and hormones: advances have revealed possible connections to disorders.

Schizophrenia shows the genetic pattern clearly: the closer your genetic relationship to someone with the disorder, the higher your own risk.

The diathesis-stress model

Most disorders arise from a combination of biological and psychosocial factors, not a single cause. The diathesis-stress model (Zuckerman, 1999) puts it this way:

Diathesis

A predisposition

An underlying vulnerability. It can be biological (genes) or psychological, such as a pessimistic, self-defeating way of thinking about life events.

Stress

Adverse experiences

Environmental or psychological events such as childhood maltreatment, negative life events or trauma.

Two assumptions follow. Both are necessary for a disorder to develop, and the amount of stress needed is inversely proportional to the diathesis: the stronger the predisposition, the less stress it takes.

Anxiety Disorders

Some anxiety is useful: it keeps us careful. An anxiety disorder is excessive, persistent fear and anxiety, and the related changes in behavior, out of proportion to any real threat.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish normal anxiety from pathological anxiety
  • List and describe the major anxiety disorders, including their main features and prevalence
  • Describe basic psychological and biological factors that are suspected to be important in the etiology of anxiety disorders
Read Section 15.4 on OpenStax ↗

Anxiety versus fear

Future-oriented

Anxiety

Apprehension and worry about a possible threat in the future.

Present-oriented

Fear

An immediate alarm reaction to an imminent threat.

Anxiety becomes a disorder when it is excessive, persistent, out of proportion to the threat and disruptive to daily life. Anxiety disorders are the most frequently occurring class of mental disorders (Kessler, Ruscio, Shear, & Wittchen, 2009) and often co-occur with each other and with other disorders.

  • About 25–30% of the U.S. population meets criteria for at least one anxiety disorder in their lifetime (Kessler et al., 2005).
  • They are more common in women: in a 12-month period, about 23% of women and 14% of men have one (National Comorbidity Survey, 2007).

Specific phobia

A specific phobia is excessive, persistent fear of a particular object or situation. Exposure brings intense anxiety, so the person goes to great lengths to avoid it. About 12.5% of Americans meet criteria at some point in their lives (Kessler et al., 2005).

Agoraphobia is listed separately in the DSM-5: intense fear and avoidance of situations where escape, or help, might be hard to get if panic symptoms struck, such as public transportation, open spaces (parking lots), enclosed spaces (stores), crowds, or being outside the home alone. About 1.4% of Americans experience it in their lifetime (Kessler et al., 2005).

How phobias are learned

Rachman (1977) proposed three learning pathways to a phobia:

Pathway 1

Classical conditioning

A neutral stimulus is paired with something frightening. A child bitten by a dog comes to fear dogs.

Pathway 2

Vicarious learning

Watching others react fearfully (modeling). A child copies a cousin’s fear of spiders. Seen in humans and nonhuman primates (Olsson & Phelps, 2007).

Pathway 3

Verbal information

Being told something is dangerous. A child told that snakes are dangerous fears snakes.

Why snakes and not motorcycles? Phobias tend to involve things that are not very dangerous today, like animals and heights, rather than modern dangers like weapons and motorcycles. Seligman’s (1971) preparedness theory proposes that evolution has primed us to learn fears of threats our ancestors faced. Fears do form more readily to snakes and spiders than to flowers and berries (Öhman & Mineka, 2001).

Monkey studies support both observational and prepared learning. Lab-reared monkeys quickly acquired a fear of snakes after watching wild-reared monkeys react fearfully to them (Mineka & Cook, 1993). Watching edited videos of fearful models, observer monkeys learned to fear fear-relevant objects (toy snakes, a toy crocodile) but not fear-irrelevant ones (flowers, a toy rabbit) (Cook & Mineka, 1989).

Social anxiety disorder

Social anxiety disorder is extreme, persistent fear and avoidance of social situations in which a person might be evaluated negatively by others: public speaking, conversations, meeting strangers, eating in restaurants, even using public restrooms. A little over 12% of Americans meet criteria in their lifetime (Kessler et al., 2005). It is linked to lower education and earnings (Katzelnick et al., 2001), poorer work performance and more unemployment (Moitra, Beard, Weisberg, & Keller, 2011), and dissatisfaction with family, friends, leisure and income (Stein & Kean, 2000).

Safety behaviors

A safety behavior is a mental or behavioral act meant to reduce anxiety in social situations by lowering the chance of a bad outcome. Rassaby, Smith and Taylor (2022) sort them into two kinds:

Avoidance

Hiding

Avoiding eye contact, talking about oneself as little as possible (Alden & Bieling, 1998), sitting at the back, wearing neutral clothing (Marker, 2013).

Impression management

Controlling how one comes across

Rehearsing sentences in one’s mind (Alden & Bieling, 1998), taking on setup roles, asking lots of questions (Marker, 2013).

Safety behaviors backfire: they keep the person from learning that the feared outcome would not have happened, so the negative beliefs are never disconfirmed. Some people self-medicate with alcohol (Battista & Kocovski, 2010), and social anxiety is highly comorbid with alcohol use disorder (Morris, Stewart, & Ham, 2005).

Possible causes

  • Negative early experiences: they can create negative self-images that are reactivated later in social situations (Hackmann, Clark, & McManus, 2000). 92% of adults with social anxiety reported severe teasing in childhood, versus 35% of adults with panic disorder (McCabe et al., 2003).
  • Behavioral inhibition: an inherited tendency to be fearful and restrained with unfamiliar people and situations (Kagan, Reznick, & Snidman, 1988), visible in cautious, shy toddlers (Fox et al., 2005). It is associated with a more than sevenfold increase in risk (Clauss & Blackford, 2012).

Panic disorder

A panic attack is a sudden episode of extreme fear or discomfort that peaks within 10 minutes, with physical and psychological symptoms (APA, 2013).

Isolated panic attacks are common: about 23% of Americans have had one without having panic disorder (Kessler et al., 2006). Panic disorder, sometimes called “fear of fear” (Goldstein & Chambless, 1978), requires:

  • recurrent, unexpected panic attacks, and
  • at least one month of persistent worry about another attack or its consequences, or self-defeating changes in behavior to avoid attacks;
  • attacks not explained by drugs, a medical condition or another disorder.

About 4.7% of Americans develop panic disorder in their lifetime (Kessler et al., 2005). It often comes with agoraphobia and with depression.

Causes of panic disorder

Genetics

Runs in families

Children of parents with the disorder are at higher risk (Biederman et al., 2001); heritability is about 43% (Hettema, Neale, & Kendler, 2001). The specific genes are not well understood.

Neurobiology

The locus coeruleus

The locus coeruleus, a brainstem area that is the brain’s main source of norepinephrine and part of the fight-or-flight response. Stimulating it caused panic-like symptoms in nonhuman primates (Charney et al., 1990), suggesting abnormal norepinephrine activity (Bremner, Krystal, Southwick, & Charney, 1996).

Conditioning

Body cues trigger panic

Attacks may become classically conditioned responses to subtle bodily sensations that resemble fear, such as the breathlessness of a child with asthma (Bouton, Mineka, & Barlow, 2001). Panic disorder is nearly three times as common in people with asthma (Weiser, 2007).

Cognition

Catastrophic thinking

People misinterpret ordinary bodily sensations as catastrophes (Clark, 1996). Reducing these catastrophic thoughts works as well as medication (Good & Hinton, 2009).

Generalized anxiety disorder

The section opens with Alex, who worries constantly: that his children will drown at the beach, that a short circuit will start a house fire, that his husband will lose his job, that his daughter’s minor staph infection will become life-threatening. Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) is a relatively continuous state of excessive, uncontrollable and pointless worry, usually about routine matters rather than any specific event. Freud called this diffuse worry “free-floating anxiety.” (OpenStax Figure 15.10: worry is GAD’s defining feature.)

Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders

Obsessive-compulsive and related disorders are a group of overlapping disorders involving intrusive, unpleasant thoughts and/or repetitive behaviors. Everyone has the odd unwanted thought or habit; here they are intense and frequent enough to disrupt daily life.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the main features and prevalence of obsessive-compulsive disorder, body dysmorphic disorder and hoarding disorder
  • Understand some of the factors in the development of obsessive-compulsive disorder
Read Section 15.5 on OpenStax ↗

Obsessive-compulsive disorder

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) involves unwanted, intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and/or repetitive behaviors or mental acts (compulsions) (APA, 2013).

Thoughts

Obsessions

Persistent, unintentional and unwanted thoughts, images, fears, sensations or urges that cause distress. More than everyday worries. Common themes: germs and contamination, doubt (did I turn off the faucet?), order and symmetry, perfectionism, overwhelming responsibility, moral concerns. Themes can overlap (Abramowitz & Siqueland, 2013). The person usually knows they are irrational but cannot ignore them.

Actions

Compulsions

Repetitive, ritualistic acts done to reduce the distress of an obsession or to prevent a feared event: handwashing, cleaning, checking (OpenStax Figure 15.11), ordering, or mental acts such as counting, praying or reciting. They are not done for pleasure and are not realistically connected to the feared event.

Ordinary cleaning after contact with someone who has the flu is not OCD. The text also warns that casually calling a habit an “obsession” or “OCD” can trivialize the real disorder.

About 2.3% of the U.S. population will have OCD in their lifetime (Ruscio, Stein, Chiu, & Kessler, 2010). Untreated, it tends to be chronic, with lifelong interpersonal and psychological problems (Norberg, Calamari, Cohen, & Riemann, 2008).

Body dysmorphic disorder

Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) is preoccupation with a perceived flaw in one’s physical appearance that is nonexistent or barely noticeable to others (APA, 2013). The focus is usually the skin, face or hair, but can be any part of the body. People may believe they are unattractive, ugly or deformed, and they check mirrors repeatedly, try to hide the flaw, compare themselves with others and, in extreme cases, seek cosmetic surgery (Phillips, 2005). About 2.4% of U.S. adults meet criteria, slightly more women than men (APA, 2013).

Hoarding disorder

Hoarding disorder is persistent difficulty discarding possessions, regardless of their value, leading to excessive clutter (OpenStax Figure 15.12). The clutter can become so severe that the person cannot use the kitchen or sleep in their bed. People keep things because they might be useful later or because of sentimental attachment. Once considered a symptom of OCD, hoarding is now treated as a separate disorder (Mataix-Cols et al., 2010). It is not diagnosed if caused by a medical condition or by another disorder such as schizophrenia (APA, 2013).

Causes of OCD

Genetics

OCD has a moderate genetic component. First-degree relatives of people with OCD are about five times more likely to develop it (Nestadt et al., 2000), and twin concordance is much higher for identical twins (Bolton, Rijsdijk, O’Connor, Perrin, & Eley, 2007).

About two dozen candidate genes have been implicated, ones that regulate serotonin, dopamine and glutamate (Pauls, 2010). Many of these studies used small samples and have not been replicated.

The brain

The orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), a frontal-lobe area involved in learning and decision making (Rushworth et al., 2011), is thought to be critical in OCD (Kopell & Greenberg, 2008). It belongs to an OCD circuit of interconnected regions that set the emotional value of stimuli and select responses (Graybiel & Rauch, 2000).

  • People with OCD show high OFC activity when viewing OCD-provoking images, such as a toilet or crooked pictures (Simon et al., 2010); other circuit regions also become overactive (Rotge et al., 2008).
  • Abnormalities in the circuit may produce the symptoms (Saxena, Bota, & Brody, 2001), and the OFC is more strongly connected to the rest of the circuit in people with OCD (Beucke et al., 2013).

Limits: imaging cannot yet explain why the content of obsessions and compulsions differs from person to person, and a correlation between brain abnormalities and symptoms does not show that one causes the other (Abramowitz & Siqueland, 2013).

Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders

Extremely stressful events, such as combat, abuse, disasters and crime, raise the risk of disorder, and so can major losses such as a death, divorce or job loss.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the nature and symptoms of posttraumatic stress disorder
  • Identify the risk factors associated with this disorder
  • Understand the role of learning and cognitive factors in its development
  • Understand recent DSM additions such as prolonged grief disorder, its nature and its impact
Read Section 15.6 on OpenStax ↗

What trauma is

Trauma is the lasting negative effect of an event that made a person feel threatened, in danger or at risk of loss. It has three parts: the event, how the person experiences it, and how it affects their life afterward (Patterson & Troy, 2022).

History: for much of the 20th century, these symptoms were called shell shock or combat neurosis, because they were seen in soldiers. By the late 1970s it was clear that women who had experienced sexual trauma (rape, domestic battery, incest) showed the same symptoms (Herman, 1997). The term posttraumatic stress disorder was adopted because anyone who experiences psychological trauma can develop them.

Posttraumatic stress disorder

Earlier editions of the DSM classed PTSD as an anxiety disorder; the DSM-5 moved it to the new group of trauma- and stressor-related disorders. Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) follows exposure to, witnessing, or learning details of (as first responders do) an event involving “actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence” (APA, 2013, p. 271): combat, physical attack, sexual assault, natural disasters, terrorist attacks or car accidents. PTSD is the only DSM disorder whose cause is specified in its definition.

Intrusion

  • Intrusive, distressing memories
  • Flashbacks: reliving the event as if it were happening now, for seconds to days

Avoidance

How common is PTSD?

Prevalence is hard to pin down: many survivors never see a professional, and no single screening instrument is used everywhere, so researchers study populations and extrapolate. Rates are higher after mass trauma and in jobs with duty-related trauma such as police, firefighting and emergency medical services (APA, 2013).

Risk factors for PTSD

Not everyone exposed to trauma develops PTSD. Brewin, Andrews and Valentine (2000) identified general predictors: experiencing trauma, greater trauma severity, lack of immediate social support and more life stress afterward. Other risk factors include female gender, low socioeconomic status, low intelligence, a personal or family history of mental disorders, and childhood adversity.

  • Type of trauma: harm caused by other people (combat, rape, sexual molestation) carries more risk than other traumas such as natural disasters (Kessler, Sonnega, Bromet, Hughes, & Nelson, 1995).
  • Gender differences in exposure: women are more likely to experience sexual trauma, childhood neglect and childhood physical abuse; men, natural disasters, life-threatening accidents and physical violence. Among adolescents, boys more often experience accidents, assault and witnessing death or injury; girls, rape or sexual assault, intimate partner violence and the unexpected death or injury of a loved one.
  • Race and exposure to violence: assaultive violence and witnessing trauma to others are more common among non-White than White people, and African American males are more likely than males of other races to be exposed to and victimized by violence (Kilpatrick, Badour, & Resnick, 2017).
  • Personality: neuroticism and somatization (experiencing physical symptoms under stress) raise risk (Bramsen, Dirkzwager, & van der Ploeg, 2000).
  • Genes: people with childhood or adult trauma who carry one or two short versions of a serotonin-regulating gene are at higher risk (Xie et al., 2009): a diathesis-stress pattern.

Learning and cognitive models

Classical conditioning

Reminders become cues

The trauma is an unconditioned stimulus that produces extreme fear (UCR). Cues present at the time (thoughts, feelings, sounds, places) become conditioned stimuli that trigger similar fear (Nader, 2001). A plane-crash survivor becomes hypervigilant whenever planes fly overhead. People with PTSD acquire conditioned fear more easily and extinguish it more slowly (Orr et al., 2000), and differences in conditionability may explain differences in symptoms (Pitman, 1988).

Cognitive model

Memory and appraisal

Ehlers and Clark (2000) name two processes that keep PTSD going: disturbed memory for the event (poorly encoded, fragmented, lacking context, so fragments intrude when cued) and negative appraisals of the trauma (“I deserved it because I’m stupid”). Appraisals lead to dysfunctional strategies, such as avoiding social situations with men after a rape, that prevent the memory from changing and the appraisals from being challenged.

Prolonged grief disorder

Some pain and lingering memories after a loss are normal and healthy. Prolonged grief disorder (PGD), new in the DSM-5-TR, is intense yearning for a person who has died, with intense sorrow and preoccupation with thoughts or memories of them, nearly every day:

Adults

At least 12 months

Intense, debilitating grief nearly every day for at least a year after the death.

Children and adolescents

At least 6 months

They may be preoccupied with the circumstances of the death rather than with the loss itself.

PGD overlaps with depression and PTSD but is distinct, causing significant suffering and lasting impairment (Golden & Dalgleish, 2010; Spuij et al., 2012). It is linked to higher risk of serious illness, including cardiovascular disease and weakened immunity (Prigerson et al., 1997). Studies of pet loss find a small but significant group with PGD-like impairment, and some researchers argue the definition should extend beyond the loss of a person (Lee, 2019).

Mood disorders

Mood disorders (OpenStax Figure 15.15) involve severe disturbances in mood and emotion. The two poles are:

Low pole

Depression

A varied mood state whose core is intense, persistent sadness, ranging in severity.

High pole

Mania

A state of extreme elation and agitation: talkativeness, recklessness, trying to do many things at once.

The DSM-5 has two broad categories: depressive disorders, in which depression is the defining feature, and bipolar and related disorders, in which mania is. DSM-5 specifiers label patterns within a diagnosis; they are not separate disorders.

Major depressive disorder

Major depressive disorder (MDD), or unipolar depression, is the best-known depressive disorder. Its two core symptoms are depressed mood “most of the day, nearly every day” (feeling sad, empty, hopeless or tearful) and loss of interest or pleasure in usual activities: an avid tennis player stops playing. Diagnosis requires at least two weeks of one or both core symptoms, plus several of these:

  • Significant weight loss or gain
  • Insomnia or hypersomnia
  • Psychomotor agitation (pacing, hand-wringing) or retardation (slowed speech and movement)
  • Fatigue
  • Worthlessness or guilt
  • Trouble concentrating or deciding
  • Suicidal ideation

Course

MDD is episodic: symptoms peak and then gradually ease. About 70% of people recover within a year, but about 12% are still seriously impaired after five years (Boland & Keller, 2009), and many who recover keep minor, fluctuating symptoms (Judd, 2012). Each episode raises the chance of another (Rothschild, 1999):

Consequences: unemployment, abandoned careers, lost wages, sometimes hospitalization. Most people with depression report discrimination, and many hold back from close relationships, job applications or education because of it (Lasalvia et al., 2013). Depression is also a risk factor for heart disease in healthy people and for worse outcomes in people who already have it (Whooley, 2006).

Prevalence and risk factors

Depression has been called the “common cold” of psychiatric disorders. Each year 6.6% of U.S. adults have MDD; 16.9% will have it in their lifetime (Kessler & Wang, 2009). Lifetime rates are about 20% for women and 13% for men (National Comorbidity Survey, 2007), a gap not explained by women reporting symptoms or seeking help more often, which points to biological and gender-linked environmental factors (Kessler, 2003).

  • Where: highest in North and South America, Europe and Australia; considerably lower in Asian countries (Hasin, Fenton, & Weissman, 2011).
  • When: higher in younger age cohorts, perhaps because younger people are more willing to admit depression (Kessler & Wang, 2009).
  • Risk factors: unemployment (including homemakers), income under $20,000 a year, living in a city, and being separated, divorced or widowed (Hasin et al., 2011).
  • Comorbidity: especially anxiety and substance use disorders (Kessler & Wang, 2009).

Subtypes and persistent depressive disorder

Specifier

Seasonal pattern

Symptoms occur only at a certain time of year, for example fall and winter (“winter blues”).

Specifier

Onset during pregnancy or within four weeks of birth (postpartum depression). Anxiety, panic, guilt, agitation, weepiness, reluctance to care for the baby (Fields, 2010).

Separate disorder

Persistent depressive disorder

Formerly dysthymia: depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two years, plus at least two other MDD symptoms. Full major depressive episodes can occur within it (APA, 2013).

Peripartum depression in numbers: most mothers with it do not harm their children, but most find caregiving difficult (Fields, 2010). The rare extreme case of Andrea Yates, who drowned her five children while suffering from severe peripartum-onset depression and other illness, drew national attention (Roche, 2002). In a screen of 10,000 women after childbirth, 14% screened positive for depression, and nearly 20% of them reported thoughts of harming themselves (Wisner et al., 2013).

Bipolar disorder

Bipolar disorder (once called manic depression) involves moods that swing between depression and mania. Diagnosis requires at least one manic episode in a lifetime; major depressive episodes are common but not required (APA, 2013).

A manic episode is a distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive or irritable mood and increased activity or energy, lasting at least one week, most of the day, nearly every day (APA, 2013, p. 124). Features include:

  • Euphoria or irritability
  • Excessive, rapid, loud talk
  • Flight of ideas: jumping from topic to topic
  • Distractibility
  • Grandiosity: inflated self-esteem
  • Little need for sleep
  • Many tasks at once
  • Reckless pleasure-seeking: spending sprees, reckless driving, foolish investments, gambling

An example of grandiosity: quitting a job to “strike it rich” on the stock market with no knowledge, experience or capital. People in a manic episode often feel nothing is wrong and resist treatment; reckless behavior can make involuntary hospitalization necessary.

The biological basis of mood disorders

Genes

Relatives of people with MDD have about double the usual risk; relatives of people with bipolar disorder, over nine times (Merikangas et al., 2011). Twin concordance points to a stronger genetic role in bipolar disorder:

Neurotransmitters

Mood disorders are linked to imbalances in norepinephrine and serotonin (Thase, 2009), which help regulate appetite, sex drive, sleep, arousal and mood. Many medications act at the synapse (OpenStax Figure 15.16): antidepressants usually boost serotonin and norepinephrine activity, while lithium, used for bipolar disorder, blocks norepinephrine activity at the synapse.

Brain regions

  • Amygdala (emotional significance): more active in depression, especially to negative stimuli such as sad faces (Surguladze et al., 2005; OpenStax Figure 15.17), even when they are not consciously perceived (Victor et al., 2010), and the activation persists after the stimulus is gone (Siegle et al., 2007).
  • Prefrontal cortex (emotion regulation): less active, particularly on the left (Davidson, Pizzagalli, & Nitschke, 2009). Normally it dampens the amygdala. The result: stronger reactions to negative events and more trouble controlling them.

Cortisol

Abnormal levels of cortisol, the stress hormone (Unit 14), have been found in depression since the 1950s. Many depressed people, especially those with early trauma such as losing a parent or childhood abuse, have elevated cortisol (Baes et al., 2012). High cortisol predicts later depression (Halligan et al., 2007) and activates the amygdala while deactivating the prefrontal cortex (McEwen, 2005). It is still unclear whether cortisol causes depression or stress raises cortisol and also triggers depression (Michaud et al., 2008).

Stress, genes and depression

  • Stressful life events such as loss, divorce, and serious health or money problems often precede depressive episodes (Brown & Harris, 1989; Mazure, 1998), especially exit events, in which someone leaves: a death, divorce or separation, a family member moving out (Paykel, 2003). They hit hardest when they humiliate or devalue the person: a breakup initiated by the partner led to more than twice the rate of MDD as the death of a loved one (Kendler et al., 2003).
  • Childhood adversity (parental separation, family turmoil, physical or sexual abuse) raises lifetime risk (Kessler, 1997). In a review of 16 studies with over 23,000 people, childhood maltreatment more than doubled the chance of recurring, persistent depression (Nanni, Uher, & Danese, 2012).
  • Genes × stress: most people who face stress do not become depressed, which fits the diathesis-stress model. Caspi et al. (2003) found that carriers of one or two short versions of the serotonin transporter gene 5-HTTLPR were more likely to become depressed after several stressful events, and rarely did without them.

Cognitive theories of depression

Beck’s cognitive theory

Aaron Beck (1976, 2008) proposed that depression-prone people hold depressive schemas: negative ways of thinking, formed in childhood, about loss, failure, rejection, worthlessness and inadequacy. They lie dormant until stress activates them. Cognitive biases keep them going: noticing negative details, interpreting events negatively, and blocking positive memories. Someone with a rejection schema notices every frown and recalls past rejections. Longitudinal studies support the theory (Dozois & Beck, 2008), and it is the basis of cognitive therapy (Unit 16).

Hopelessness theory

In hopelessness theory (Abramson, Metalsky, & Alloy, 1989), a negative thinking style leads to hopelessness (expecting bad outcomes that nothing can prevent), which leads to depression. The key is attributing negative events, especially important ones, to causes that are stable (lasting) and global (affecting many areas of life).

People with high cognitive vulnerability were seven times more likely to develop depression in the six months after uncontrollable stressors (Kleim, Gonzalo, & Ehlers, 2011).

Rumination theory

Developed in the late 1980s by Susan Nolen-Hoeksema (1987, 1991) to explain why depression is more common in women. Rumination is repetitively and passively focusing on the fact that one is depressed, and on its meaning and consequences, rather than distracting oneself or solving problems. Women ruminate more than men when sad (Butler & Nolen-Hoeksema, 1994), and rumination predicts more depressive symptoms (Nolen-Hoeksema, Larson, & Grayson, 1999), major depressive episodes (Abela & Hankin, 2011) and chronic depression (Robinson & Alloy, 2003).

Suicide

If this topic is personal

Any sign of suicidal thinking or self-harm should be taken seriously. The resources the textbook lists:

  • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (U.S.): call or text 988, 24 hours a day
  • Crisis Text Line (U.S.): text HOME to 741741
  • International Association for Suicide Prevention: iasp.info lists crisis resources by country

Suicide is death caused by intentional, self-directed injurious behavior (CDC, 2013a). It is not a DSM disorder but a serious outcome of many disorders: about 90% of people who die by suicide have at least one disorder, most often a mood disorder (Fleischman et al., 2005). It usually takes a combination of factors: vulnerability, access to lethal means and a lack of protective factors such as social support, religion, and coping and problem-solving skills.

Who is at risk (U.S. data, 2010)

  • Suicide was the 10th leading cause of death for all ages, with 38,364 deaths, about 105 a day (CDC, 2012). Official figures may undercount, since some deaths ruled accidents may be suicides.
  • Men die by suicide at about four times the rate of women and account for 79% of suicides.
  • Rates have risen in middle age since 2001; baby boomers may be vulnerable because of economic worries and easy access to prescription medication (Parker-Pope, 2013). The highest rates are among American Indian/Alaska Native and non-Hispanic White people, in the western mountain states (Berman, 2009), and in spring (April and May), with the lowest rates generally in winter (Postolache et al., 2010).

Risk factors

  • Alcohol dependence: about 10× the general risk (Wilcox, Conner, & Caine, 2004)
  • A previous attempt: 16% try again within a year (Owens, Horrocks, & House, 2002)
  • A firearm in the home (Brent & Bridge, 2003)
  • A family member’s suicide (Agerbo, Nordentoft, & Mortensen, 2002)

Warning signs

  • Withdrawing from others
  • Feeling like a burden
  • Reckless behavior (Berman, 2009)
  • Feeling entrapped, unable to escape one’s situation (O’Connor et al., 2013)

Contagion

  • U.S. suicides rose for a month after front-page New York Times suicide stories (Phillips, 1974)
  • Coverage of a celebrity suicide is over 14× as likely to be followed by copycat suicides (Stack, 2000)
  • Cyberbullying (McCabe, 2010) and online suicide notes (Ruder et al., 2011) can also play a role

Brain chemistry: low serotonin is linked to suicidal behavior (Pompili et al., 2010). It predicts attempts and deaths, appears in post-mortem studies (Mann, 2003), and is associated with aggression and impulsivity (Stanley et al., 2000). Depressed patients with very low serotonin attempted suicide more often and more violently (Asberg et al., 1976).

The CDC’s current suicide data page (see Resources) still lists American Indian/Alaska Native and non-Hispanic White people as the groups with the highest rates in 2023.

Nonsuicidal self-injury

Nonsuicidal self-injury (self-harm) is deliberately harming one’s own body to cope with emotional distress or regain a sense of control. It does not necessarily mean the person wants to die (Elena-Rodica et al., 2023), but it is a significant risk factor for suicide, especially alongside a mental disorder (Harris et al., 2019). It is associated with borderline personality disorder, depression, anxiety disorders and eating disorders (APA, 2022). People use it to manage pain, regulate emotions, express anger at themselves, distract from distress or communicate how they feel.

Signs include unexplained injuries, covering up in warm weather, social isolation, emotional instability and evasiveness about injuries. Treatment involves risk assessment, psychotherapy and treatment of underlying conditions. Scars can bring stigma and discrimination, for example in hiring and housing (Burke, 2019; Corrigan et al., 2014); mental health education helps reduce it.

Symptoms

Causes: genes

Schizophrenia runs in families: risk is nearly six times higher if a parent has it (Goldstein, Buka, Seidman, & Tsuang, 2010), and it rises with genetic relatedness (Gottesman, 2001; Figure 15.5). But relatives share environments, and identical twins may be treated more alike than fraternal twins, so family and twin studies cannot rule out environment. Adoption studies can:

  • Heston (1966) followed 97 adoptees for up to 36 years. Of 47 born to mothers with schizophrenia, 5 (11%) developed it; of 50 controls, none did.
  • Biological relatives of adoptees with schizophrenia are at higher risk than their adoptive relatives (Shih, Belmonte, & Zandi, 2004).
  • Tienari et al. (2004) studied 303 adoptees: 145 at high genetic risk (biological mother with schizophrenia) and 158 at low risk, raised in adoptive families rated healthy or disturbed (high criticism, conflict, poor problem solving).

Causes: neurotransmitters and the brain

Dopamine is the most studied neurotransmitter in schizophrenia. Drugs that increase dopamine can produce schizophrenia-like symptoms, and drugs that block it reduce symptoms (Howes & Kapur, 2009). The dopamine hypothesis (Snyder, 1976) holds that too much dopamine or too many dopamine receptors cause the onset and maintenance of schizophrenia. Newer work suggests the picture differs by region (Davis, Kahn, Ko, & Davidson, 1991):

Hallucinations and delusions

An overabundance of dopamine may produce these symptoms.

Prefrontal cortex · too little

Negative symptoms

Low dopamine levels may be mainly responsible for avolition, alogia and the rest.

Serotonin is also involved: newer antipsychotic medications work partly by blocking serotonin receptors (Baumeister & Hawkins, 2004).

Brain structure: people with schizophrenia show enlarged ventricles (fluid-filled cavities), implying loss of surrounding tissue (Green, 2001); less gray matter in the frontal lobes (Lawrie & Abukmeil, 1998); and less frontal-lobe activity during cognitive tasks (Buchsbaum et al., 1990). The frontal lobes handle planning, attention, speech, movement and problem solving, the abilities most affected.

Causes: pregnancy and marijuana

  • Obstetric complications are more common in the births of people who later develop schizophrenia (Cannon, Jones, & Murray, 2002).
  • Influenza during the mother’s first trimester raises risk (Brown et al., 2004), as does a death in the family during the first trimester (Khashan et al., 2008).

People with schizophrenia are more likely to use marijuana (Thornicroft, 1990), but a correlation alone cannot show which causes which. Longitudinal studies help. Andréasson et al. (1987) followed over 45,000 Swedish conscripts for 15 years:

A review of 35 longitudinal studies found higher risk of schizophrenia and other psychoses, greatest in the heaviest users (Moore et al., 2007), and use is linked to earlier onset (Large et al., 2011). Marijuana seems to play a causal role, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient: not everyone with schizophrenia has used it, and most users never develop schizophrenia (Casadio, Fernandes, Murray, & Di Forti, 2011). Early use may disrupt brain development in adolescence (Trezza, Cuomo, & Vanderschuren, 2008) and trigger the disorder in people already vulnerable.

Early warning signs

Early detection has improved outcomes for heart disease and cancer, so researchers are trying the same with psychosis. Prodromal symptoms are early, minor psychotic symptoms: unusual thought content, paranoia, odd communication, delusions, problems at school or work, and declining social functioning. Following people with these symptoms shows who goes on to develop a psychotic disorder. The best predictors (Fusar-Poli et al., 2013):

  1. Genetic risk (family history of psychosis)
  2. Recent decline in functioning
  3. High levels of unusual thought content
  4. High levels of suspicion or paranoia
  5. Poor social functioning
  6. A history of substance abuse

Better prediction could direct early intervention to those at greatest risk.

Dig deeper: forensic psychology

In August 2013, 17-year-old Cody Metzker-Madsen killed 5-year-old Dominic Elkins, believing he was fighting goblins and that Dominic was their commander. Forensic psychologist Dr. Alan Goldstein testified that he believed the goblins were real, and he was found not guilty by reason of insanity (Nelson, 2014). He will stay in a psychiatric facility until he is no longer dangerous; such confinement often lasts as long as or longer than a prison sentence. Forensic psychology assesses and treats people in the legal system and advises on personal injury, workers’ compensation, competency to stand trial and insanity pleas. Keep the opening case in mind: only 3–5% of violent acts are committed by people with severe mental illness, who are more than 10 times as likely as others to be victims of crime (MentalHealth.gov, 2017).

Dissociative amnesia

Amnesia is the partial or total forgetting of an experience or event. In dissociative amnesia it follows extreme stress such as combat, a natural disaster or violence. About 1.8% of people in upstate New York communities had it in the past year (Johnson, Cohen, Kasen, & Brook, 2006).

A form of dissociative amnesia is dissociative fugue (from the French “to flee”): suddenly wandering away from home, confused about one’s identity and sometimes adopting a new one. Most episodes last hours or days (Cardeña & Gleaves, 2006).

A disputed diagnosis

  • Some question it outright (Pope, Hudson, Bodkin, & Oliva, 1998); McNally (2003, p. 275) called it “a piece of psychiatric folklore” lacking convincing empirical support.
  • Publications on it rose in the 1980s, peaked in the mid-1990s and fell sharply by 2003, when only 13 cases appeared in the literature worldwide (Pope, Barry, Bodkin, & Hudson, 2006).
  • No fictional or nonfictional work before 1800 describes trauma-related dissociative amnesia (Pope, Poliakoff, Parker, Boynes, & Hudson, 2006).
  • On the other side, nearly 10% of 82 people at a psychiatric outpatient clinic met criteria, suggesting it is underdiagnosed in psychiatric populations (Foote, Smolin, Kaplan, Legatt, & Lipschitz, 2006).

Depersonalization/derealization disorder

The self

Depersonalization

Feeling unreal, detached or unfamiliar with oneself (APA, 2013, p. 302): thoughts and feelings seem not one’s own; feeling robotic, without control of movements or speech; a distorted sense of time; at the extreme, an “out-of-body” experience of watching oneself from outside.

The world

Derealization

Feeling that the world, people, objects or surroundings are unreal, detached or unfamiliar (APA, 2013, p. 303): as if in a fog or dream, or as if the world were artificial.

People with this disorder often find their symptoms hard to describe and may fear they are “going crazy” (APA, 2013).

Dissociative identity disorder

Dissociative identity disorder (DID), formerly multiple personality disorder, is the best known. It involves two or more separate personality states, each distinct in affect, behavior, consciousness, memory, perception, cognition and/or sensory-motor functioning. When another identity is in control, there may be memory gaps: finding unfamiliar items in shopping bags or among one’s possessions. Diagnosis requires memory disruptions, for traumatic events, daily events and/or personal information, more serious than ordinary forgetting. Some people can talk internally with other personality states, which can resemble hearing voices (APA, 2013). About 1.5% of the upstate New York sample had DID symptoms in the past year (Johnson et al., 2006).

Doubts

A controversial diagnosis

  • More cases were identified in the five years before 1986 than in the previous two centuries (Putnam, Guroff, Silberman, Barban, & Post, 1986).
  • The 1970s book and film Sybil, about a woman with 16 personalities, may have encouraged overdiagnosis (Piper & Merskey, 2004).
  • The False Memory Foundation, formed by families accused of childhood abuse, questioned its validity (Pope, 1996).
  • Many psychologists think DID is iatrogenic: induced by therapists who look for it.
Support

Evidence for the disorder

  • Some reports of childhood trauma have been corroborated by medical or legal records (Cardeña & Gleaves, 2006).
  • Brain scans (fMRI) differed significantly between people with DID and controls told to fake it (Reinders et al., 2019).
  • Trauma can produce dissociative states, and dissociation may be a psychologically important way of coping with threat (Dalenberg et al., 2012).

Research continues on childhood trauma, attachment, genetics and environment (Dell & O’Neil, 2009), and qualitative work describes non-pathological experiences of “multiplicity” that some people do not see as disordered or caused by trauma (Eve, Heyes, & Parry, 2023).

Prevalence and consequences

  • About 8% of children have ADHD (Danielson et al., 2016), and about 60% of them still have it as adults (Sibley et al., 2016).

  • In childhood: lower grades and test scores, more expulsion, grade retention and dropping out (Loe & Feldman, 2007); more peer rejection (Hoza et al., 2005). Adolescents and young adults with ADHD get more traffic tickets and have more accidents (Thompson et al., 2007).
  • Comorbidity: 81% of those whose ADHD persisted into adulthood had at least one other disorder, versus 47% of those whose ADHD did not persist (Barbaresi et al., 2013).
  • In adulthood: 135 adults diagnosed in the 1970s, compared with 136 controls, had less education, lower socioeconomic status and less prestigious jobs, more unemployment and lower earnings, worse work and social functioning, more divorce, and more non-alcohol substance abuse (Klein et al., 2012). Childhood ADHD also predicts later drinking, daily smoking and drug use (Molina & Pelham, 2003), especially with antisocial tendencies (Marshal & Molina, 2006). Outcomes are better with treatment (Harpin, 2013; Arnold et al., 2015).

Causes of ADHD

  • Genes have the strongest evidence. Heritability is about 71% for inattention and 73% for hyperactivity (Nikolas & Burt, 2010).
  • Dopamine: implicated genes include at least two involved in dopamine regulation (Gizer, Ficks, & Waldman, 2009). Stimulant medications such as methylphenidate (Ritalin) and amphetamine with dextroamphetamine (Adderall) increase dopamine activity, and people with ADHD show less dopamine activity in motivation and reward areas (Volkow et al., 2009; Swanson et al., 2007).
  • The brain: children with ADHD have smaller frontal lobes and less frontal activation during mental tasks. The frontal lobes help inhibit behavior, which may explain the impulsivity.
  • Prenatal nicotine exposure is linked to ADHD (Linnet et al., 2003), and maternal smoking to more severe symptoms (Thakur et al., 2013).
  • Not supported: sugar has no effect on children’s behavior or cognition (a review of 16 studies: Wolraich, Wilson, & White, 1995), and food additives have only a small effect, even on children without ADHD (McCann et al., 2007). Poor parenting is not a cause: twin and adoption data show family environment plays only a small role.

Dig deeper: is ADHD on the rise?

Parent-reported ADHD among U.S. children aged 4–17 rose 22%, from 7.8% in 2003 to 9.5% in 2007 (CDC, 2010), with the largest increases at ages 15–17, among multiracial and Hispanic children, and among children whose primary language was not English. Another survey found a rise from 6.9% (1998–2000) to 9.0% (2007–2009) among ages 5–17 (Akinbami et al., 2011). These are parents’ reports of what a provider told them, not formal diagnoses. Possible explanations are speculative: overdiagnosis and quick medication, greater awareness and more referrals, heavy use of electronic devices shortening attention spans, and changes in diagnostic criteria.

Autism spectrum disorder

In 1943, Leo Kanner described “early infantile autism”: children who could not form close emotional ties, had speech and language abnormalities, repeated behaviors and could not tolerate small changes in routine (Bregman, 2005). The DSM-5’s autism spectrum disorder (ASD) extends his work and covers three areas (APA, 2013):

1 · Social interaction

Not starting conversations, turning away when spoken to, little eye contact, preferring to play alone, seeming to live in a world of one’s own.

2 · Communication

From no speech at all, to one-word answers, to echolalia (repeating others’ speech, immediately or days later), to trouble keeping a conversation going and reading nonverbal cues.

3 · Repetitive behavior, restricted interests

Stereotyped movements (rocking, head-banging, dropping and picking up an object), distress at small changes, and intense, fixated interests such as memorizing details for no apparent purpose.

Symptoms appear early and cause serious impairment. ASD is not intellectual disability, though the two often co-occur. “Spectrum” replaces the narrower “autistic disorder” of earlier editions to reflect a range of severity. The DSM-5 also dropped Asperger’s disorder, a milder form with average or high intelligence and strong vocabulary but impaired social communication (Wing, Gould, & Gillberg, 2011), because research did not show it differs qualitatively from autism; some worried children would lose access to services (“Asperger’s Syndrome Dropped,” 2012). Some adults with ASD, especially those with stronger language and intellectual skills, live and work independently; most do not.

Prevalence

Reported cases rose 273% in California from 1987 to 1998 (Byrd, 2002) and U.S. diagnoses rose 78% from 2000 to 2008 (CDC, 2012), probably reflecting broader criteria, more screening, greater awareness and acceptance, and better-trained clinicians (Novella, 2008).

The CDC’s latest report shows the rise continuing: about 1 in 31 eight-year-olds in 2022 (CDC, 2025; switch the figure above to see it).

Causes

Despite two decades of research, the exact causes are unknown (Meek et al., 2013). Concordance is 60–90% for identical twins but 5–10% for fraternal twins and siblings (Autism Genome Project Consortium, 2007). Many genes and mutations are involved, including genes for forming synaptic circuits (Gauthier et al., 2011). Environmental factors, perhaps acting by causing new mutations, include pollutants such as plant emissions and mercury, urban versus rural living, and vitamin D deficiency (Kinney et al., 2009).

Dig deeper: the vaccine myth

In the late 1990s, an article in a prestigious medical journal claimed the MMR (measles, mumps, rubella) vaccine triggered autism; it reported that, according to their parents, 8 of 12 children developed autism-like symptoms soon after vaccination. The article was retracted after evidence of fraud and unethical practices by the lead researcher, but the fear lingered: about one-third of surveyed parents worried about a vaccine-autism link (Kennedy et al., 2011), and more than 10% of parents of young children refuse or delay vaccines (Dempsey et al., 2011).

The evidence: none supports a link (Hughes, 2007). DeStefano, Price and Weintraub (2013) compared the vaccine records of 256 children with ASD and 752 controls, counting the immunogens (infection-fighting substances in vaccines) received from birth to 3 months, 7 months and 2 years. Exposure did not differ between the groups (OpenStax Figure 15.19). The myth persists because the internet spreads exaggerated claims, complex science is hard to judge (Downs, 2008), symptoms appearing after a vaccination look like cause and effect, and, like the sugar theory of ADHD, it offers a simple explanation for a disorder that has none.

Personality Disorders

Personality is our stable, distinctive way of thinking, feeling, acting and relating to the world. A personality disorder is a style that deviates markedly from cultural expectations and is pervasive and inflexible.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the nature of personality disorders and how they differ from other disorders
  • List and distinguish between the three clusters of personality disorders
  • Identify the basic features of borderline personality disorder and antisocial personality disorder, and the factors that are important in the etiology of both

What a personality disorder is

The style must differ markedly from cultural expectations, be pervasive and inflexible, begin in adolescence or early adulthood, and cause distress or impairment (APA, 2013). People with personality disorders often clash with others, struggle to form and keep relationships, and fail to reach realistic goals. Slightly over 9% of the U.S. population has one; avoidant and schizoid are the most frequent (Lenzenweger, Lane, Loranger, & Kessler, 2007).

Borderline personality disorder

The term was coined in the late 1930s for patients who seemed anxious but had brief psychotic episodes, “on the borderline” between anxiety and psychosis (Freeman, Stone, Martin, & Reinecke, 2005). Its meaning today is entirely different. Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is marked by instability in relationships, self-image and mood, and marked impulsivity (APA, 2013):

  • Fear of abandonment: cannot tolerate being alone; frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined separation, including suicidal gestures and self-mutilation.
  • Intense, unstable relationships: a partner may be idealized, then devalued at the first sign of waning interest.
  • Unstable self-view: sudden shifts in goals, interests, career plans and friends, like a successful law student suddenly thinking of dropping out.
  • Self-destructive impulsivity: gambling, spending, substance abuse, unsafe sex, reckless driving.
  • Intense, poorly controlled anger: moodiness, sarcasm, bitterness, verbal abuse.

Prevalence: about 1.4% of the U.S. population (Lenzenweger et al., 2007), but about 10% of mental health outpatients and 20% of psychiatric inpatients (APA, 2013). It is often comorbid with anxiety, mood and substance use disorders.

Causes: the core traits, impulsivity and emotional instability, are highly heritable (Livesley, 2008), and rates among relatives reach 24.9% (White, Gunderson, Zanarini, & Hudson, 2003). People with BPD report childhood physical, sexual and/or emotional abuse far more often than others (Afifi et al., 2010). Rates are highest in people with a “borderline temperament” (high novelty seeking and high harm avoidance) who also suffered childhood abuse or neglect (Joyce et al., 2003): genes and environment together.

Antisocial personality disorder

Most people learn a moral compass early: don’t lie, cheat, steal or exploit others, and keep your responsibilities. People with antisocial personality disorder (ASPD) seem to lack one. They show no regard for others’ rights or feelings and no remorse for harming them: repeated illegal acts, lying and conning, impulsivity and recklessness, irritability and aggression, and irresponsibility such as leaving debts unpaid. Signs can appear early, but the diagnosis requires being at least 18 years old. They tend to see the world as self-serving and unkind, others as tools to use, and themselves with inflated arrogance, often with superficial charm and a lack of empathy.

Alternative DSM-5 model: rates the level of personality functioning, impairment of self (identity, self-direction) and interpersonal functioning (empathy, intimacy) (Hopwood et al., 2012), plus traits. ASPD’s predominant traits are antagonism (disregard for others, manipulation, deceit) and disinhibition (impulsivity, irresponsibility, risk-taking). A psychopathy specifier adds attention seeking and low anxiousness about the consequences of risky behavior (Crego & Widiger, 2014).

Prevalence: about 3.6% of the population, with men affected 3 to 1 (Compton, Conway, Stinson, Colliver, & Grant, 2005). It is more common among men who are younger, widowed, separated or divorced, of lower socioeconomic status, urban, or living in the western U.S.

Causes of ASPD

  • Genes and environment: family, twin and adoption studies show both contribute to ASPD and to antisocial behavior generally (Baker, Bezdjian, & Raine, 2006); fearlessness, impulsive antisociality and callousness have a substantial genetic component (Livesley & Jang, 2008). Adoptees whose biological parents had ASPD were more likely to become antisocial if raised in adverse adoptive homes (marital problems, divorce, drug use, legal problems) than in normal ones (Cadoret et al., 1995; Rhee & Waldman, 2002).
  • Environment: research focuses on community, family structure and functioning, and peer groups. Herrenkohl et al. (2000) followed more than 800 Seattle-area youths, measuring risk factors at ages 10, 14, 16 and 18.
  • Low fear: people with antisocial tendencies show little fear of cues that signal punishment or pain. When anticipating an electric shock, they show lower skin conductance (sweat-gland activity, a measure of sympathetic arousal or anxiety) than others (Hare, 1965). Low skin conductance may reflect emotional deficits and impulsivity (Fung et al., 2005).
  • Rewards: in 119 incarcerated men, large rewards impaired perception in those with ASPD, worsened executive function when they were aware of the reward, and worsened inhibition when a task taxed working memory (Stuppy-Sullivan & Baskin-Sommers, 2019).
