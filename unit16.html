Unit 16 · Overview

Therapy and Treatment

How mental health care has changed from asylums to community clinics, the main kinds of psychotherapy and biomedical therapy, the formats therapy takes, the special case of addiction, and the role culture plays in who seeks help.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

What therapy looks like

Ask people to picture therapy and many imagine the classic Freudian scene: a patient on a couch talking about childhood while the therapist takes notes. Therapy can just as easily be someone wearing virtual reality headgear to overcome a fear of snakes. OpenStax Figure 16.1 shows a third version: marines who served in Iraq and Afghanistan sitting in a circle on a beach in the Ocean Therapy program at Camp Pendleton, which combines learning to surf with group discussion to help veterans recover, especially from PTSD.

Therapy covers both psychological and biological interventions, all aimed at reducing distress. Because psychological problems can stem from biology, genetics, childhood experiences, conditioning and sociocultural influences, psychologists have developed many techniques, and no single method works for everyone.

The point of treatment

Seeking help can be hard and frightening, and the chapter credits people who do so with strength and courage in facing a highly stigmatized problem. The goal of treatment is to address symptoms and/or underlying conditions, not to change who a person is.

Unit 16 · Section 16.1

Mental Health Treatment: Past and Present

For most of history, people with psychological disorders were feared, punished or locked away. Humane care is recent, and access to it is still uneven.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how people with psychological disorders have been treated throughout the ages
  • Discuss deinstitutionalization
  • Discuss the ways in which mental health services are delivered today
  • Distinguish between voluntary and involuntary treatment
Read Section 16.1 on OpenStax ↗

Who gets treatment

Mental illness is common. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services reported that 18.9% of U.S. adults experienced mental illness in 2017. The rate for teens (13–18) is similar, and about 13% of children aged 8–15 have a mental illness in a given year (NIMH, 2017).

Far fewer are treated. SAMHSA reported that 14.8% of adults received treatment for a mental health issue in 2017 (inpatient or outpatient care and/or prescription medication). Between 2004 and 2008 the share hovered at or just below 13%, rising toward 14% (OpenStax Figure 16.2). Among children with a mental disorder, the CDC’s NHANES survey found that only about half (50.6%) received treatment in the past year, and disorders that show up in behavior are the most likely to be treated.

Treatment in the past

Early explanations blamed mental illness on demonic possession, witchcraft or an angry god (Szasz, 1960). Treatment followed from those beliefs:

  • Exorcism: priests or other religious figures recited incantations and prayers, sometimes with medicinal drinks.
  • Trephining: a small hole was made in the skull to release the spirits. Most people treated this way died.
  • Others were executed, imprisoned or left to live as beggars. From the late 1400s to the late 1600s, people accused of pacts with the devil were tried, often condemned and burned; tens of thousands of mentally ill people were killed after being accused of witchcraft (Hemphill, 1966).

In the 18th century came the asylum, the first institution built specifically to house people with psychological disorders. The focus was isolation, not treatment: people were kept in windowless dungeons, beaten and chained to their beds (OpenStax Figure 16.4 shows Goya’s The Madhouse). Reformers changed this:

France · 1795

Philippe Pinel

At La Salpêtrière in Paris, Pinel unchained patients and talked with them (OpenStax Figure 16.5). Many improved and were able to leave.

United States · 19th century

Dorothea Dix

Found an underfunded, unregulated system that abused poor people with mental illness. Her lobbying of state legislatures and Congress led to the first U.S. mental asylums (OpenStax Figure 16.6).

Asylums were still filthy, offered little treatment and often held people for decades. At New York’s Willard Psychiatric Center, treatments included long, cold baths and electroshock that sometimes broke patients’ backs; in 1943 its doctors gave 1,443 shock treatments. Willard did not close until 1995. Electroconvulsive treatment is still used today, but now as a brief stimulus under anesthesia, and whether its benefits outweigh its side effects is still debated.

Deinstitutionalization

Two changes emptied the asylums. Antipsychotic medications (introduced in 1954) helped control hallucinations and delusions, symptoms that signal a loss of contact with reality. Then in 1963 President John F. Kennedy signed the Mental Retardation Facilities and Community Mental Health Centers Construction Act, which funded community mental health centers. This began deinstitutionalization: closing large asylums so that people could stay in their communities and be treated locally.

The idea was that released patients would be served by the new centers. The system did not work as planned:

  • the centers were underfunded, and staff were not trained for severe illnesses such as schizophrenia
  • staff burnout was high
  • basic needs such as housing, food and job training were not provided

Many people released from institutions became homeless instead.

Treatment today

Many people with mental illness now end up homeless or in the corrections system (OpenStax Figure 16.7). About one in four homeless people in U.S. shelters has a severe mental illness (HUD, 2011), and in 2006 the Bureau of Justice Statistics counted large numbers of adults with mental health issues behind bars. People with mental illness are also two to four times more common on probation and parole than in the general population. Incarceration often makes conditions worse, exposing people to violence and isolation and adding long-term effects such as PTSD and depression.

Asylums have been replaced by state psychiatric hospitals and local community hospitals that focus on short-term care. Stays now average under two weeks, often only several days. A night in a psychiatric hospital costs about $800–$1,000 (Stensland et al., 2012), so insurers often limit stays. Hospitalization is usually reserved for people who are an imminent threat to themselves or others.

Who seeks treatment, and how

Most people with mental illness are never hospitalized. People reach treatment in several ways: on their own, at the urging of a friend, spouse or parent, or by referral from a primary care physician. Child protective services may refer parents whose children were removed for abuse or neglect; the children typically receive trauma treatment, with the aim of reuniting the family if the parents can change, or of helping the child adjust to foster care or adoption if they cannot. Play may be part of therapy with children (OpenStax Figure 16.8).

Chosen by the person

Voluntary treatment

The person chooses to attend therapy to get relief from symptoms.

Required of the person

Involuntary treatment

Therapy that is not the person’s choice, such as court-mandated counseling or therapy as a condition of parole.

Therapy takes place in community mental health centers, private or community practices, schools (counselors, psychologists and social workers) and prisons (often group therapy). It is offered by psychologists, psychiatrists, clinical social workers, marriage and family therapists and trained religious personnel, with licensing rules that vary by state.

Paying for and reaching care

Treatment is paid for by health insurance, government programs or the client. The Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act of 2008 requires group health plans and insurers to offer mental health and substance use coverage on par with physical illness: copays, visit limits and deductibles for mental health care cannot be more restrictive.

Barriers remain: few options in rural and low-income urban areas, waiting lists, poor care for people who cannot pay, and costs such as copays, deductibles and time off work. Over 85% of the 1,669 federally designated mental health professional shortage areas are rural, so primary care physicians and police are often the first line of mental health care despite lacking specialized training (Ivey et al., 1998). Rural care faces problems of availability, accessibility and acceptability (stigma), and about two-thirds of people with symptoms receive no care at all (HHS, 2005; Wagenfeld et al., 1994). In 2013 the U.S. Department of Agriculture announced $50 million to improve rural access.

Unit 16 · Section 16.2

Types of Treatment

Portia Nelson’s poem Autobiography in Five Short Chapters (1993) describes falling into the same hole in the sidewalk again and again, until the walker learns to see it and finally walks down another street. That is the aim of therapy: to break a destructive pattern and find a better solution.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish between psychotherapy and biomedical therapy
  • Recognize various orientations to psychotherapy
  • Discuss psychotropic medications and recognize which medications are used to treat specific psychological disorders

Two kinds of treatment

Psychological

Psychotherapy

Psychological treatment that uses a range of methods to help someone overcome personal problems or attain personal growth. The textbook notes that in modern practice it has evolved into psychodynamic therapy.

Biological

Biomedical therapy

Treatment with medication and/or medical procedures.

Both can help people with disorders such as depression, anxiety and schizophrenia. Table 16.1 previews the six psychotherapies covered next.

Psychoanalysis

Psychoanalysis, developed by Sigmund Freud, was the first form of psychotherapy and dominated the early 20th century, though its popularity has since declined sharply. Freud believed problems come from repressed impulses and childhood trauma, so therapy aims to bring long-buried feelings to light. Its techniques:

  • Free association: the patient relaxes and says whatever comes to mind. When the ego blocks painful material, the patient shows resistance.
  • Dream analysis: the therapist interprets the hidden meaning of dreams to reveal unconscious wishes or conflicts.
  • Transference: the patient transfers feelings from other relationships onto the analyst. For example, Crystal comes to see her therapist as a father figure.

Treatment typically lasted years. Freud’s patients lay on a couch facing away from him so they would feel less inhibited (OpenStax Figure 16.9); today patients usually sit facing the therapist (Prochaska & Norcross, 2010). Modern psychodynamic therapy keeps the focus on internal drives and forces but is much less intensive.

Play therapy

Play therapy is used mostly with children, who may not sit still for talk therapy. Children play out hopes, fantasies and traumas with dolls, stuffed animals and sandbox figurines, and therapists also use play to help diagnose problems.

Child leads

Nondirective play therapy

Children play freely while the therapist observes (LeBlanc & Ritchie, 2001).

Therapist leads

Directive play therapy

The therapist gives structure: suggests topics, asks questions and may play along (Harter, 1977).

In sandplay (sandtray) therapy, children build three-dimensional worlds that reflect their inner state (Kalff, 1991; OpenStax Figure 16.10).

Behavior therapy

Behavior therapy uses the principles of learning to change undesirable behaviors such as phobias and bedwetting, rather than exploring the unconscious. It draws on both classical and operant conditioning.

Classical techniques, from Pavlov’s work, treat a dysfunctional behavior as a conditioned response. Eight-year-old Emmie wets the bed; a moisture-sensing pad sets off an alarm that wakes her, and after repeated pairings she stops. A central technique is counterconditioning: the client learns a new response to a stimulus that used to trigger an undesirable behavior. It takes two forms:

Make it unpleasant

Aversive conditioning

Pairs an unpleasant stimulus (a mild shock, a bad taste) with an unwanted behavior such as smoking or nail biting. Used for addictions: Antabuse causes nausea, vomiting, a racing heart, headache and shortness of breath when combined with alcohol, building a conditioned aversion to drinking.

Face the fear

Exposure therapy

The client confronts the feared object or situation, in reality, imagination or virtual reality, until the fear fades through habituation.

Exposure was first reported in 1924 by Mary Cover Jones, the “mother of behavior therapy.” Her patient Peter was afraid of rabbits.

Systematic desensitization

Joseph Wolpe (1958) refined Jones’s approach into systematic desensitization, gradual exposure used for phobias and anxiety disorders. The client:

  1. builds an anxiety hierarchy: situations ranked from least to most feared
  2. learns relaxation techniques such as deep breathing and muscle relaxation
  3. works up the hierarchy, relaxing at each step before moving on

The principle: you cannot feel anxious and relaxed at the same time.

Example: Jayden fears elevators even though nothing bad has ever happened to him in one, and his new job is on the 29th floor. He builds a hierarchy running from feeling nervous near others in an elevator up to being trapped, practices progressive relaxation while imagining each step, and then practices in real elevators.

Virtual reality exposure therapy uses a simulation when real exposure would be impractical, expensive or embarrassing. It has worked for fear of public speaking, claustrophobia, fear of flying (aviophobia) and PTSD (Gerardi et al., 2010). In Virtual Iraq, a program for combat veterans with PTSD, about 80% of those who completed treatment showed clinically significant reductions in PTSD, anxiety and depression (Rizzo et al., 2010).

Operant techniques: token economies

Operant conditioning holds that reinforced behaviors are repeated and unreinforced behaviors are extinguished. In a token economy, a controlled setting, people earn tokens (such as poker chips) for desirable behavior and exchange them for items or privileges. Psychiatric hospitals use token economies to encourage cooperation and activity, with rewards such as extra TV time or a private room (Dickerson, Tenhula, & Green-Paden, 2005).

Cognitive therapy

Cognitive therapy focuses on how thoughts lead to distress. Its core idea: how you think determines how you feel and act. Therapists help clients recognize cognitive distortions and replace them with more rational beliefs. Ray fails one Psychology 101 test and concludes he will fail the course and probably flunk out of college; a cognitive therapist would help him see that one test does not justify that conclusion.

Psychiatrist Aaron Beck developed cognitive therapy in the 1960s, first for depression (Beck, Rush, Shaw, & Emery, 1979). Its techniques include questioning that helps clients spot dysfunctional ideas and challenge catastrophizing (Beck, 2011). As OpenStax Figure 16.13 shows, the same failed test can lead to depression (“I’m worthless and stupid”) or not (“I’m smart, but I didn’t study”): emotions follow from interpretations, not just events.

Cognitive-behavioral therapy

Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) works on both thinking and behavior, helping clients change cognitive distortions and self-defeating behaviors. It focuses on present issues rather than childhood or the past. One of its first forms was rational emotive therapy (RET), founded by Albert Ellis, partly out of his dislike of Freudian psychoanalysis and with influence from behaviorists such as Wolpe.

Hundreds of studies support CBT for depression, PTSD, anxiety disorders, eating disorders, bipolar disorder and substance abuse. For example, it reduced hopelessness and suicidal thoughts in suicidal teenagers (Alavi et al., 2013) and reduced PTSD in transit workers (Lowinger & Rombom, 2012).

CBT uses the ABC model: an Action (activating event), the Belief about it, and the Consequences of that belief.

Common cognitive distortions

Black or white

All-or-nothing thinking

Seeing everything as one extreme or the other; common in depression. Jon: “I will be alone forever.”

Small to huge

Overgeneralization

Turning a small event into a sweeping verdict: “I am ugly, a loser, and no one is ever going to be interested in me.”

No evidence

Jumping to conclusions

Assuming others think badly of you. Savannah decides Hillaire doesn’t want to be friends when she doesn’t call back, though Hillaire may have lost her phone or just been busy.

A client quoted in the textbook (Martin, 2007) found CBT the most useful approach for his depression because it gave him practical tools for managing his mood every day.

Humanistic therapy

Humanistic therapy, based on humanistic psychology’s focus on reaching one’s potential, aims for greater self-awareness and self-acceptance. It works with conscious thoughts and the present and future, not the unconscious or the past.

Carl Rogers developed Rogerian (client-centered therapy). He said “client” instead of “patient” because “patient” implies someone sick who needs a cure. It is a nondirective therapy: the therapist gives no advice or interpretations but helps clients identify their conflicts and understand their feelings (Rogers, 1951). Its tools:

  • Active listening: the therapist acknowledges, restates and clarifies what the client expresses.
  • Unconditional positive regard: accepting the client fully, without judgment, whatever they say or do.
  • Genuineness, empathy and acceptance, which help clients accept themselves and grow.

Does therapy work?

The American Psychological Association names three factors in successful treatment:

1 · Evidence

Evidence-based treatment

A treatment shown to work for this specific problem.

2 · Therapist

Clinical expertise

The skill of the psychologist or therapist.

3 · Client

The client

Their characteristics, values, preferences and culture.

Research findings are mixed. A review of 16 meta-analyses found CBT as effective as or more effective than other therapies for PTSD, generalized anxiety disorder, depression and social phobia (Butler et al., 2006). Others found psychodynamic therapy as effective as CBT (Shedler, 2010), and no study has shown one approach to be more effective than another overall (Abbass, Kisely, & Kroenke, 2006; Chorpita et al., 2011). Clinicians’ training and experience have not been linked to outcomes (Wampold, 2007). What does matter is the therapeutic relationship between client and therapist.

Psychotropic medications

Biomedical therapy mostly means psychotropic medications, often given alongside psychotherapy but sometimes alone. They are prescribed by medical doctors, including psychiatrists; the textbook notes that in Louisiana and New Mexico psychologists may also prescribe some of them (APA, 2014). Medications work by changing the levels or effects of neurotransmitters: SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) increase serotonin, and SNRIs increase serotonin and norepinephrine.

Update since the textbook

More states have since given psychologists prescribing authority. By mid-2023, the APA’s Monitor on Psychology listed six: New Mexico (2002), Louisiana (2004), Illinois, Iowa, Idaho and Colorado, plus Guam and some federal settings such as the Department of Defense and the Indian Health Service (DeAngelis, 2023; see Resources).

Medications relieve symptoms but usually do not cure the disorder. Some people need them only briefly; people with severe disorders such as bipolar disorder or schizophrenia may need long-term treatment. Medication tends to work best combined with psychotherapy, especially for depressive and anxiety disorders (Cuijpers et al., 2014).

Other biomedical treatments

Electrical current

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)

Once called “electroshock therapy.” A current induces seizures to relieve severe depression, usually after medication has failed. How it works is unknown, but about 85% of people treated improve (Reti, n.d.). Because repeated treatments can cause memory loss, it is a last resort.

Magnetic fields

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)

Magnetic fields stimulate nerve cells in the brain to improve depression. Approved by the FDA in 2008, it is used when other treatments have not worked (Mayo Clinic, 2012).

Dig deeper: evidence-based practice

Evidence-based practice uses research to guide clinical decisions and reduce errors in choosing treatments (Sackett & Rosenberg, 1995); medicine has used it for about two decades. Organizations such as the APA recommend specific treatments for specific disorders (Chambless & Ollendick, 2001), and the APA defines the approach as integrating the best research with clinical expertise and the patient’s characteristics, culture and preferences.

For

Treatment manuals

Best practices are written into manuals that trained therapists follow. This reduces variation between therapists and raises the chance clients get an effective treatment (Charman & Barkham, 2005).

Against

Individual judgment

Critics argue that results from trials cannot simply be applied to individuals, and that treatment should rest on the therapist’s judgment (Mullen & Streiner, 2004).

Unit 16 · Section 16.3

Treatment Modalities

Any orientation can be delivered in different formats. The four most common are individual, group, couples and family therapy.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish between the various modalities of treatment
  • Discuss benefits of group therapy
Read Section 16.3 on OpenStax ↗

Intake and treatment planning

Treatment begins with an intake, the therapist’s first meeting with the client, used to gather information and address immediate needs. The therapist then explains confidentiality: a therapist cannot share a client’s communications with third parties unless the law requires or allows it.

Four modalities

In individual therapy (also called individual psychotherapy or counseling), client and clinician meet one-on-one, usually for 45 minutes to 1 hour, weekly or every other week, in a confidential setting. The clinician helps the client explore feelings, work through challenges, identify what they want to change and set goals. Treatment may last a few sessions or a year or more.

Group therapy

In group therapy, a clinician meets with several clients who share similar problems, usually 5–10 people, on issues such as divorce, grief, eating disorders, substance abuse or anger management (OpenStax Figure 16.16). With children, matching members by age and problem matters especially.

Benefits

Why groups help

  • Less shame and isolation, plus support from the therapist and other members (APA, 2014): a sexually abused nine-year-old learns he is not alone; a woman with postpartum depression feels less guilty and more supported
  • Members can confront each other about their patterns
  • A child with poor social skills benefits from a skills curriculum
  • The recommended treatment for some problems, such as sexual abuse perpetration
Limitations

What can go wrong

  • Members may fear speaking in front of strangers
  • Personalities can clash and arguments break out
  • A member might repeat outside the group what others said, a risk to confidentiality

For people who have committed sexual abuse, groups are more economical; members admit offenses more readily when others model openness, may accept feedback from peers more readily than from a therapist, and can practice social skills (McGrath et al., 2009).

Psycho-educational groups have a strong teaching component, such as a group for children whose parents have cancer that covers the disease, its treatment and side effects like hair loss. School groups, often led by a school counselor, psychologist or social worker, tackle test anxiety, social isolation, self-esteem, bullying and school failure, and group therapy has been found effective for children facing many kinds of challenges (Shechtman, 2002).

Groups can have a theme, be time-limited, have open or closed membership, and be structured (planned activities and goals) or unstructured (members set the direction). Unstructured groups can be complex and emotionally charged but offer chances for personal growth (Page & Berkow, 1994). The clinician’s job is to make sure everyone benefits and participates and no one dominates.

Couples therapy

Couples therapy involves two people in an intimate relationship (dating, partnered, engaged or married) who want to work through their difficulties. The main orientation is cognitive-behavioral (Rathus & Sanderson, 1999). The therapist helps the couple see how their backgrounds, beliefs and actions affect the relationship and teaches skills such as listening, arguing and expressing feelings. Some couples decide in therapy that they are too incompatible and separate; others come to decide whether to stay together. Psychologists Peter Pearson and Ellyn Bader compared working with high-conflict couples to “piloting a helicopter in a hurricane” (Weil, 2012).

Family therapy

Family therapy is a special form of group therapy with one or more families. A predominant orientation is the systems approach: the family is an organized system in which each member influences and is influenced by the others, and the goal is growth for each member and for the family as a whole (Minuchin, 1985). It is used when communication patterns cause conflict, or when one member’s problem, such as a parent’s depression, a teen’s eating disorder or a father’s alcohol dependence, affects everyone. Usually the nuclear family takes part, sometimes with others in the household such as a grandparent.

Boundaries and rules

Structural family therapy

Examines the family’s structure: who makes the rules, how decisions are made, who undermines whom. Parents who don’t back each other up on rules may have children who act out. The therapist helps the family communicate more effectively.

Specific problems, short term

Strategic family therapy

Targets specific problems that can be solved in a relatively short time. The therapist directs the sessions and designs a detailed plan for each member’s problem (Madanes, 1991).

Unit 16 · Section 16.4

Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders: A Special Case

Addiction is often treated as a chronic disease: the first use is a choice, but long-term use changes the brain, and relapse is common.

Learning objectives

  • Recognize the goal of substance-related and addictive disorders treatment
  • Discuss what makes for effective treatment
  • Describe how comorbid disorders are treated
Read Section 16.4 on OpenStax ↗

A chronic disease

The initial decision to use a substance is voluntary, but chronic use can permanently alter the prefrontal cortex, the brain area involved in decision making and judgment, so the person becomes driven to use (Muñoz-Cuevas et al., 2013). That helps explain why relapse, returning to drug or alcohol abuse after a period of improvement, is so common. OpenStax Figure 16.18 charts drug use by age group, and the caption of Figure 16.19 notes that substance use and abuse costs the United States over $600 billion a year (NIDA, 2012).

The goal of treatment

The goal is to help a person with addiction stop compulsive drug-seeking behaviors (NIDA, 2012). Like hypertension or diabetes, addiction often needs long-term treatment, usually behavioral therapy and/or medication, depending on the person. Specialized therapies exist for alcohol, cocaine and opioid disorders (McGovern & Carroll, 2003), and treatment is more cost-effective than incarceration or no treatment at all (NIDA, 2012).

What makes treatment effective

  • Duration: at least three months of treatment is generally needed for a positive outcome (Simpson, 1981; Simpson et al., 1982; NIDA, 2012), because addiction has psychological, physiological, behavioral and social sides.
  • Behavioral therapy motivates people to stay in treatment and teaches strategies for handling cravings and preventing relapse.
  • The whole person: treatment should address needs beyond drug use, such as communication, stress management, relationships, parenting, work and legal concerns (McGovern & Carroll, 2003; NIDA, 2012).
  • Group therapy is the most widespread modality (Weiss et al., 2004). People are more likely to stay sober in a group, which offers support, affiliation, identification and even confrontation (Center for Substance Abuse Treatment, 2005). Still, neither individual nor group therapy has been shown to be more effective (Weiss et al., 2004).
  • Family involvement for teens: the more parents take part, the greater the drop in teen substance use, and mothers who took part showed better mental health and more warmth toward their children (Bertrand et al., 2013).
  • Medication can help people detox safely after an overdose, prevent seizures and agitation during detox, prevent reuse and manage withdrawal. Detox can be difficult and dangerous, and some detox medications can be just as addictive.

Whatever the format, treatment aims for abstinence, or at least a significant reduction in use (McGovern & Carroll, 2003).

Comorbid disorders

A comorbid disorder means a person has two or more diagnoses, often a substance-related diagnosis plus another psychiatric diagnosis such as depression, bipolar disorder or schizophrenia. People in this group are sometimes called MICA (mentally ill and chemically addicted); their problems are often chronic and expensive to treat, with limited success. People who use illicit drugs or misuse prescription drugs are twice as likely as the general population to have a mood or anxiety disorder, and the link runs both ways:

The best approach is thought to be treating all the disorders at the same time (NIDA, 2012), with behavioral therapies and often psychotropic medication alongside psychotherapy. For example, bupropion (Wellbutrin, Zyban), approved for depression and nicotine dependence, may also reduce craving for and use of methamphetamine (NIDA, 2011). More research is needed on combined treatment for people with comorbid disorders.

The Sociocultural Model and Therapy Utilization

A person’s culture shapes how their symptoms should be understood, which treatments will fit, and whether they seek help at all.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how the sociocultural model is used in therapy
  • Discuss barriers to mental health services among ethnic minorities
Read Section 16.5 on OpenStax ↗

Culture in therapy

The sociocultural perspective looks at a person’s behaviors and symptoms in the context of their culture and background. José, an 18-year-old Hispanic man from a traditional family, comes to treatment for depression. He reveals that he is gay, is nervous about telling his family, and was raised in a religion that teaches that being gay is wrong. His background shapes his distress and how his family may react, so his therapist needs to take it into account (OpenStax Figure 16.20 asks how your own cultural and religious beliefs affect your attitude toward treatment).

This requires cultural competence: a therapist’s understanding of and attention to issues of race, culture and ethnicity, and strategies for groups for whom Eurocentric therapies fit poorly (Sue, 2004). A therapist focused on individual decision making may not help a Chinese client who takes a collectivist approach to solving problems.

Sue, 2001

Multicultural counseling and therapy

Uses methods and goals consistent with the client’s life experience and cultural values; recognizes individual, group and universal dimensions of identity; uses both universal and culture-specific strategies; balances individualism and collectivism in assessment, diagnosis and treatment.

Stewart et al., 2012

Sociocultural therapy

Builds cultural and social norms into treatment from the start. The therapist learns the client’s cultural patterns to plan individualized treatment, in individual, group, family or couples formats.

Barriers to treatment

Ethnic minorities use mental health services less than White, middle-class Americans (Alegría et al., 2008; Richman, Kohn-Wood, & Williams, 2007). Ethnic minorities and people of low socioeconomic status cite no insurance, no transportation and no time (Thomas & Snowden, 2002), yet the gap remains even when income, insurance and access are comparable (Richman et al., 2007).

An example: a study of thousands of women (Marques et al., 2011) found anorexia equally common across races, and bulimia more common among Hispanic and African American women than non-Hispanic White women, yet Hispanic and African American women with eating disorders sought and engaged in treatment far less. Suggested reasons included a lack of bilingual treatment, stigma, fear of not being understood, family privacy and a lack of education about eating disorders.

Attitudes

Doubts about therapy

A King’s College London study (Clement et al., 2014) found people avoid treatment out of self-sufficiency, not seeing a need, doubting therapy works, worries about confidentiality, and stigma and shame.

Fear and preference

Other sources of help

African Americans with depression were less willing to seek treatment out of fear of psychiatric hospitalization and of treatment itself (Sussman, Robins, & Earls, 1987). Many prefer self-reliance or spiritual practice (Snowden, 2001; Belgrave & Allison, 2010), and the Black church often runs prevention and treatment-like programs (Blank et al., 2002).

Stigma and language

A second stigma

Groups already facing discrimination may see mental illness as an added stigma (Gary, 2005; Townes et al., 2009; Scott et al., 2011). Language matters too: Korean Americans in the study below had no Korean-speaking mental health professionals nearby.

As the population grows more diverse, therapists need to build cultural competence (Ahmed, Wilson, Henriksen, & Jones, 2011) and approach each client within their own cultural context (Sue & Sue, 2007).

Dig deeper: supporting mental health treatment

About 1 in 6 U.S. children and 1 in 5 U.S. adults have a mental health disorder, but fewer than half get professional help (Whitney & Peterson, 2019). Stigma is a main reason. In interviews with more than 1,300 U.S. adults, researchers from Indiana University, the University of Virginia and Columbia University found beliefs that children with depression are prone to violence and that children in treatment are more likely to be rejected by peers. The study’s author, Bernice Pescosolido, calls these misconceptions and warns that stigma can devastate emotional and social well-being. Public figures such as Naomi Osaka, Simone Biles, Michael Phelps, Kevin Love, Dak Prescott, Adele, Bruce Springsteen, Ariana Grande, Big Sean and Bebe Rexha have helped by speaking openly about their own mental health.

The National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI) suggests five ways to help:

  1. Talk and listen openly about mental health.
  2. Avoid assumptions, generalizations and judgments.
  3. Choose language that reduces stigma and bias.
  4. Encourage treating mental illness the same as physical illness.
  5. Encourage people to seek help: a doctor, a counselor or a support group.

Finding help

If you or someone you know is struggling, these free, confidential U.S. services can help (outside sources, linked in Resources):

  • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline: call or text 988, 24 hours a day
  • SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-HELP (4357), 24 hours a day, every day, in English and Spanish, for treatment referrals and information on mental health and substance use
  • NAMI (nami.org): information and support groups for people with mental illness and their families

16.1 Treatment: Past and Present

asylum
institution built specifically to house people with psychological disorders
deinstitutionalization
closing large asylums so people stay in their communities and are treated locally
involuntary treatment
therapy that is not the person’s choice, such as court-mandated counseling
voluntary treatment
therapy a person chooses to attend to get relief from symptoms

16.2 Types of Treatment

aversive conditioning
counterconditioning that pairs an unpleasant stimulus with an unwanted behavior
behavior therapy
using principles of learning to change undesirable behaviors
biomedical therapy
treatment with medication and/or medical procedures
cognitive therapy
psychotherapy focused on how thoughts lead to distress
cognitive-behavioral therapy
psychotherapy that changes cognitive distortions and self-defeating behaviors
counterconditioning
learning a new response to a stimulus that used to trigger an undesirable behavior
dream analysis
interpreting dreams to reveal unconscious wishes or conflicts
electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)
electrical current induces seizures to relieve severe depression
exposure therapy
gradually facing a feared object or situation until the fear fades
free association
psychoanalytic technique: the patient says whatever comes to mind
humanistic therapy
therapy aimed at greater self-awareness and self-acceptance
nondirective therapy
the therapist gives no advice or interpretations, but helps the person explore conflicts and feelings
play therapy
therapy, mostly for children, that uses toys to work through problems
psychoanalysis
Freud’s therapy: free association, dream analysis and transference to uncover repressed feelings
psychotherapy
psychological treatment to overcome personal problems or attain personal growth
rational emotive therapy (RET)
Albert Ellis’s therapy, one of the first forms of CBT
Rogerian (client-centered therapy)
Carl Rogers’s nondirective humanistic therapy, built on unconditional positive regard
systematic desensitization
gradual exposure up an anxiety hierarchy, paired with relaxation; for phobias and anxiety
token economy
controlled setting where desired behaviors earn tokens traded for items or privileges
transference
the patient transfers feelings about other relationships onto the analyst
unconditional positive regard
accepting a person fully, whatever they say or do
virtual reality exposure therapy
exposure therapy using a simulation instead of the real situation

16.3 Treatment Modalities

confidentiality
a therapist cannot share a client’s communications unless the law requires or allows it
couples therapy
therapy for two people in an intimate relationship working through difficulties
family therapy
group therapy with one or more families, treated as a system
group therapy
a clinician meets with a small group who share a common issue
individual therapy
one-on-one treatment between a client and a clinician
intake
first meeting: the therapist gathers information and addresses immediate needs
strategic family therapy
the therapist directs sessions and plans short-term solutions to each member’s specific problem
structural family therapy
examines family boundaries and structure: rules, roles and decisions

16.4 Substance-Related Disorders

comorbid disorder
two or more diagnoses, often a substance-related one plus another psychiatric one
relapse
returning to drug or alcohol use after a period of improvement

16.5 The Sociocultural Model

cultural competence
a therapist’s understanding of and attention to race, culture and ethnicity in treatment
