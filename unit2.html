Unit 2 · Overview

Psychological Research

Why psychology depends on evidence, the methods psychologists use to collect it, how to read correlations and experiments correctly, and the ethical rules that protect research participants.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening question

Does watching violence on television make people more aggressive, or could it drain aggression out of them? How is a child shaped by the media they see? A psychologist interested in violent images and behavior would ask exactly these questions.

Worries about new technology are ancient. Socrates feared that writing, new in his day, would weaken memory because people could lean on written records. Today the same questions surround screen time, GPS and smart cars, home assistants and doorbell cameras, and people often hold strong, opposite opinions about them. Psychological research offers a way to settle such questions with evidence everyone can agree on rather than with opinion.

Unit 2 · Section 2.1

Why Is Research Important?

Research is what separates fact from opinion. It replaces intuition, authority and luck with evidence, and that evidence guides both public policy and personal decisions.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how scientific research addresses questions about behavior
  • Discuss how scientific research guides public policy
  • Appreciate how scientific research can be important in making personal decisions

Evidence over intuition

Without research we would rely only on intuition, other people’s authority and blind luck. History shows how wrong confident beliefs can be: people were once certain that the sun circled a flat earth, that the continents never moved and that mental illness came from possession. Some ancestors even practiced trephination, boring a hole in the skull so evil spirits could escape. Systematic research strips away such preconceptions and superstitions.

Psychologists study behavior and the cognitive (mental) and physiological (body) processes beneath it. Unlike intuition or personal experience, science requires evidence for every claim. Scientific knowledge is empirical: grounded in objective, tangible evidence that can be observed again and again, no matter who observes it.

Behavior is observable; the mind is not. We can see someone cry, but not whether they are sad, in pain or happy. Asking “Why are you crying?” sometimes works, but people may be uncomfortable, unwilling or unable to answer honestly (an infant cannot explain at all). In those cases psychologists must find creative ways to study behavior.

Using research information

Consensus takes time

An internet search on almost any topic turns up contradictory studies; that is the scientific community still working toward consensus. Technology in education is an example. A smartphone app for surgery residents raised engagement and test scores (Shaw & Tan, 2015), yet technology use among undergraduates hurt sleep, communication and time management (Massimini & Peterson, 2009). Until enough research accumulates, there is no clear answer.

Healthy skepticism

Meanwhile, think critically. Many ads and websites claim “scientific evidence” for what is really the view of a few people selling a product or a perspective. Examine any claim from several angles:

  • What is the claimant’s expertise?
  • What would they gain if the claim is valid?
  • Does the evidence justify the claim?
  • What do other researchers think?

Research and public policy

Imagine you are a new governor deciding whether to keep funding early intervention programs, which offer services to children from low-income backgrounds, with special needs or facing other disadvantages (Blann, 2005). Research shows such programs are generally effective (Neil & Christensen, 2009; Peters-Scheffer, Didden, Korzilius, & Sturmey, 2011). Not all are equally effective and short-term effects are stronger, but many bring long-term benefits (Barnett, 2011). A good steward of tax money would ask which programs work best, why, and which produce the best outcomes.

Research and personal decisions

Your sister Maria worries that her two-year-old, Umberto, speaks less and less clearly than other children. His pediatrician recommends a speech pathologist and sees differences in his jaw and facial muscles rather than signs of a developmental disorder. Searching online, you face blog posts, top-ten lists, provider ads and advocacy groups. Which are based on research?

Fact or opinion?

A fact is an observable reality; in science, facts can be established only with evidence from empirical research. An opinion is a personal judgment, conclusion or attitude that may or may not be accurate.

The scientific method: deductive and inductive reasoning

Science advances through the scientific method: ideas (theories and hypotheses) are tested against empirical observations, which in turn suggest new ideas. The process is circular and uses two inseparable kinds of reasoning, like inhaling and exhaling.

  • Deductive reasoning starts with a generalization (a hypothesis) and reaches logical conclusions about the world. If the premise is right, the conclusion is right; a wrong premise can still produce a logical but wrong conclusion. Scientists use it to test hypotheses empirically.
  • Inductive reasoning builds broad generalizations from observations. Its conclusions may or may not be correct, however many observations support them. Scientists use it to form theories.
Deductive example

All living things need energy to survive. Ducks are living things. Therefore ducks need energy to survive.

Inductive example

Apples, bananas and oranges grow on trees, so all fruit must grow on trees. Strawberries, blueberries and kiwi prove this wrong.

Inductive reasoning produces theories; theories generate hypotheses; hypotheses are tested deductively. Approaches differ in emphasis: case studies lean inductive, gathering many observations and looking for patterns, while experimental research leans deductive.

Theories, hypotheses and falsifiability

A theory is a well-developed set of ideas that proposes an explanation for observed phenomena. Theories are usually too complex to test all at once, so researchers test specific parts of them with hypotheses. A hypothesis is a testable prediction of how the world will behave if an idea is correct, often worded as an if-then statement (if I study all night, I will pass the test). It bridges the realm of ideas and the real world.

Example: the James-Lange theory of emotion

This theory says emotional experience depends on the physiological arousal of the emotional state. Meet an aggressive snake on your doorstep and your heart races and stomach churns; according to the theory, those changes produce your fear. A hypothesis follows: someone unaware of that arousal will not feel fear.

Falsifiability

A scientific hypothesis must be falsifiable: capable of being shown incorrect. A major criticism of Freud is that many of his ideas are not falsifiable; no observation could disprove the id, ego and superego. His theories are still taught for their historical importance to personality psychology and psychotherapy.

The James-Lange theory is falsifiable. People with serious spinal injuries cannot feel many bodily changes of emotion, so their emotions can be compared with those of people who can. They turn out to feel emotion, though perhaps less intensely (Chwalisz, Diener, & Gallagher, 1988). Because science depends on falsifiability, information accepted by the scientific community has usually been tested repeatedly.

Unit 2 · Section 2.2

Approaches to Research

Each research method trades depth, realism, sample size, cost and control against one another, and suits only some questions.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the different research methods used by psychologists
  • Discuss the strengths and weaknesses of case studies, naturalistic observation, surveys, and archival research
  • Compare longitudinal and cross-sectional approaches to research
  • Compare and contrast correlation and causation

Methods at a glance

Methods range from pure observation, through simple questions and in-depth interviews, to well-controlled experiments. Case studies, naturalistic observation, surveys and archival research are all correlational: they reveal relationships between variables but cannot show cause and effect. Only an experiment can. Experiments give great control, but they are often run in artificial settings, which raises questions about how findings apply in the real world, and many questions cannot be studied experimentally for ethical reasons.

Clinical or case studies

In 2011 the New York Times profiled Krista and Tatiana Hogan, Canadian twins conjoined at the head. Evidence suggests their brains connect at the thalamus, a major sensory relay center through which most incoming sensory information passes before reaching the cerebral cortex. One twin might therefore feel the other’s sensations, for example smiling at a show only her sister is watching. Because the condition is so rare, scientists will likely follow them for life if the family agrees (Dominus, 2011). They share some sensory experiences and motor control yet remain two distinct individuals (Egnor, 2017).

A clinical or case study is observational research focusing on one person or just a few; some scientists study only 10–20 people in their whole career. The depth of insight is unmatched. The same strength is the weakness: people chosen for a rare characteristic are not like most people, so it is hard to generalize, that is, to apply the findings to larger segments of society.

Naturalistic observation

People change their behavior when they know they are watched. Ask a class who always washes their hands after using the restroom and nearly everyone raises a hand. To learn the truth, send an observer who blends in (say, someone at the sink pretending to put in contact lenses) to record what really happens. This is naturalistic observation: observing behavior in its natural setting. Think of how differently you drive on an empty highway compared with a police car behind you.

  • Preschool playground. To study peer exclusion, Suzanne Fanger and colleagues at the University of Texas fitted a few children with wireless microphones, which the children soon forgot, and took notes from a distance. The children were also used to observers (Fanger, Frankel, & Hazen, 2012).
  • Animals in the field. Researchers keep their distance to study social hierarchies from ground squirrels to gorillas. Jane Goodall observed chimpanzees in Africa for nearly five decades; some scientists criticized her for naming the chimps instead of numbering them, saying it undermined the emotional detachment objectivity needs (McKie, 2010).

Strengths and weaknesses

The greatest benefit is validity (accuracy): behaving normally in a natural setting gives high ecological validity, or realism, so findings generalize better to real life. Reality TV is not naturalistic observation, because camera crews and on-camera confessionals violate inconspicuous observation. The downside: the researcher has no control over when, or whether, the behavior happens (no one enters the restroom; the gorillas migrate overnight), and the work demands time, money and luck.

Structured observation

In structured observation, people are observed while doing set tasks. Mary Ainsworth’s Strange Situation evaluates infant–caregiver attachment: in a room full of toys, a stranger enters, the caregiver leaves and then returns. The infant’s behavior on reunion is the most telling.

Observer bias

Observer bias occurs when observers, often closely involved in the project, unconsciously skew observations toward their expectations. Safeguards: clear criteria for which behaviors are recorded and how they are classified, and comparing several observers’ records of the same event to test inter-rater reliability, the consistency of observations across observers.

Surveys

A survey is a list of questions for participants, delivered on paper, electronically or verbally. Surveys are quick and easy to administer, so they reach large numbers of people. Researchers study a sample, a subset of individuals selected from the population (the overall group they care about), and try to generalize from one to the other.

Measures of central tendency

Analysis usually starts with three summaries of a typical response: the mode (most frequent response), the median (middle of the data set) and the mean (arithmetic average). The mean is most useful for further analyses but is very sensitive to outliers.

Strengths and weaknesses

Compared with case studies, a larger and more diverse sample better reflects the population, so survey results generalize with more certainty. The cost is depth: far less information per person. People also give inaccurate answers. They may lie, misremember or answer to look good, for example by under-reporting how much alcohol they drink.

Research example: subtle prejudice

Jenkins, Ruppel, Kizer, Yehl and Griffin (2012) asked whether negative attitudes toward Arab-Americans persisted nearly a decade after the September 11, 2001 attacks. In one study, 140 participants answered a 10-question survey with direct questions about prejudice toward several ethnic groups and indirect questions about how likely they were to interact with members of each group. Participants reported no prejudice toward any group, yet indicated less willingness to interact with Arab-Americans than with other groups, suggesting subtle prejudice.

Archival research

Archival research answers questions using existing records or data sets, without interacting with a single participant. For example, a researcher could pull ten years of college records (time to degree, course loads, grades, extracurricular activities) to learn who is most likely to graduate and which students are at risk.

  • Cheaper: no direct contact with participants, so far less time and money.
  • No control over the original data: questions must be tailored to what was recorded.
  • No guaranteed consistency between sources, which can make comparing data sets problematic.

Longitudinal and cross-sectional research

To see how people change over time, researchers choose between two designs.

  • Longitudinal research tests the same group repeatedly over an extended period, such as surveying the same people about their diet at 20, 30 and 40.
  • Cross-sectional research compares different segments of the population at the same time, such as 20-, 30- and 40-year-olds surveyed now.

The cohort problem

Cross-sectional research is quicker, but its age groups are different cohorts (generations) whose social and cultural experiences differ for reasons unrelated to age. Younger people are generally more supportive of same-sex marriage than older people (Jones, 2013). Do people become less open as they age, or did older people grow up in a different social climate? Longitudinal research follows the same individuals, so cohort differences matter far less.

Longitudinal studies of disease

Studies of disease risk often follow tens of thousands of people for decades, so findings generalize confidently. The American Cancer Society’s Cancer Prevention Study-3 (CPS-3) surveys participants about their lives and family histories on entry and again every few years, tracking hundreds of thousands of people over 20 years to see who develops cancer. Earlier American Cancer Society longitudinal studies gave some of the first scientific evidence linking smoking and cancer.

Limitations of longitudinal research

It demands huge investments of time and money, and results take years or decades to arrive. Participants move, marry and change names, fall ill, die or simply quit, so attrition (loss of participants who drop out) is high and grows over time. Researchers recruit extra participants and keep checking that the sample still represents the population.

Unit 2 · Section 2.3

Analyzing Findings

Correlations describe how strongly and in which direction variables move together, but only a well-designed experiment can show that one causes the other.

Learning objectives

  • Explain what a correlation coefficient tells us about the relationship between variables
  • Recognize that correlation does not indicate a cause-and-effect relationship between variables
  • Discuss our tendency to look for relationships between variables that do not really exist
  • Explain random sampling and assignment of participants into experimental and control groups
  • Discuss how experimenter or participant bias could affect the results of an experiment
  • Identify independent and dependent variables

Correlational research

As ice cream sales rise, so does crime (e.g., Harper, 2013), but it would be foolish to conclude that one causes the other. A correlation is a relationship between two or more variables: as one changes, so does the other. It does not necessarily mean cause and effect.

A correlation coefficient, written r, is a number from −1 to +1 describing that relationship:

  • The number gives strength. The closer it is to 1 (positive or negative), the stronger and more predictable the relationship; 0.9 is far stronger than 0.3. Unrelated variables have r = 0.
  • The sign gives direction. In a positive correlation both variables rise or fall together (ice cream and crime, height and weight, age and wrinkles). In a negative correlation one rises as the other falls (daytime tiredness and hours slept the night before).

A real example: student researchers at the University of Minnesota found a weak negative correlation (r = −0.29) between how many days a week students slept under 5 hours and their GPA (Lowry, Dean, & Manders, 2010). Note that a negative correlation is not the same as no correlation; hours of sleep and shoe size are probably not correlated at all.

Correlations also predict. A university admissions committee could correlate current students’ college GPAs with their SAT or ACT scores, then use the strongest correlations to predict how applicants will do.

Correlation does not indicate causation

Variables sometimes correlate because one causes the other, but a confounding variable, an outside factor, may be driving both. In the ice cream example it is temperature: warm weather sends people outdoors, where they interact and sometimes annoy one another and commit crimes, and it also makes a cool treat appealing.

Even without an obvious confound, a correlation does not license a causal claim. Smoking seems obviously to cause cancer, yet correlational research alone would not justify saying so. Ads and news stories often blur this line: people who eat certain breakfast cereals may have lower heart disease risk (Anderson, Hanna, Peng, & Kryscio, 2000), but does the cereal make them healthier, or is something else going on? A cause-and-effect relationship, where changes in one variable cause changes in another, can be shown only by an experiment, which controls for alternative explanations.

Illusory correlations

An illusory correlation (false correlation) is believing two things are related when they are not, especially after unsystematic observation. Many people insist the full moon makes people act strangely: the moon moves the tides, and our bodies are largely water. Yet a meta-analysis of nearly 40 studies found no relationship between the moon and behavior (Rotton & Kelly, 1985). Odd behavior occurs at the same rate throughout the lunar cycle; we just notice it more at the full moon.

Why do we believe them?

  • We accept what we read or hear without checking.
  • Confirmation bias: we look for evidence supporting a hunch and ignore evidence against it.
  • We rely on whatever information comes most easily to mind, however limited.

The consequences can be serious: illusory correlations that wrongly link behaviors to particular groups help form prejudice, which can lead to discrimination (Fiedler, 2004).

Designing an experiment

In everyday speech, “experimenting” means trying something new, like a hairstyle. In science an experiment has precise design requirements, and it is the only way to establish cause and effect. It starts with a specific hypothesis, formed from direct observation or a review of past research. Example hypothesis: technology in the classroom decreases learning, perhaps because you noticed that classmates who take notes on laptops score lower than those who write by hand. Personal observations and anecdotes inspire hypotheses but cannot rigorously test them.

Experimental and control groups

The basic design has two groups that are identical except for the experimental manipulation:

  • The experimental group receives the manipulation: here, learning algebra from a computer program.
  • The control group does not: it learns algebra from a teacher in a traditional classroom, and is otherwise treated the same.

Because the manipulation is the only difference, any difference in results can be credited to it rather than to chance.

Operational definitions

Researchers must operationalize their variables. An operational definition precisely describes how each variable is measured or manipulated; learning might be defined as performance on a test of the material taught, or as how well participants summarize it. Anyone reading the study then knows exactly what “learning” meant, can interpret the data and can repeat the experiment. Here, participants spend 45 minutes learning algebra one way or the other and then take a test.

Experimenter bias, blind studies and the placebo effect

Experimenter bias is the possibility that researchers’ expectations skew the results. Researchers have a stake in their hypotheses; a scorer who knows a student’s group might judge ambiguous answers (sloppy handwriting, small calculation slips) differently. Keeping scorers unaware of group membership protects against this.

Participants’ expectations matter too. The placebo effect occurs when people’s expectations or beliefs influence or determine their experience: simply expecting something can make it happen. Testing a new antidepressant, a participant who takes a pill might feel better just because they expect to. So the control group gets a placebo (here a sugar pill), and everyone receives a pill.

Independent and dependent variables

Experiments ask whether changes in one variable (something that can change) cause changes in another.

  • The independent variable is manipulated or controlled by the experimenter. In a well-designed study it is the only important difference between the experimental and control groups. Here: the type of instruction (computer program or in-person teacher).
  • The dependent variable is what the researcher measures to see the independent variable’s effect. Here: the learning participants show on the test.

The dependent variable depends on the independent variable. A useful check: “What effect does the independent variable have on the dependent variable?”

Selecting and assigning participants

Participants are the subjects of psychological research; they actively take part. Historically most psychology research used college students (Sears, 1986; Arnett, 2008), who tend to be younger, more educated, more liberal and less diverse than the general population, which makes findings harder to generalize.

Random sampling

Populations are usually too large to test, so researchers draw a sample. Ideally it is a random sample, in which every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected. A large enough random sample is representative: its percentages of sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic level and other relevant traits are close to the population’s. In the example, the population is algebra students in one city, spanning income brackets, family situations, races, ethnicities, religions and neighborhoods; working with local schools, researchers randomly select about 200.

Random assignment

Next, random assignment splits the sample so every participant has an equal chance of landing in either group; statistical software can do it. With large enough samples it makes systematic differences between groups (say, one group all male or of one religion) unlikely. Otherwise we could not tell whether a difference existed beforehand or was caused by the independent variable.

Limits of experiments and interpreting results

Issues to consider

  • Some variables cannot be manipulated. To study whether sex affects spatial memory, you can compare males and females but cannot assign anyone’s sex. Such research is quasi-experimental, and it cannot support cause-and-effect claims.
  • Ethical limits. No one could test whether childhood abuse lowers adult self-esteem by randomly assigning children to be abused.

Statistical analysis

A statistical analysis determines how likely it is that a difference between groups is due to chance (and so not meaningful). If people taking a placebo improve as much as people taking a nutritional supplement, the supplement is not effective. Psychologists generally call a difference statistically significant when there is less than a 5% chance of observing it if the groups did not truly differ, limiting “false positive” claims to 5% or less.

The great strength of experiments: random selection, random assignment and a design that limits experimenter bias and participant expectancy create groups that are alike in composition and treatment, so a significant difference can be attributed to the independent variable. If violent television produced more violent behavior than nonviolent television in such an experiment, we could say it causes the increase.

Reporting research: peer review and replication

The APA publishes a manual on writing papers for scientific journals. Unlike magazines such as Psychology Today, written for a general audience, journals publish peer-reviewed journal articles for professionals and researchers.

Peer reviewers check for a strong rationale, a clear description of the methods, evidence of ethical conduct, flaws in design, methods or statistics, conclusions that fit the observations, and how much the work advances the field (which limits needless duplication). The journal editor weighs their feedback and decides.

Peer review is quality control: weak studies are weeded out, good ones improved, and methods described clearly enough to replicate, meaning to repeat the study with different samples to test its reliability. Successful replications make scientists more likely to adopt a finding; repeated failures cast doubt on it.

The replication crisis

Concern has grown about a replication crisis across sciences, including psychology: some well-known studies have failed to replicate (Shrout & Rodgers, 2018). Nobel Prize-winning scientist Frances Arnold retracted a paper in 2020 because she had difficulty replicating her results. Some argue the crisis is improving science by pushing researchers to work more openly and collaboratively (Aschwanden, 2018).

Dig deeper: the vaccine–autism myth

Several peer-reviewed publications claimed routine childhood vaccines cause autism. Large-scale epidemiological research has since shown they do not, and that vaccinating is much safer than not; several original studies were retracted. A retraction rescinds published work whose data are in question because of falsification, fabrication or serious design problems, and can be started by the lead researcher, collaborators, the employing institution or the journal’s editorial board. Here the lead researcher had a financial conflict of interest in linking vaccines and autism (Offit, 2008). The heavy media coverage made many parents hesitant to vaccinate. Between January and October 2019 the U.S. had 22 measles outbreaks and more than 1,000 cases (Patel et al., 2019), likely linked to anti-vaccination movements rooted in the debunked research. The text recommends Paul Offit’s book Autism’s False Prophets for the full story.

Reliability and validity

Reliability is the ability to produce a result consistently: research instruments should collect data in consistent, reproducible ways. Validity is the extent to which an instrument accurately measures what it is supposed to measure. Consistency alone is not enough.

Everyday connection: how valid are the SAT and ACT?

College Board research suggests SAT scores have high predictive validity for first-year college GPA (Kobrin, Patterson, Shaw, Mattern, & Barbuti, 2008). Critics argue the tests are biased against students from historically marginalized populations (Santelices & Wilson, 2010), and one analysis suggests the SAT’s predictive validity may be overestimated by as much as 150% (Rothstein, 2004). Many institutions began considering de-emphasizing scores (Rimer, 2008), cheating scandals added scrutiny, and the text reports that by March 2019 more than 1,000 institutions had relaxed or dropped SAT/ACT requirements (Strauss, 2019).

Unit 2 · Section 2.4

Ethics

Good research respects human dignity and safety. Committees review every study involving people or animals, and participants must consent, may withdraw and must be told the truth afterward.

Learning objectives

  • Discuss how research involving human subjects is regulated
  • Summarize the processes of informed consent and debriefing
  • Explain how research involving animal subjects is regulated

Research involving human participants

Studies with human participants follow extensive, strict guidelines to prevent harm. Any institution receiving federal support for such research must have access to an Institutional Review Board (IRB): a committee often made up of administrators, scientists and community members that reviews proposals for human research. IRB approval is generally required before a study can go ahead.

Informed consent

Each participant must sign an informed consent form before taking part. It describes in writing:

  • what participants can expect, including potential risks and implications;
  • that participation is completely voluntary and can be stopped at any time without penalty;
  • that all data collected will remain confidential.

For participants under 18, parents or legal guardians sign.

Deception and debriefing

Sometimes knowing the exact research question would change participants’ responses, as in a study of how a person’s attire affects our opinion of them. Researchers may then use deception, purposely misleading participants to protect the experiment’s integrity, but never to a harmful degree. Deception requires a full debriefing at the end: complete, honest information about the study’s purpose, how the data will be used, why deception was needed and how to learn more.

  • Informed consent
  • The study (deception only if needed)
  • Debriefing

Dig deeper: the Tuskegee syphilis study

These guidelines were not always applied. In 1932 the U.S. Public Health Service recruited rural Black men from Tuskegee, Alabama, to study syphilis in Black men. In exchange for free medical care, meals and burial insurance, 600 men took part. A little over half tested positive and formed the experimental group; the rest were the control group. Because the researchers could not randomly assign men to groups, it was a quasi-experiment. The men with syphilis were never told they had it.

No treatment existed when the study began, but by 1947 penicillin was recognized as effective. The men never received it, and those who stayed in the study were not allowed to seek treatment elsewhere. Over 40 years many unknowingly infected their wives (and, through them, children) and died untreated. The study ended in 1972 when the national press discovered it (Tuskegee University, n.d.).

The text asks you to consider: Why is this study unethical? How were the men and their families harmed?

Beyond the textbook: the CDC’s timeline gives the split as 399 men with syphilis and 201 without, and dates penicillin as the treatment of choice by 1943 (CDC). The National Research Act, signed July 12, 1974, created the commission that wrote the Belmont Report, whose three principles (respect for persons, beneficence and justice) still guide IRBs (HHS).

Research involving animal subjects

Many psychologists study animals, usually rodents or birds; the APA estimates these make up 90% of animal research in psychology. Because many basic processes in these animals are similar enough to those in humans, they can substitute for research that would be unethical with people.

Animal researchers still face ethical obligations: experiments must be designed to minimize pain and distress. Proposals are reviewed by an Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), which ensures humane treatment, inspects all animal facilities twice a year, and must approve every animal project before it can proceed.

Key terms 50 terms

2.1 Why Is Research Important?

empirical
grounded in objective, tangible evidence that anyone can observe again and again
fact
objective, verifiable observation established with evidence from empirical research
opinion
personal judgment, conclusion or attitude that may or may not be accurate
deductive reasoning
predicting results from a general premise
inductive reasoning
drawing conclusions from observations
theory
well-developed set of ideas that explains observed phenomena
hypothesis
testable prediction, often an if-then statement, of what happens if an idea is correct
falsifiable
able to be disproven by experimental results

2.2 Approaches to Research

clinical or case study
observational study of one or a few people
generalize
inferring that a sample’s results apply to the larger population
naturalistic observation
observing behavior in its natural setting
observer bias
observations skewed to fit the observer’s expectations
inter-rater reliability
agreement among observers on how they record and classify the same event
survey
list of questions (paper, electronic or spoken) for collecting data from many people
sample
subset of individuals selected from the population
population
the overall group of individuals the researchers are interested in
archival research
using past records or data sets to answer questions or find patterns
longitudinal research
measuring the same group repeatedly over a long period
cross-sectional research
comparing different segments of a population at one time
attrition
loss of participants as some drop out of a study over time

2.3 Analyzing Findings

correlation
relationship between variables: as one changes, so does the other
correlation coefficient
number from −1 to +1, written r, giving a relationship’s strength and direction
positive correlation
both variables change in the same direction
negative correlation
variables change in opposite directions; not the same as no correlation
confounding variable
outside factor that changes both variables, falsely suggesting one causes the other
cause-and-effect relationship
changes in one variable cause changes in the other; shown only by experiments
illusory correlation
seeing a relationship between two things when none exists
confirmation bias
tendency to ignore evidence that disproves our ideas or beliefs
experimental group
group that receives the experimental manipulation
control group
comparison group, treated the same except for the manipulation, that controls for chance factors
operational definition
description of how variables will be measured and manipulated
experimenter bias
researcher expectations skewing a study’s results
single-blind study
the researcher knows who is in each group; participants do not
double-blind study
neither researchers nor participants know the group assignments
placebo effect
expectations or beliefs influencing or determining a person’s experience
independent variable
variable the experimenter manipulates; the only important difference between groups
dependent variable
variable measured to see how much effect the independent variable had
participants
subjects of psychological research
random sample
subset in which every population member has an equal chance of being selected
random assignment
every participant has an equal chance of being placed in either group
statistical analysis
determines how likely a difference between groups is due to chance
peer-reviewed journal article
article checked by expert scientists, usually anonymously, before publication
replicate
repeat a study with different samples to test its reliability
reliability
consistency and reproducibility of a result
validity
accuracy of a result in measuring what it is designed to measure

2.4 Ethics

Institutional Review Board (IRB)
committee of administrators, scientists and community members that reviews research with human participants
informed consent
explaining what to expect, the risks and the implications of a study, then getting a participant’s agreement
deception
purposely misleading participants to protect the experiment’s integrity
debriefing
after a study that used deception, telling participants the complete truth about it
Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC)
committee of administrators, scientists, veterinarians and community members that reviews animal research
