Unit 3 · Overview

Biopsychology

How genes shape body and behavior, how neurons send signals, how the nervous system and brain are organized, and how the endocrine system works alongside them.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening question

Take a device apart and you find that its inner workings look nothing like its user interface. Turning up the volume on a phone, we think about the sound, not the microchips and circuits. The human body works the same way: its inner workings are often distinct from how they show on the outside. Psychologists look for the connection between the two, for example how the firings of millions of neurons become a thought.

This chapter covers the biological mechanisms that underlie behavior. These physiological and anatomical foundations are the basis for many areas of psychology. The chapter opens with three kinds of brain scan (PET, CT and fMRI), each giving insight into a different aspect of how the brain works; you will meet all three in Section 3.4.

Unit 3 · Section 3.1

Human Genetics

We share the same biological parts, yet each of us is unique. Genetics helps explain why, and why genes alone never tell the whole story.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the basic principles of the theory of evolution by natural selection
  • Describe the differences between genotype and phenotype
  • Discuss how gene-environment interactions are critical for expression of physical and psychological characteristics

Sickle-cell anemia and natural selection

Psychologists study genetics to understand the biological factors behind behavior. Why do two people with the same disease have different outcomes? How are genetic diseases passed down families? Do disorders such as depression or schizophrenia have genetic components?

Sickle-cell anemia is a genetic condition in which normally round red blood cells take on a crescent shape. Sickle-shaped cells can clog blood vessels and block blood flow, causing high fever, severe pain, swelling and tissue damage. Many people with the disorder die young, yet the sickle-cell gene stays relatively common among people of African descent. Why?

Luwi and Sena

Two sisters live in rural Zambia. Luwi is a carrier: she has one copy of the sickle-cell gene, so she has symptoms only when severely dehydrated or deprived of oxygen. Carriers are thought to be immune to malaria, because changes in their blood chemistry and immune functioning stop the parasite (Gong, Parikh, Rosenthal, & Greenhouse, 2013). Sena does not carry the gene. Both are bitten by malaria-carrying mosquitoes; Sena dies two weeks later, while Luwi survives and may pass the mutation to her children. Two copies of the gene (full sickle-cell anemia) give no malaria protection.

Where malaria is rare, as in the United States, the gene benefits nobody: carriers get minor health problems and people with two copies get a severe disease. Where malaria is common, carriers gain protection. This fits Charles Darwin’s theory of evolution by natural selection (proposed in On the Origin of Species, 1859): organisms better suited to their environment survive and reproduce, while poorly suited ones die off. Whether a trait is adaptive depends on the environment.

Dig deeper: two perspectives on genetics and behavior

Both fields agree that genes code for physical traits and also contribute to patterns of cognition and behavior. They differ in what they explain.

Evolutionary psychology

How universal patterns of behavior and cognition evolved over millions of years, because they helped individuals reproduce. Topics include fear response, food preferences, mate selection and cooperation (Confer et al., 2010).

Behavioral genetics

How individual differences arise in the present from genes interacting with the environment. Uses twin studies (identical vs fraternal twins) and adoption studies (biological vs adopted relatives).

Genetic variation: chromosomes, genes and alleles

Genetic variation, the genetic difference between individuals, is what lets a species adapt to its environment. In humans it begins with an egg, about 100 million sperm, and fertilization. Roughly once a month an active ovary releases an egg; a sperm may fertilize it on its way through the fallopian tubes to the uterus.

  • The egg and the sperm each contain 23 chromosomes: long strings of genetic material called deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), a helix-shaped molecule made of nucleotide base pairs.
  • Within each chromosome, sequences of DNA form genes that control or partially control visible characteristics (traits), such as eye or hair color.
  • A gene can have several variations. An allele is a specific version of a gene: one gene codes for hair color, and its alleles decide which color.

Sperm and egg fuse into a zygote with 46 chromosomes (23 pairs), so each parent contributes half the offspring’s genetic information. The genotype is an individual’s genetic makeup. The phenotype is their inherited physical characteristics, such as hair color, skin color, height and build, which result from genetic and environmental influences. In genetics, “parent” means the individual who contributed genetic material, which can differ from social or legal parenthood.

Dominant and recessive traits

A few traits depend on a single gene from each parent. Take cleft chin: call its allele B and the smooth-chin allele b.

  • A dominant allele is expressed whenever it is present. Cleft chin is dominant, so BB and Bb both give a cleft chin.
  • A recessive allele is expressed only in someone homozygous for it (two identical alleles). Smooth chin appears only with bb.
  • Someone with two different alleles (Bb) is heterozygous.

A Punnett square predicts how parents’ alleles combine. If a homozygous cleft-chinned parent (BB) has a child with a smooth-chinned parent (bb), every child has a cleft chin. If the cleft-chinned parent is heterozygous (Bb), each child has a 50% chance of either chin.

Sickle-cell anemia is one of many disorders caused by two recessive alleles. Carriers like Luwi are heterozygous and resist malaria; homozygous people either lack the variant (like Sena, vulnerable to malaria) or have two copies (sickle-cell anemia). Another is phenylketonuria (PKU): people lack an enzyme that turns harmful amino acids into harmless byproducts. Untreated, it causes significant cognitive deficits, seizures and higher risk of psychiatric disorders. Each parent must carry at least one recessive allele; two heterozygous parents have a 25% chance of a child with PKU.

Polygenic traits and mutations

Few human characteristics depend on one gene. Most traits are polygenic, controlled by more than one gene: height, skin color and weight, for example.

Harmful genes like the one behind PKU can come from a mutation, a sudden, permanent change in a gene. Most mutations are harmful or lethal, but now and then one gives an advantage. Evolution needs this variability: if everyone were identical, an environmental change would affect them all the same way and there would be nothing to select. Genetic diversity lets some individuals cope slightly better, so they reproduce and pass on their genes.

Dig deeper: human diversity

Viewed strictly through biology, race is a weak construct. After the human genome was sequenced around the turn of the millennium, many scientists argued that race is not a useful variable in genetic research and that using it risks confusion and harm. Skin tone, eye color and hair texture are genetic, but there is more genetic variation within a racial category than between categories. A focus on race has contributed to misdiagnosis or under-diagnosis of diseases from sickle-cell anemia to cystic fibrosis. Some researchers propose distinguishing ancestry from race and focusing on ancestry (Yudell, Roberts, DeSalle, & Tishkoff, 2016). Race, prejudice and discrimination return in the social psychology chapter.

Gene–environment interactions

Genes do not exist in a vacuum. The environment helps decide when, how and in what combination they are expressed. The text describes three ways to think about this interaction.

Some disagree with range of reaction and argue that genes do not cap potential. Early neglect or abuse, for example, raises the risk of lasting psychological and physical problems in people from very different genetic backgrounds (Miguel, Pereira, Silveira, & Meaney, 2019; Short & Baram, 2019).

Epigenetics often uses twins. Identical twins develop from a single fertilized egg that splits, so their genetic material is the same. Fraternal twins usually come from two eggs fertilized by different sperm, so they differ like ordinary siblings. Even identical twins vary in how their genes are expressed over a lifetime.

Genes are also linked to behavioral characteristics, from basic personality traits to sexual orientation and spirituality (Mustanski et al., 2005; Comings, Gonzales, Saucier, Johnson, & MacMurray, 2000), and to temperament and disorders such as depression and schizophrenia.

Genes and environment in schizophrenia

Tienari and colleagues (2004) followed adoptees. High genetic risk meant the biological mother had schizophrenia; family environments were either disturbed or healthy.

Adoptees with high genetic risk were most likely to develop schizophrenia when raised in disturbed homes. This supports the idea that both genetic vulnerability and environmental stress are necessary for schizophrenia to develop; genes alone do not tell the full tale. Which of the three views above fits best is a question the text leaves to you.

Unit 3 · Section 3.2

Cells of the Nervous System

Neurons pass signals along their length electrically and across the gap to the next neuron chemically. Many psychiatric drugs work by changing that chemical step.

Learning objectives

  • Identify the basic parts of a neuron
  • Describe how neurons communicate with each other
  • Explain how drugs act as agonists or antagonists for a given neurotransmitter system

Two kinds of cells: glia and neurons

The nervous system is built from two basic cell types.

Glial cells (glia)

Traditionally seen as support for neurons. They provide scaffolding, help neurons line up closely enough to communicate, insulate neurons, transport nutrients and waste, and mediate immune responses.

Neurons

Interconnected information processors, essential for every task of the nervous system. There are roughly 100 billion at birth.

Researchers long believed glia far outnumber neurons. Work from Suzana Herculano-Houzel’s laboratory suggests the ratio may be nearly 1:1, which would make human brains more similar to other primate brains than once thought (Azevedo et al., 2009; Herculano-Houzel, 2009, 2012).

Neuron structure

Each part of a neuron has a specialized job. Follow the signal from input to output:

  • The outer surface is a semipermeable membrane: small or uncharged molecules pass through, large or highly charged ones are stopped.
  • The soma (cell body) holds the nucleus. Its branching extensions, dendrites, are input sites that receive signals from other neurons.
  • Signals travel electrically across the soma and down the axon, a major extension of the soma, which can be a fraction of an inch to several feet long.
  • The axon ends in multiple terminal buttons, which contain synaptic vesicles that store neurotransmitters, the chemical messengers of the nervous system.
  • On some axons, glial cells form a fatty myelin sheath that insulates the axon and speeds up the signal. The sheath has small gaps called Nodes of Ranvier.

When myelin is lost

Losing myelin’s insulation harms normal function. PKU (from Section 3.1) reduces myelin and causes abnormal white matter, with severe cognitive deficits, exaggerated reflexes and seizures (Anderson & Leuzzi, 2010; Huttenlocher, 2000). Multiple sclerosis (MS), an autoimmune disorder, destroys myelin across the nervous system. Signals can no longer travel quickly, causing dizziness, fatigue, loss of motor control and sexual dysfunction. Treatments can modify its course and manage some symptoms, but there is no known cure.

The synapse

The signal reaches the terminal buttons, where synaptic vesicles release neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft, the very small space between two neurons where communication happens. The neurotransmitters cross it and bind to receptors, proteins on the dendrite of the next neuron. Receptors come in different shapes that match different neurotransmitters: a lock-and-key relationship. A neurotransmitter binds to any receptor it fits.

The membrane and the resting potential

A neuron sits in extracellular fluid and contains intracellular fluid (cytoplasm). The membrane keeps the two apart, which matters because the signal depends on the fluids being electrically different. That difference in charge across the membrane, the membrane potential, powers the signal. The charge comes from dissolved charged molecules (ions), whose movement the semipermeable membrane restricts.

Between signals, the membrane is held ready in a resting potential, like a stretched rubber band:

  • Sodium (Na+) is more concentrated outside the cell, so it tends to move in.
  • Potassium (K+) is more concentrated inside, so it tends to move out.
  • The sodium-potassium pump moves three Na+ out for every two K+ in, leaving the inside slightly negative. This pulls sodium inward even more.

The action potential

When neurotransmitters bind at the dendrites, small pores (gates) open and Na+ flows in, driven by both charge and concentration. If the inside becomes positive enough to reach the threshold of excitation, the neuron fires. Step through the phases below or press “Fire” to run one spike.

This spike is the action potential, the electrical signal that moves from the cell body down the axon to the terminals. It jumps leapfrog-fashion between Nodes of Ranvier: at each node, some entering sodium ions diffuse to the next section and push it past threshold, triggering a new influx.

All-or-none

The action potential is all-or-none: an incoming signal either reaches threshold or it doesn’t, and once started it cannot be turned off, like a text message after you press send. It is regenerated at full strength along the whole axon, like the lit fuse of a firecracker. That is why an injury to your toe can feel as painful as one to your nose.

After the signal: reuptake, and electrical synapses

At the terminal buttons the vesicles release neurotransmitters, which bind to the next neuron; if the signal is strong enough, the process repeats there. Excess neurotransmitter then drifts away, is broken down into inactive fragments, or is pumped back into the neuron that released it, called reuptake. Clearing the synapse gives a clean “on” and “off” between signals and regulates production: full vesicles signal that no more neurotransmitter is needed.

Neuronal communication is an electrochemical event: electrical down the axon, chemical across the synapse. A few connections are entirely electrical. In an electrical synapse, two neurons physically connect through gap junctions and current passes directly. They are far rarer in the brain but much faster than chemical synapses (Connors & Long, 2004).

Neurotransmitters

Different neurons release different neurotransmitters, each associated broadly with certain functions. Much of this knowledge comes from studying drug effects in psychological disorders.

The biological perspective holds that disorders like depression and schizophrenia are associated with imbalances in one or more neurotransmitter systems. Psychotropic medications treat psychiatric symptoms by restoring that balance.

How drugs act on neurotransmitter systems

Psychoactive drugs can act as agonists or antagonists for a neurotransmitter system, or block reuptake.

The drug LSD is structurally very similar to serotonin and affects the same neurons and receptors. Psychotropic drugs are not instant fixes: people often take one for several weeks before improving, many have significant side effects, and responses vary widely. Some research suggests that combining drug therapy with psychological or behavioral therapy works better than any one treatment alone (e.g., March et al., 2007).

Unit 3 · Section 3.3

Parts of the Nervous System

The brain and spinal cord form the central nervous system; everything else is peripheral. Part of the peripheral system works under voluntary control, and part runs the body’s internal organs automatically.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the difference between the central and peripheral nervous systems
  • Explain the difference between the somatic and autonomic nervous systems
  • Differentiate between the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions of the autonomic nervous system

Central and peripheral nervous systems

The nervous system, made of billions of neurons, controls our thoughts, responses and movements. It has two major subdivisions:

  • The central nervous system (CNS): the brain and spinal cord (Section 3.4).
  • The peripheral nervous system (PNS): connects the CNS to the rest of the body. It is made of nerves, thick bundles of axons carrying messages between the CNS and the muscles, organs and senses in the periphery (everything outside the CNS).

The somatic nervous system

The somatic nervous system handles activities traditionally thought of as conscious or voluntary. It relays sensory and motor information to and from the CNS, so it has two kinds of neurons:

Sensory neurons · afferent

Carry sensory information to the CNS. Afferent means “moving toward.” Afferent = arrive.

Motor neurons · efferent

Carry instructions from the CNS to the muscles. Efferent means “moving away from.” Efferent = exit.

Each nerve is a two-way superhighway: a bundle of thousands of axons, both afferent and efferent.

The autonomic nervous system

The autonomic nervous system controls internal organs and glands and is generally outside voluntary control. Its two divisions have opposite, complementary effects and work in tandem to maintain homeostasis, a state of equilibrium in which biological conditions such as body temperature stay at optimal levels.

  • The sympathetic nervous system prepares the body for stress-related activities.
  • The parasympathetic nervous system returns the body to routine, day-to-day operations.

Picture an early ancestor hunting small game who suddenly disturbs a large bear with her cubs. Sympathetic activation dilates the pupils, raises heart rate and blood pressure, relaxes the bladder, makes the liver release glucose and sends adrenaline surging into the bloodstream. This is the fight or flight response: it gives the body access to energy reserves and heightened senses to fight the threat or run to safety. Once the threat is over, the parasympathetic system takes over: heart rate and blood pressure return to normal, the pupils constrict, bladder control returns and the liver stores glucose as glycogen.

Fight or flight in the modern world

The response kept our ancestors alive amid real physical threats. Today many high-arousal situations, such as giving a presentation or sitting a big test, are psychological, yet we still respond with fight or flight. That is far less adaptive: facing constant threats we can neither fight nor flee harms health. Persistent, repeated stress is linked to greater susceptibility to heart disease (Chandola, Brunner, & Marmot, 2006) and impaired immune function (Glaser & Kiecolt-Glaser, 2005). Some stress reactivity can be wired by early experiences of trauma.

Unit 3 · Section 3.4

The Brain and Spinal Cord

The brain is a two-sided organ of billions of neurons and glia, divided into lobes and regions that each have typical jobs. All of them interact to produce thought and behavior.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the functions of the spinal cord
  • Identify the hemispheres and lobes of the brain
  • Describe the types of techniques available to clinicians and researchers to image or scan the brain

The spinal cord

The spinal cord connects the brain to the outside world; it is a smart relay station. It routes messages to and from the brain and runs its own automatic processes, reflexes.

  • At the top it merges with the brain stem, which controls basic life processes such as breathing and digestion. At the bottom it ends just below the ribs, not at the base of the spine.
  • It has 30 segments matching the vertebrae, each linked to a specific body part through the PNS. Sensory nerves bring messages in; motor nerves send them out to muscles and organs.
  • Bony vertebrae protect it and cerebrospinal fluid cushions it. Damage to one segment cuts off all lower segments from the brain, causing paralysis, so the lower the injury, the fewer functions are lost.

Some sensory messages are acted on by the spinal cord alone, such as pulling away from a hot object or the knee jerk. Skipping the round trip to the brain saves seconds, which matters for survival.

Neuroplasticity

ABC reporter Bob Woodruff suffered a traumatic brain injury when a bomb exploded beside his vehicle in Iraq, leaving him with memory and language deficits. With intensive cognitive and speech therapy he made an incredible recovery (Fernandez, 2008).

That recovery relied on neuroplasticity, the nervous system’s ability to change and adapt. It can follow personal experience, development or injury, and involves creating new synapses, pruning unused ones, changes in glial cells and even the birth of new neurons. The nervous system is most plastic when we are very young but can still change remarkably later in life.

The two hemispheres

The brain’s surface, the cerebral cortex, is folded into bumps called gyri (singular: gyrus) and grooves called sulci (singular: sulcus). These landmarks divide the brain into functional centers. The most prominent sulcus, the longitudinal fissure, splits the brain into the left and right hemispheres.

Each hemisphere controls the opposite half of the body. There is evidence of lateralization, specialization of function in each hemisphere, mainly for language. Decades of work by Michael Gazzaniga and colleagues suggest some hemispheric dominance for functions from cause-and-effect reasoning to self-recognition (Gazzaniga, 2005).

Left hemisphere superior for

Forming associations in memory, selective attention, positive emotions

Right hemisphere superior for

Pitch perception, arousal, negative emotions (Ehret, 2006)

Results on hemispheric dominance have been inconsistent, so it is better to think about how the two hemispheres interact to produce a behavior than to assign behaviors to one side (Banich & Heller, 1998).

The corpus callosum and split-brain patients

The hemispheres are joined by the corpus callosum, a thick band of about 200 million axons that lets information processed on one side be shared with the other. In some cases of severe epilepsy, surgeons cut it to stop seizures spreading. The treatment works but leaves patients with “split brains.”

A split-brain patient cannot name a picture shown in the left visual field, because only the largely nonverbal right hemisphere receives it. They can still recreate it with the left hand, also controlled by the right hemisphere. Once the verbal left hemisphere sees the drawing, the patient can name it (if the left hemisphere can interpret what the left hand drew).

Learning from brain damage

Much of what we know about brain areas comes from studying people with brain damage. A stroke interrupts blood flow to a region and causes loss of function there. When the damaged area is small, researchers can link the resulting behavioral changes to that area; the deficits depend on where the damage is.

Theona’s stroke

Theona, an intelligent, self-sufficient 62-year-old, had a stroke in the front of her right hemisphere. She now has great difficulty moving her left leg: the right hemisphere controls the left side, and the main motor centers are in the frontal lobe. She also sometimes eats fruit straight from the grocery bins before paying, which fits damage to the prefrontal cortex (judgment, reasoning and impulse control).

The forebrain and the four lobes

The brain has three main divisions: the forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain. The forebrain, the largest, contains the cerebral cortex and structures beneath it (subcortical structures): the thalamus, hypothalamus, pituitary gland and the limbic system. The cortex handles higher-level processes: consciousness, thought, emotion, reasoning, language and memory. Each hemisphere has four lobes. Select a region to see what it does.

The frontal lobe runs from the front of the brain back to the central sulcus. It handles reasoning, motor control, emotion and language, and holds the motor cortex, the prefrontal cortex and Broca’s area. The parietal lobe sits behind it and contains the somatosensory cortex, whose map of the body mirrors the body’s spatial layout. After an amputation, that map can reorganize, an example of neuroplasticity linked to “phantom limb” experiences. The temporal lobe holds the auditory cortex and Wernicke’s area. The occipital lobe holds the primary visual cortex; you will see more of it in the sensation and perception chapter.

Phineas Gage

The most famous case of frontal lobe damage. On September 13, 1848, Gage, a 25-year-old railroad foreman in Vermont, was tamping explosives with an iron rod. A spark set off the charge and the rod shot through his face and skull, passing behind his left eye and out the top of his head, landing about 80 feet away. His left prefrontal cortex was severely damaged. He stayed conscious and could get up, walk and speak.

In the months that followed, friends said he was no longer himself: a once well-mannered, soft-spoken man now behaved in odd, inappropriate ways, consistent with lost impulse control. Later work on the rod’s path suggests it also damaged pathways linking the frontal lobe to the limbic system, cutting planning off from emotional processes.

There is evidence the changes were exaggerated and embellished. The case arose during a 19th-century debate over localization (whether brain areas have particular functions), and with very little information, scientists found support for whichever side they already held (Macmillan, 1999).

Beneath the cortex: thalamus and limbic system

The thalamus is the brain’s sensory relay: every sense except smell passes through it before going to other areas for processing. The limbic system, a collection of structures, processes emotion and memory. Smell projects directly to it, which is why smells can evoke emotion in ways other senses cannot. Three of its key structures:

  • The hippocampus: essential for learning and memory.
  • The amygdala: our experience of emotion and tying emotional meaning to memories.
  • The hypothalamus: regulates homeostatic processes such as body temperature, appetite and blood pressure; links the nervous and endocrine systems; regulates sexual motivation and behavior.

The case of Henry Molaison (H.M.)

In 1953, 27-year-old Henry Gustav Molaison had his hippocampus and amygdala removed to control severe seizures. The seizures eased, but he lost the ability to form many kinds of new memories. He could not learn new facts (such as who was U.S. president), remember new faces or recall events even right after they happened. He could learn new skills, such as using a computer, but had no memory of learning them. One of the most studied cases in medical and psychological history (Hardt, Einarsson, & Nader, 2010; Squire, 2009), it showed the hippocampus’s role in consolidating new learning into explicit memory. His intact cerebellum likely explains why he could still learn tasks.

Musician Clive Wearing also lost the ability to form new memories after illness damaged his hippocampus (see Resources for the documentary).

Midbrain and hindbrain

The midbrain lies deep in the brain between the forebrain and hindbrain.

  • The reticular formation is centered in the midbrain but extends up into the forebrain and down into the hindbrain. It regulates the sleep/wake cycle, arousal, alertness and motor activity.
  • The substantia nigra (Latin for “black substance”) and the ventral tegmental area (VTA) contain cell bodies that produce dopamine and are critical for movement. Their degeneration is involved in Parkinson’s disease. They also play roles in mood, reward and addiction (Berridge & Robinson, 1998; Gardner, 2011; George, Le Moal, & Koob, 2012).

The hindbrain, at the back of the head, looks like an extension of the spinal cord. It contains:

  • the medulla, which controls autonomic processes such as breathing, blood pressure and heart rate;
  • the pons (“bridge”), which connects the hindbrain to the rest of the brain and helps regulate brain activity during sleep;
  • the cerebellum (“little brain”), which controls balance, coordination, movement and motor skills and is thought to support procedural memory, memory for how to perform tasks.

The medulla, pons and related structures form the brainstem, which spans the midbrain and hindbrain.

What do you think? Brain dead and on life support

On February 25, 1990, Terri Schiavo of Florida went into cardiac arrest. She was revived, but her brain had gone without oxygen for a long time. Scans showed no activity in her cerebral cortex and severe, permanent cerebral atrophy: she was in a vegetative state and needed a feeding tube. Her eyes sometimes moved and she sometimes groaned, which her parents took as attempts to communicate. Doctors disagreed.

After 12 years her husband argued she would not have wanted to live without feelings, sensations or brain activity; her parents opposed removing the tube. State and federal courts eventually sided with her husband, the tube was removed on March 18, 2005, and she died 13 days later. Her eye movements and groans came from her intact brainstem: the medulla and pons kept her breathing and caused involuntary movements. Over 15 years her medical costs may have topped $7 million (Arnst, 2003).

Similar questions returned in 2013, with a 13-year-old declared brain dead after tonsil surgery, and in a 2013–14 Texas case where state fetal-protection law kept a pregnant, brain-dead EMT on life support against her stated wishes until the fetus was found unviable. The text asks: whose decision should it be, and should cost be a factor?

Unit 3 · Section 3.5

The Endocrine System

A second messaging system: glands release hormones into the blood, giving slower, longer-lasting and more widespread effects than neurotransmitters.

Learning objectives

  • Identify the major glands of the endocrine system
  • Identify the hormones secreted by each gland
  • Describe each hormone’s role in regulating bodily functions

Hormones versus neurotransmitters

The endocrine system is a series of glands that produce hormones, chemical messengers that, like neurotransmitters, must bind to a receptor to send their signal.

Table 3.6How the two chemical messengers differ, as described in Section 3.5
NeurotransmittersHormones
Released byNeurons (terminal buttons)Endocrine glands
TravelAcross the synapse to nearby cells with receptorsThrough the bloodstream to any cell with receptors
Reach of effectsLocalizedWidespread
Speed and durationFastSlower to take effect, longer lasting
BindingBoth must bind to a matching receptor

Hormones regulate all sorts of bodily functions and are ultimately controlled by interactions between the hypothalamus (in the central nervous system) and the pituitary gland (in the endocrine system). Hormone imbalances are related to a number of disorders.

Major glands

  • The pituitary gland descends from the hypothalamus at the base of the brain and works closely with it. Called the “master gland” because its messenger hormones control all the other endocrine glands, though it mostly carries out the hypothalamus’s instructions. It also secretes growth hormone, endorphins for pain relief and key hormones that regulate the body’s fluid levels.
  • The thyroid, in the neck, releases hormones that regulate growth, metabolism and appetite. In hyperthyroidism it secretes too much thyroxine, causing agitation, bulging eyes and weight loss; one cause is Graves’ disease, an autoimmune disease. In hypothyroidism, low hormone levels cause tiredness and feeling cold. Medication can often restore the balance.
  • The adrenal glands sit atop the kidneys and secrete stress-response hormones: epinephrine (adrenaline) and norepinephrine (noradrenaline).
  • The pancreas secretes insulin (lowers blood glucose) and glucagon (raises it) to keep blood sugar stable. People with diabetes do not produce enough insulin, so they take medication that stimulates or replaces it and closely control sugars and carbohydrates.
  • The gonads secrete sexual hormones important for reproduction and mediate sexual motivation and behavior. Ovaries secrete estrogens and progesterone; testes secrete androgens such as testosterone.

Dig deeper: athletes and anabolic steroids

Anabolic steroids are against federal law and banned by many professional leagues (the NFL, for example), yet amateur and professional athletes still use them. They mimic the body’s own steroid hormones, such as testosterone, and may boost muscle mass, strength and endurance, though not for every user. Risks range from cosmetic (acne) to life-threatening (heart attack), and use can cause profound mood changes and more aggressive behavior (National Institute on Drug Abuse, 2001).

Baseball’s Alex Rodriguez excelled while using illegal performance-enhancing drugs (PEDs), which helped him win a contract as the highest-paid player in the sport. The scandal and suspension that followed tarnished his reputation and, by his own account after retiring, cost him over $40 million. Users have also been exposed in cycling and Olympic sports. The text asks: should PEDs be banned, and what would you tell an athlete thinking of using them?
