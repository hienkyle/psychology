Unit 4 · Overview

States of Consciousness

What consciousness is, how the body’s clock runs the sleep–wake cycle, what happens in each stage of sleep, what goes wrong in sleep disorders, how drugs alter the brain, and what hypnosis and meditation do.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening idea

Our awareness of ourselves and our surroundings changes dramatically and regularly. We are highly alert while awake, show little awareness in deep sleep, and dream at other times. Some people also reach altered states on purpose, through meditation or hypnosis, or by using alcohol and other drugs.

The chapter opens with Albert Anker’s 1895 painting Zwei schlafende Mädchen auf der Ofenbank (“two sleeping girls on the stove”), a reminder of how quiet and mysterious sleep looks from the outside. Most of the chapter is about sleep: its stages, why we do it and what can go wrong. It ends with the altered states produced by psychoactive drugs, hypnosis and meditation.

Unit 4 · Section 4.1

What Is Consciousness?

Consciousness runs on a schedule. A clock in the brain, set by light, times our sleep and waking, and things go wrong when that clock and the world fall out of step.

Learning objectives

  • Understand what is meant by consciousness
  • Explain how circadian rhythms are involved in regulating the sleep-wake cycle, and how circadian cycles can be disrupted
  • Discuss the concept of sleep debt

Defining consciousness

Consciousness is our awareness of internal and external stimuli. Internal stimuli include pain, hunger, thirst, sleepiness, and being aware of our own thoughts and emotions. External stimuli include seeing light from the sun, feeling the warmth of a room and hearing a friend’s voice.

Consciousness is best thought of as a continuum, from full awareness to deep sleep. Sleep is a state of relatively low physical activity and reduced sensory awareness; it is different from the rest we take while awake. Wakefulness is marked by high levels of sensory awareness, thought and behavior. In between lie states such as daydreaming, intoxication and the unconsciousness produced by deep anesthesia.

Biological rhythms

A biological rhythm is an internal rhythm of biological activity. The menstrual cycle repeats roughly every 28 days. Body temperature follows a 24-hour cycle: higher temperature goes with alertness, lower temperature with sleepiness.

A rhythm that runs on about a 24-hour cycle is a circadian rhythm. The sleep-wake cycle is the best-known example, but heart rate, blood pressure, blood sugar and body temperature are circadian too. Some of these rhythms help maintain homeostasis, the tendency to keep a balance, or optimal level, within a biological system. The hypothalamus is one of the brain’s main homeostatic centers.

The brain’s clock and melatonin

The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), a small area of the hypothalamus, is the brain’s clock. Light-sensitive neurons in the retina send it information about light, which keeps the clock in sync with the outside world.

Most people sleep at night and are awake during the day. The pineal gland, an endocrine structure inside the brain, releases melatonin, a hormone that helps regulate the sleep-wake cycle and other biological rhythms. Darkness stimulates melatonin release; light inhibits it.

People also differ in when they prefer to be active. These chronotypes include “morning larks” and “night owls”, and the two groups differ in sleep regulation: the brain’s control of switching between sleep and wakefulness, and of coordinating that cycle with the outside world (Taillard, Philip, Coste, Sagaspe, & Bioulac, 2003).

Disruptions: jet lag and shift work

Normally the internal clock and the environment are in step. When they are not, problems follow.

Travel

Jet lag

Jet lag is the fatigue, sluggishness, irritability and insomnia that come from crossing several time zones: the body’s circadian cycles no longer match the local day.

Work

Rotating shift work

Rotating shift work switches people between early, late and night shifts, often weekly. It is common in health care and service jobs and is linked to sleep problems, symptoms of depression and anxiety, exhaustion and more errors on the job.

Both can bring insomnia: a persistent difficulty falling or staying asleep, at least three nights a week for at least a month. A qualitative study of middle-aged nurses on rotating shifts found the schedule also strained their family relationships. One remedy is bright light: exposure to bright light during work shifts, and darkness when not working, can help with insomnia and with symptoms of anxiety and depression (Huang, Tsai, Chen, & Hsu, 2013). Bright light devices (OpenStax Figure 4.4) are also used by people affected by seasonal changes in daylight.

Insufficient sleep and sleep debt

Chronically getting too little sleep builds up a sleep debt, which reduces alertness and mental efficiency. Average sleep has fallen since electric light was invented, and many adults now sleep less than the 7–8 hours a night recommended for them. College students are known for large sleep debts. One sign of a debt: falling asleep very easily whenever you get the chance to nap.

What sleep deprivation does (OpenStax Figure 4.5)

  • Body: linked to obesity, higher blood pressure, raised stress hormones and weaker immune functioning, and to a higher risk of heart disease.
  • Mood: linked to symptoms resembling depression.
  • Thinking and movement: impaired as much as, or more than, by alcohol intoxication.
  • Severe cases: the worst effects come after more than 24 hours awake. Getting fewer than four hours of sleep is linked to irritability, distractibility and impaired judgment, and after 48 hours awake a person may start to hallucinate.

Sleep across the lifespan

Sleep needs fall with age. Young children may sleep up to 16 hours a day, while a meta-analysis, a study that combines the results of many related studies, found that by age 65 people average fewer than 7 hours of sleep a day (Ohayon, Carskadon, Guilleminault, & Vitiello, 2004).

Unit 4 · Section 4.2

Sleep and Why We Sleep

We spend about a third of our lives asleep. Several brain areas and hormones are involved, yet what sleep is for is still debated; the strongest evidence links it to learning and memory.

Learning objectives

  • Describe areas of the brain involved in sleep
  • Understand hormone secretions associated with sleep
  • Describe several theories aimed at explaining the function of sleep

What is sleep?

Sleep varies widely across the animal kingdom. Some fish and amphibians never sleep at all, giraffes sleep very little with no apparent ill effects, and some animals, such as rats, die after two weeks of total sleep deprivation (Siegel, 2008).

In humans, sleep is regulated by both circadian and homeostatic mechanisms. Evidence for the homeostatic side is sleep rebound: people who have been deprived of sleep fall asleep faster at their next chance to sleep. EEG recordings show that sleep is not one uniform state but a series of distinct phases (Section 4.3).

Brain areas and hormones involved in sleep

The hypothalamus, along with other nuclei, works with the thalamus to regulate slow-wave sleep. The pons plays a key role in regulating REM sleep. The hypothalamus also holds the SCN, the biological clock. Two glands release hormones during sleep:

  • The pineal gland releases melatonin, involved in regulating biological rhythms and the immune system (Hardeland et al., 2006).
  • The pituitary gland secretes follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH), which regulate the reproductive system, and growth hormone, which plays a role in physical growth, maturation and metabolism.

What is sleep for? Adaptive explanations

Researchers have proposed explanations rooted in evolutionary psychology, the study of how universal patterns of behavior and cognition evolved through natural selection. Neither main idea is strongly supported.

Table 4.2Two adaptive explanations of sleep, from Section 4.2
IdeaClaimEvidence
Restoring resourcesSleep restores energy used during the day, much as bears hibernate to conserve resources in winter.Little support. Rest and inactivity could save energy without sleep (Frank, 2006; Rial et al., 2007), and energy demands correlate negatively with sleep time (Capellini, Barton, McNamara, Preston, & Nunn, 2008).
Avoiding predatorsSleeping in a safe place during the dangerous dark hours keeps an animal away from predators.Mixed. Species at higher risk of predation sleep fewer hours (Capellini et al., 2008), but deep-sleep time shows no link with predation risk (Lesku, Roth, Amlaner, & Lima, 2006).

Sleep may not serve one universal function; different species may have evolved different patterns in response to their own pressures. Even so, sleep clearly matters: the National Sleep Foundation cites benefits for maintaining health, lowering stress, improving mood, motor coordination, and cognition and memory.

Sleep, cognition and memory

Sleep is clearly linked to cognitive function and memory formation (Rattenborg, Lesku, Martinez-Gonzalez, & Lima, 2007).

  • Sleep deprivation disrupts cognition and causes memory deficits (Brown, 2012), and the more sleep is lost, the worse the impairment (Alhola & Polo-Kantola, 2007).
  • Slow-wave sleep after learning a new task improves later performance on it (Huber, Ghilardi, Massimini, & Tononi, 2004) and seems essential for effective memory formation (Stickgold, 2005).
  • So cramming all night before an exam may not work and can even backfire.
  • Sleep is also linked to creative thinking, language learning and inferential judgments, and may influence how we process emotional information (Walker, 2009).

Unit 4 · Section 4.3

Stages of Sleep

An EEG shows that a night’s sleep is a cycle of distinct stages, each with its own brain-wave signature. Most dreaming happens in REM sleep.

Learning objectives

  • Differentiate between REM and non-REM sleep
  • Describe the differences between the three stages of non-REM sleep
  • Understand the role that REM and non-REM sleep play in learning and memory

Two kinds of sleep: NREM and REM

Sleep is not a uniform state. It is split into rapid eye movement (REM) sleep and non-REM (NREM) sleep, which is any sleep that is not REM. NREM sleep is divided into three stages, each marked by its own pattern of brain waves. As we move from wakefulness into deeper sleep, the waves get slower (lower frequency) and larger (higher amplitude); REM breaks the pattern, with waves that look like those of an awake brain.

REM sleep, rebound and memory

When people are deprived of REM sleep and then allowed to sleep undisturbed, they spend more time in REM: REM rebound. This suggests that REM sleep, like sleep in general, is homeostatically regulated.

REM sleep has been linked to emotional processing and regulation; REM rebound may even be an adaptive response to stress in non-depressed people, reducing the emotional weight of upsetting events from the day. Both NREM and REM sleep have been implicated in learning and memory, which is part of why losing sleep has so many negative consequences.

A night of sleep

Over a night, a sleeper cycles through the stages several times. Early cycles contain the most stage 3 sleep; later in the night stage 3 drops out and REM periods grow longer. Brief awakenings are normal. A graph of these changes is a hypnogram.

Why do we dream?

The meaning and function of dreams have puzzled people for centuries. The text covers four views.

Table 4.4Theories of dreaming, from Section 4.3
TheoristViewKey ideas
Sigmund FreudDreams give access to the unconscious.Manifest content is the dream’s storyline; latent content is its hidden meaning. A dream about a snake might stand for a fear of sexual intimacy.
Carl JungDreams tap a store of knowledge shared by everyone.The collective unconscious is common to all people across cultures; some dream symbols are universal archetypes.
Rosalind CartwrightDreams reflect important life events.Among women going through divorce, the more they thought about their former spouse while awake, the more often he appeared in their dreams (Cartwright et al., 2006). This view has empirical support.

In a lucid dream, aspects of wakefulness carry into the dream: the dreamer knows they are dreaming and can control the dream’s content (LaBerge, 1990). Hobson sees lucid dreams as a chance to better understand dreaming in general.

Unit 4 · Section 4.4

Sleep Problems and Disorders

Between 30% and 50% of people have a sleep disorder at some point. Some make sleep hard to get, some disrupt it from within, and some are dangerous.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the symptoms and treatments of insomnia
  • Recognize the symptoms of several parasomnias
  • Describe the symptoms and treatments for sleep apnea
  • Recognize risk factors associated with sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) and steps to prevent it
  • Describe the symptoms and treatments for narcolepsy

Insomnia

Insomnia, a persistent difficulty falling or staying asleep, is the most common sleep disorder. People may take a long time to fall asleep or wake repeatedly and struggle to get back to sleep. To be diagnosed, symptoms must occur at least three nights a week for at least a month.

Insomnia can feed itself: anxiety about not sleeping raises arousal, which makes sleep even harder. Chronic insomnia is usually tied to feeling overtired and sometimes to depression. Age, drug use, exercise, mental state and bedtime routines all contribute.

Treatments

  • Change habits: cut down on stimulants such as caffeine and exercise more during the day.
  • Sleep medication, sparingly: it can cause dependence, alter the normal sleep cycle and make insomnia worse over time.
  • Cognitive-behavioral therapy: psychotherapy that targets thought processes and problem behaviors. For insomnia it may include stress management and changing habits such as time spent in bed. Studies find it quite effective.
  • See a professional if insomnia persists or affects quality of life.

Everyday connection: supporting healthy sleep

Many college students lose sleep because of stress rather than late nights out. A doctor should be consulted if sleep problems last more than a night or two. The text’s tips:

  • Same sleep schedule every day, weekends included
  • No stimulation (exercise, bright screens) in the hour before bed
  • Exercise daily
  • Avoid naps
  • Keep the bedroom cool, about 60–67 °F
  • No alcohol, cigarettes, caffeine or heavy meals before bed
  • Can’t sleep? Get up and do something else until sleepy

The last tip keeps the bed associated with sleep, not with lying awake.

Parasomnias

A parasomnia is one of a group of disorders with unwanted, disruptive movements or experiences during sleep. They can happen in either REM or NREM sleep.

Slow-wave sleep

Sleepwalking

Sleepwalking (somnambulism): fairly complex behaviors, from wandering to driving a car, with eyes open but no response to attempts to communicate. Usually happens in slow-wave sleep. Benzodiazepines did not help, but treating co-occurring breathing problems sharply reduced it.

REM sleep

REM sleep behavior disorder

In RBD the normal muscle paralysis of REM does not happen, so people act out their dreams and can injure themselves or a bed partner. Linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s. Treated with clonazepam, sometimes with melatonin, plus a safer sleep environment.

Sleep onset

Restless leg syndrome

Restless leg syndrome: uncomfortable sensations in the legs during inactivity or when trying to fall asleep, relieved by moving the legs. Linked to conditions such as chronic kidney disease and diabetes; treated with benzodiazepines, opiates and anticonvulsants.

NREM sleep

Night terrors

Night terrors: sudden panic, possibly with screaming or attempts to flee, while the person seems awake. They happen in NREM sleep and are not remembered. Usually no treatment is needed unless there is an underlying medical or psychological condition.

Dig deeper: a sleepwalking defense?

In 1997 Scott Falater killed his wife. He said he had no memory of the act and had a history of sleepwalking, but in 1999 a jury found him guilty of first-degree murder. Some researchers nonetheless think homicidal sleepwalking is possible in people with certain sleep disorders, which raises hard questions about responsibility.

Sleep apnea

Sleep apnea is defined by episodes in which breathing stops during sleep, for 10–20 seconds or longer, often with brief arousals. Sufferers may not know it but feel tired during the day; a bed partner often notices loud snoring or pauses in breathing. It is more common in overweight people and may worsen cardiovascular disease, so anyone who snores loudly or gasps for air in their sleep should be checked. Untreated daytime sleepiness can be serious: patients in the text describe car accidents and worries about losing their jobs.

Treatment: the most common is a continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) device: a mask over the nose (and sometimes the mouth), attached to a pump that pushes air into the airway and keeps it open (OpenStax Figure 4.13). CPAP works for mild to severe apnea, but many people struggle to use it consistently; newer masks that cover only the nose can help. An expiratory positive air pressure (EPAP) device showed promise in double-blind trials as an alternative.

Sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS)

In sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) an infant stops breathing during sleep and dies. Babies under 12 months are at highest risk, and boys more than girls. Risk factors include premature birth, smoking in the home and hyperthermia (overheating). Infants who die of SIDS also show differences in brain structure and function.

Safe to Sleep (OpenStax Figure 4.14)

  • Place babies on their backs to sleep.
  • Keep the crib free of anything that could suffocate: blankets, pillows, padded crib bumpers.
  • No hat when the baby is put down to sleep, to avoid overheating.
  • No smoking in the home.

The Safe to Sleep campaign is sponsored in part by the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development.

Narcolepsy

Unlike the other disorders, narcolepsy means a person cannot resist falling asleep at inappropriate times. Episodes last from a minute or two up to half an hour, and people wake feeling refreshed. Attacks are often accompanied by cataplexy, a sudden loss of muscle tone or strength that can amount to complete paralysis of the voluntary muscles, and about a third of patients have dream-like hallucinations during them. Heightened arousal or stress can trigger an episode, which is dangerous when driving.

Treatment: usually psychomotor stimulants such as amphetamines. Narcolepsy is linked to reduced levels of the signaling molecule hypocretin, which stimulants do not target directly, so newer drugs aim at the hypocretin system. In one case in the text, a 50-year-old woman’s cataplexy (on her right side) improved only when a stimulant was combined with an antidepressant.

Unit 4 · Section 4.5

Substance Use and Abuse

Psychoactive drugs alter consciousness by acting on neurotransmitter systems. Which system a drug targets explains what it does and how addictive it is.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the diagnostic criteria for substance use disorders
  • Identify the neurotransmitter systems impacted by various categories of drugs
  • Describe how different categories of drugs affect behavior and experience

Substance use disorders

Clinicians diagnose substance use disorders with the DSM-5. People with these disorders often use more of a substance than they meant to and keep using it despite significant harm. Use is compulsive and usually involves physical and/or psychological dependence.

Body

Physical dependence

Physical dependence: the body has changed in response to the drug, so stopping brings withdrawal.

Mind

Psychological dependence

Psychological dependence (drug craving): an emotional need for the drug, often to ease distress. A recent addition to the DSM-5 criteria.

Dose

Tolerance

Tolerance: more and more of the drug is needed for the same effect. Chasing that effect can lead to overdose and death.

Stopping

Withdrawal

Withdrawal: negative symptoms when use stops, usually the opposite of the drug’s effects; withdrawal from a sedative often brings agitation.

Tolerance and withdrawal on their own do not mean someone has a substance use disorder: people can develop both with drugs they take as prescribed and do not abuse.

Drug categories and the brain

Psychoactive drugs work through the brain’s neurotransmitter systems, either as agonists (which enhance a system’s activity) or antagonists (which impede it). The text groups them into four classes.

Alcohol and other depressants

A depressant suppresses central nervous system activity. Alcohol (ethanol), barbiturates and benzodiazepines all act as GABA agonists.

  • Alcohol brings euphoria at low doses and sedation at higher doses. It slows reaction time, reduces visual acuity, alertness and behavioral control, and in excess can cause blackouts or loss of consciousness.
  • Drinking during pregnancy can cause fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD), including fetal alcohol syndrome (FAS).
  • Repeated use leads to physical dependence, tolerance and withdrawal, and possibly psychological dependence. After long-term heavy use, withdrawal can be life-threatening, so people should stop only under medical supervision.

Stimulants

A stimulant increases overall neural activity. Many act as dopamine agonists, which gives them high abuse potential. The class includes cocaine, amphetamines, cathinones (“bath salts”), MDMA, nicotine and caffeine.

  • Amphetamines and methamphetamine. Methamphetamine is a widely manufactured and abused amphetamine that can be made from pseudoephedrine, found in some cold and flu medicines; despite changes in the law it stays cheap and easy to get.
  • MDMA (“ecstasy”, “Molly”) is a mild stimulant with perception-altering effects, usually taken as a pill. Repeated stimulant use can deplete monoamines (dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin), which may drive compulsive use just to feel normal.
  • Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors. In a case study, a 40-year-old woman who drank about three liters of soda a day showed signs of dependence; her health improved after she cut back.
  • Nicotine acts on acetylcholine and is highly addictive. Programs to quit may help in the short term, but their long-term effect is unclear. Vaping is increasingly popular, especially among teens and young adults, and is now known to cause serious lung disease and death.

Dig deeper: methamphetamine

Smokable “crystal meth” is highly addictive. Its brief, intense euphoric high (intense elation and pleasure) drives users to repeat doses, sometimes in “runs” lasting days. Long-term use brings dental problems, skin sores, memory loss, sleep problems, violence, paranoia and hallucinations.

Opioids

An opioid is one of a class of drugs with strong pain-relieving (analgesic) effects, including heroin, morphine, methadone and codeine. Opiates come from the opium poppy; synthetic opioids are often more potent. Because the body’s own endogenous opioids relieve pain, drugs that act on this system are highly addictive.

  • Heroin can be snorted, smoked or injected. It brings a rush, then hours of drowsy, semi-conscious “nodding”. Injecting raises the risk of tuberculosis and HIV.
  • Withdrawal is like a severe flu: very unpleasant but not life-threatening. It can be eased with methadone, a synthetic opioid that is less euphoric than heroin, given at a methadone clinic, or with buprenorphine.
  • Codeine is a low-potency opioid often prescribed for minor pain. Abuse of prescription opioids is a worldwide concern.

Everyday connection: the opioid crisis

Hallucinogens

A hallucinogen produces profound changes in sensory and perceptual experience, often with vivid hallucinations, altered body sensations and a distorted sense of time. Hallucinogens act on different neurotransmitter systems: LSD and mescaline are serotonin agonists, while PCP and ketamine are NMDA glutamate receptor antagonists. In general they are not thought to carry the same abuse potential as the other classes.

Dig deeper: medical marijuana

Marijuana laws changed a great deal between 2010 and 2019. When the textbook was written, eleven states allowed limited recreational possession, and medical marijuana was legal in more than half of U.S. states and in Washington, D.C. Medical marijuana may help with symptoms such as loss of appetite during chemotherapy, but scientific evidence is still limited. Federal law still treats marijuana as illegal, which makes research harder; in 2013 the U.S. Department of Justice said it would not challenge state medical-marijuana laws.

Laws have kept changing since the 2020 edition; check current state and federal law rather than relying on these figures.

Unit 4 · Section 4.6

Other States of Consciousness

Hypnosis and meditation both alter consciousness through focused attention, and both show promise as treatments, but they work in different ways.

Learning objectives

  • Define hypnosis and meditation
  • Understand the similarities and differences of hypnosis and meditation

Hypnosis

Hypnosis is a state of extreme self-focus and attention in which minimal attention is given to external stimuli. In therapy, a clinician uses relaxation and suggestion to try to alter a patient’s thoughts and perceptions. Hypnosis works best for people who are very open to suggestion, and brain-imaging studies link hypnotic states to global changes in brain functioning.

Popular portrayals have spread misconceptions (OpenStax Figure 4.20). Therapeutic hypnosis is empirically based, done by trained professionals with consenting clients, often as part of ongoing therapy, and very different from stage entertainment. It may enhance memory or a skill, but only very modestly (Raz, 2011).

Combined with other techniques, hypnosis is at least somewhat effective for pain management, depression and anxiety, quitting smoking and weight loss.

How does hypnosis work?

Dissociation view

A divided consciousness

Hypnosis is a dissociated state, like driving to work while your attention is elsewhere. In Ernest Hilgard’s experiments, hypnotized people with an arm in ice water reported no pain, yet pressed a button showing that some part of them did feel it (Hilgard & Hilgard, 1994).

Social-cognitive view

Playing a role

People under hypnosis are not in an altered state; they are acting out the social role of a hypnotized person, shaped by their expectations of how such a person behaves (Coe & Sarbin, 1966).

Meditation

Meditation is the act of focusing on a single target, such as the breath or a repeated sound, to increase awareness of the moment. All its forms center on clearing the mind to reach a state of relaxed awareness and focus. In mindfulness meditation, popular in recent years, attention is directed to an internal process or an external object.

Meditation has roots in religious practice (OpenStax Figure 4.21) and is popular in alternative medicine. Research suggests it may help lower blood pressure; the American Heart Association says it may be used alongside traditional treatment for hypertension, though the data are not yet strong enough for a formal recommendation. It also shows promise for stress, sleep quality, mood and anxiety disorders, and pain.

Key terms

All 60 Chapter 4 key terms in one place, grouped by the section that introduces them.

Key terms 60 terms

4.1 What Is Consciousness?

consciousness
awareness of internal and external stimuli
wakefulness
high levels of sensory awareness, thought and behavior
sleep
low physical activity and reduced sensory awareness, distinct from resting while awake
biological rhythm
internal rhythm of biological activity
circadian rhythm
biological rhythm on roughly a 24-hour cycle
homeostasis
tendency to keep a balance, or optimal level, in a biological system
suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)
area of the hypothalamus that holds the body’s biological clock
pineal gland
endocrine structure inside the brain that releases melatonin
melatonin
pineal-gland hormone that helps regulate the sleep-wake cycle
sleep regulation
brain’s control of switching between sleep and wake, and of syncing that cycle with the outside world
jet lag
fatigue and sleep problems when internal circadian cycles and the local day fall out of step after crossing time zones
rotating shift work
work schedule that changes between early, late and night shifts
insomnia
persistent trouble falling or staying asleep, at least three nights a week for a month
sleep debt
result of chronically insufficient sleep
meta-analysis
study that combines the results of several related studies

4.2 Sleep and Why We Sleep

sleep rebound
sleep-deprived people fall asleep faster at their next chance to sleep
evolutionary psychology
study of how universal patterns of behavior and cognition evolved through natural selection

4.3 Stages of Sleep

non-REM (NREM) sleep
all sleep that is not REM sleep; divided into three stages
beta wave
very low-amplitude, high-frequency (13–30 Hz) wave of wakefulness
stage 1 sleep
transition between wakefulness and sleep
alpha wave
low-amplitude, 8–12 Hz wave early in stage 1
theta wave
moderately low-amplitude, 4–7 Hz wave later in stage 1 (and in stage 2)
stage 2 sleep
deep relaxation, with sleep spindles and K-complexes
sleep spindle
brief burst of rapid waves in stage 2; may aid learning and memory
K-complex
very high-amplitude pattern in stage 2, possibly a response to outside stimuli
stage 3 sleep
deep, slow-wave sleep marked by delta waves
delta wave
slow (under 3 Hz), high-amplitude wave of stage 3
rapid eye movement (REM) sleep
waking-like brain waves and darting eyes under closed lids; most dreaming
manifest content
the storyline of a dream (Freud)
latent content
the hidden meaning of a dream (Freud)
collective unconscious
Jung’s store of information shared by all people across cultures
lucid dream
dream in which you know you are dreaming and can control its content

4.4 Sleep Problems and Disorders

cognitive-behavioral therapy
psychotherapy targeting thoughts and problem behaviors; used for insomnia
parasomnia
sleep disorder with unwanted movements or experiences during sleep
sleepwalking (somnambulism)
sleeper carries out fairly complex behaviors
REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD)
normal REM paralysis is missing, so dreams are acted out
restless leg syndrome
uncomfortable leg sensations at sleep onset, relieved by moving the legs
night terror
sudden panic during sleep, possibly with screaming or trying to flee
sleep apnea
episodes during sleep in which breathing stops
obstructive sleep apnea
breathing stops because the airway is blocked
central sleep apnea
breathing stops because brain signals to the breathing muscles are disrupted
continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP)
mask and pump that keep the airway open; treats sleep apnea
sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS)
infant under one year stops breathing during sleep and dies, with no known cause
narcolepsy
cannot resist falling asleep at inappropriate times
cataplexy
sudden loss of muscle tone or strength, sometimes temporary paralysis

4.5 Substance Use and Abuse

physical dependence
bodily changes that bring withdrawal when the drug is stopped
psychological dependence
emotional need (craving) for a drug, often to relieve distress
tolerance
needing more and more of a drug for the same effect
withdrawal
negative symptoms when drug use stops
depressant
drug that suppresses central nervous system activity
stimulant
drug that increases overall neural activity, e.g. caffeine, nicotine, amphetamines, cocaine
methamphetamine
widely made and abused amphetamine that can be synthesized from pseudoephedrine
euphoric high
intense feelings of elation and pleasure from drug use
opiate/opioid
strong pain-relieving drug such as heroin, morphine, methadone or codeine; opiates come from the opium poppy
codeine
low-potency opiate often prescribed for minor pain
methadone
synthetic opioid, less euphoric than heroin, used to manage withdrawal
methadone clinic
facility that uses methadone to treat withdrawal in opioid users
hallucinogen
drug that profoundly changes sensation and perception, often with vivid hallucinations

4.6 Other States of Consciousness

hypnosis
extreme self-focus and attention, with minimal attention to outside stimuli
meditation
clearing the mind to reach a relaxed state of awareness and focus
