Unit 5 · Overview

Sensation and Perception

How sense organs turn light, sound, molecules and pressure into signals the brain can use; how those signals become the world we experience; and why that experience is shaped by attention, expectation and culture.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening idea

Imagine standing on a city street corner. Cars and people move everywhere, a street musician plays, a horn honks in the distance, exhaust and food from a vendor fill the air, and hard pavement presses under your feet. The chapter opens with exactly such a scene: a person playing a piano on a sidewalk beside a busy intersection (OpenStax Figure 5.1).

We rely on our sensory systems for information about our surroundings, and we use it to find nourishment, seek shelter, maintain social relationships and avoid danger. The chapter first separates sensation from perception, then covers the physics of light and sound, the structure and function of the major senses, and finally the historically important Gestalt theory of perception.

Unit 5 · Section 5.1

Sensation versus Perception

Sensation is the physical act of detecting a stimulus. Perception is what the mind makes of it, and attention, motivation, expectation and culture all shape that second step.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish between sensation and perception
  • Describe the concepts of absolute threshold and difference threshold
  • Discuss the roles attention, motivation, and sensory adaptation play in perception

Sensation and transduction

Sensory receptors are specialized neurons that respond to specific types of stimuli. When a sensory receptor detects sensory information, sensation has occurred. Light entering the eye, for example, causes chemical changes in cells lining the back of the eye, and these cells relay messages, as action potentials, to the central nervous system. The conversion of sensory stimulus energy into action potentials is called transduction.

The idea that we have five senses is oversimplified. Besides the five familiar ones, we have senses for balance, body position and movement, pain, and temperature (Table 5.1).

Absolute threshold and subliminal messages

A sensory system’s sensitivity can be expressed as an absolute threshold: the minimum amount of stimulus energy that must be present for the stimulus to be detected 50% of the time. In other words, how dim can a light be, or how soft a sound, and still be detected half the time? Receptors can be remarkably sensitive:

  • On a clear night, the most sensitive cells at the back of the eye can detect a candle flame 30 miles away (Okawa & Sampath, 2007).
  • Under quiet conditions, the hair cells of the inner ear can detect the tick of a clock 20 feet away (Galanter, 1962).

A stimulus reaches this physiological threshold when it is strong enough to excite receptors and send nerve impulses to the brain. A message presented below the threshold for conscious awareness is a subliminal message: we receive it but are not consciously aware of it. There has been much speculation about subliminal messages in advertising, rock music and self-help audio programs. In laboratory settings people can process and respond to information outside awareness, but we do not obey such messages “like zombies”: hidden messages have little effect on behavior outside the laboratory (Kunst-Wilson & Zajonc, 1980; Rensink, 2004; Nelson, 2008; Radel et al., 2009; Loersch et al., 2013).

Difference threshold and Weber’s law

Absolute thresholds are measured under highly controlled conditions that are optimal for sensitivity. Often we care more about how much two stimuli must differ before we can tell them apart. That amount is the just noticeable difference (jnd), or difference threshold. Unlike the absolute threshold, the difference threshold changes with the stimulus intensity.

Ernst Weber proposed this idea in the 1830s, and it is now known as Weber’s law: the difference threshold is a constant fraction of the original stimulus. The brighter the background, the bigger a change must be before we notice it.

Perception: bottom-up and top-down

Receptors constantly collect information, but how we interpret it decides how we act. Perception is the way sensory information is organized, interpreted and consciously experienced. It involves two kinds of processing (Egeth & Yantis, 1997; Fine & Minnery, 2009; Yantis & Egeth, 1999):

Bottom-up processing is driven by sensory information from a stimulus in the environment. Top-down processing is driven by knowledge and expectancy; it is generally goal directed, slow, deliberate, effortful and under your control (Fine & Minnery, 2009; Miller & Cohen, 2001; Miller & D’Esposito, 2005).

One way to hold the distinction: sensation is physical, perception is psychological. Walking into a kitchen where cinnamon rolls are baking, the sensation is your scent receptors detecting cinnamon; the perception might be “this smells like the bread Grandma baked when the family gathered for holidays.”

Sensory adaptation

Perceptions are built from sensations, but not every sensation becomes a perception. We often stop perceiving stimuli that stay relatively constant over long periods. This is sensory adaptation.

That you stop perceiving the flashing light shows that sensation and perception, although closely linked, are different.

Attention and inattentional blindness

Attention plays a large role in what is sensed versus what is perceived. At a noisy party, deep in conversation with a friend, you tune out the background; if someone asked which song just ended, you probably could not say.

In a famous study, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris (1999) had participants count how many times a team dressed in white passed a basketball. During the video a person in a black gorilla costume walked among the players, clearly visible for nine seconds. Nearly half of the viewers did not notice the gorilla. Inattentional blindness is this failure to notice something completely visible because attention was on something else (Mack & Rock, 1998; Simons & Chabris, 1999).

In a similar experiment, participants watched shapes moving on a screen and attended to either the white or the black ones. When a red cross passed across the screen, about one third did not notice it (Most, Simons, Scholl, & Chabris, 2000). Try a version of that task:

Motivation and signal detection

Motivation also affects perception. If you are waiting for an important call, you may “hear” the phone ring while in the shower when it has not. Motivation to detect a meaningful stimulus shifts our ability to tell a true stimulus from background noise. Signal detection theory describes the ability to identify a stimulus embedded in a distracting background; its key-term definition is a change in stimulus detection as a function of current mental state.

  • It may explain why a mother wakes to a quiet murmur from her baby but not to other sounds while she sleeps.
  • It has practical uses: air traffic controllers must pick out planes among many radar blips. The original work behind the theory aimed to make controllers more sensitive to plane blips (Swets, 1964).

Beliefs, experience and culture

Perception is also shaped by beliefs, values, prejudices, expectations and life experiences. People deprived of binocular vision during critical periods of development have trouble perceiving depth (Fawcett, Wang, & Birch, 2005). Shared cultural experience matters too: in a multinational study, Segall, Campbell and Herskovits (1963) found that people from Western cultures were more prone to some visual illusions than people from non-Western cultures, and vice versa. One illusion Westerners were more likely to experience is the Müller-Lyer illusion.

These differences matched the environments people saw every day. Westerners live in a “carpentered world” of buildings with straight lines (Segall et al., 1966). People with an “uncarpentered” view, such as the Zulu of South Africa, whose villages are made of round huts arranged in circles, are less susceptible to the illusion (Segall et al., 1999). Culture affects other senses as well: the ability to identify an odor and rate its pleasantness and intensity varies cross-culturally (Ayabe-Kanamura et al., 1998).

Personality and attitudes play a part, too. Children described as thrill seekers are more likely to prefer intensely sour flavors (Liem et al., 2004), and people with positive attitudes toward reduced-fat foods rate foods labeled reduced fat as tasting better (Aaron, Mela, & Evans, 1994).

Unit 5 · Section 5.2

Waves and Wavelengths

Light and sound both travel as waves. The same few physical features of a wave, its height and its length, map onto what we see and hear.

Learning objectives

  • Describe important physical features of wave forms
  • Show how physical properties of light waves are associated with perceptual experience
  • Show how physical properties of sound waves are associated with perceptual experience

Amplitude, wavelength and frequency

Visual and auditory stimuli are very different in composition, but both occur as waves, and their wave forms share features that matter for perception. Two physical characteristics of a wave are:

  • Amplitude: the height of a wave, measured from the center line to the top of the peak (also called the crest), the highest point, or to the bottom of the trough, the lowest point.
  • Wavelength: the length of a wave from one peak to the next.

Wavelength is directly related to frequency, the number of waves that pass a given point in a given time period, often expressed in hertz (Hz), or cycles per second. Longer wavelengths have lower frequencies; shorter wavelengths have higher frequencies.

Light waves

The electromagnetic spectrum covers all the electromagnetic radiation in our environment: gamma rays, x-rays, ultraviolet light, visible light, infrared light, microwaves and radio waves. The visible spectrum is the small portion of it that we can see, wavelengths from 380 to 740 nm (a nanometer is one billionth of a meter).

In humans, wavelength is associated with color: red goes with longer wavelengths, green is intermediate, and blue and violet are shorter. A mnemonic is ROYGBIV: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. Amplitude is associated with brightness, or intensity of color: larger amplitudes appear brighter.

Sound waves and pitch

The physical properties of sound waves are also tied to what we perceive. A sound wave’s frequency is associated with its pitch: high-frequency waves are heard as high-pitched, low-frequency waves as low-pitched. The audible range for humans is 20 to 20,000 Hz, and we are most sensitive to frequencies in the middle of that range. Other species hear different ranges (Strain, 2003):

Loudness and decibels

Loudness is closely associated with a sound wave’s amplitude: higher amplitudes sound louder. Loudness is measured in decibels (dB), a logarithmic unit of sound intensity. A typical conversation is about 60 dB; a rock concert might reach 120 dB.

  • A whisper 5 feet away or rustling leaves sit at the low end of our hearing range; a window air conditioner, normal conversation, and even heavy traffic or a vacuum cleaner are within a tolerable range.
  • Hearing damage is possible from about 80 dB to 130 dB: a food processor, power lawnmower, heavy truck (25 feet away), subway train (20 feet away), live rock music and a jackhammer.
  • About one third of all hearing loss is due to noise exposure, and the louder the sound, the shorter the exposure needed to cause damage (Le et al., 2017).
  • Earbuds at maximum volume (around 100–105 dB) can cause noise-induced hearing loss after 15 minutes. Even when it seems harmless, listening at maximum volume increases the risk of age-related hearing loss (Kujawa & Liberman, 2006).
  • The threshold for pain is about 130 dB, a jet plane taking off or a revolver firing at close range (Dunkle, 1982).

Frequency and amplitude also interact in loudness. A 10 Hz wave is inaudible however large its amplitude, while a 1000 Hz wave’s perceived loudness changes dramatically as its amplitude increases.

Timbre

Different instruments can play the same note at the same loudness and still sound quite different. This quality is timbre, which refers to a sound’s purity and is affected by the complex interplay of frequency, amplitude and timing of the sound waves. Unit 5 · Section 5.3

Vision

The visual system builds a mental model of the world. Light is focused onto the retina, turned into signals by rods and cones, sent to the back of the brain, and combined into color and depth.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the basic anatomy of the visual system
  • Discuss how rods and cones contribute to different aspects of vision
  • Describe how monocular and binocular cues are used in the perception of depth

Anatomy of the eye

The visual system constructs a mental representation of the world, which helps us move through space and interact with important people and objects. The eye is the major sensory organ of vision. Light waves cross the cornea, the transparent covering over the eye, which acts as a barrier to the outside world and helps focus incoming light. Light then enters through the pupil, the small opening at the front of the eye.

The pupil’s size changes with light level (and with emotional arousal). In low light it dilates to let more light in; in bright light it constricts to let less in. Its size is controlled by muscles connected to the iris, the colored portion of the eye.

Behind the pupil, the lens provides additional focus. Muscles change its shape to focus light from near or far objects. In a normal-sighted person, the lens focuses images on the fovea, a small indentation in the retina, the light-sensitive lining of the eye.

Rods and cones

The retina contains photoreceptors, light-detecting cells, of two kinds:

You feel the difference walking from a bright lobby into a dark movie theater. At first you can barely see; after a few minutes you adjust. In bright light, vision is dominated by cones; in the dark, rods take over, but the switch takes time. If rods do not turn light into nerve impulses as easily and efficiently as they should, a person has trouble seeing in dim light, a condition called night blindness.

From the eye to the brain

Rods and cones connect, through several interneurons, to retinal ganglion cells. The ganglion cells’ axons converge and leave the back of the eye as the optic nerve, which carries visual information from the retina to the brain.

Where the optic nerve leaves, there is a blind spot: light from a small object focused there is not seen. We are not aware of our blind spots for two reasons. Each eye gets a slightly different view, so the two blind spots do not overlap; and the visual system fills in the blind spot, so we do not notice anything is missing.

The optic nerves from the two eyes meet just below the brain at the optic chiasm, an X-shaped structure. Here information from the right visual field (which comes from both eyes) is sent to the left side of the brain, and information from the left visual field to the right side.

Inside the brain, visual information passes through a number of structures to the occipital lobe, at the back of the brain, for processing. It may be processed in parallel pathways (Milner & Goodale, 2008; Ungerleider & Haxby, 1994):

Object recognition

The “what pathway”

Identifies what an object is, such as recognizing that the thing rolling down the street is a ball.

Location and action

The “where/how pathway”

Tracks where the object is and how it moves in space, and how you might interact with it.

What do you think? The ethics of research using animals

David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel won the 1981 Nobel Prize in Medicine for research on the visual system. Over more than twenty years, mostly with cats and monkeys, they used single-unit recordings (tiny electrodes recording when a single brain cell fires). They found that specific brain cells respond to lines of specific orientations and mapped how those cells are arranged into columns and hypercolumns in the visual cortex.

In some studies they sutured one eye of newborn kittens closed and found a critical period for vision: when one eye was deprived of input, other areas of the visual cortex took over the area that eye normally used. Neural connections present at birth can be lost without sensory input.

The debate in brief

  • The dilemma: to many animal advocates, sewing a kitten’s eye shut seems brutal and unethical; yet such research might help children born with certain conditions develop full vision.
  • The law: like nearly every industrialized nation, the United States allows animal experimentation with few limits, given scientific justification. Laws aim to limit unnecessary suffering through standards for humane treatment and housing, not to ban testing.
  • A continuum of regulation (Latham, 2012): from strong government regulation (the United Kingdom has the most significant scheme) to self-regulation by researchers (Japan), with the United States in the middle.
  • Alternatives: patient-drug databases, virtual drug trials, computer models and simulations, noninvasive imaging such as MRI and CT scans, microdosing in humans, and in vitro methods using human cells and tissue cultures, stem cells and genetic testing.
  • Oversight: any U.S. facility that uses animals and receives federal funding must have an Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) to make sure NIH guidelines are followed. It includes researchers, administrators, a veterinarian and at least one person with no ties to the institution, and it inspects laboratories and protocols.

Color vision

Normal-sighted people have three types of cones, each most sensitive to a slightly different wavelength of light. According to the trichromatic theory of color vision, all colors in the spectrum can be produced by combining red, green and blue, and each cone type is receptive to one of these colors.

Connect the concepts: a personal story of colorblindness

A single father learned he was colorblind only after his 7-year-old daughter told him his clothes did not match. A store clerk confirmed it: bright green pants, a reddish-orange shirt and a brown tie. An eye doctor’s test (the Ishihara test, in which people try to find numbers hidden in circles of colored dots; OpenStax Figure 5.15) showed he cannot tell most greens, browns and reds apart. Apart from being unknowingly badly dressed, it rarely affects his daily life.

Some forms of color deficiency are rare. Seeing only in grayscale is extremely rare; people who do so have only rods, so they have very low visual acuity. The most common X-linked inherited abnormality is red-green color blindness (Birch, 2012), and it is far more common in males:

A second major theory is the opponent-process theory. Color is coded in opponent pairs: black-white, yellow-blue and green-red. Some cells are excited by one color of a pair and inhibited by the other: a cell excited by wavelengths associated with green is inhibited by those associated with red, and vice versa. Two implications follow:

  • We do not experience greenish-reds or yellowish-blues as colors.
  • We see negative afterimages. An afterimage is the continuation of a visual sensation after the stimulus is removed, like the spot of light you see after glancing at the sun. When the stimulus is colored, the afterimage shows the opponent colors.

The two theories are not mutually exclusive; they apply at different levels of the nervous system (Land, 1959; Kaiser, 1997):

Depth perception

Depth perception is our ability to perceive spatial relationships in three-dimensional (3-D) space: whether things are in front of, behind, above, below or beside other things. We use a variety of cues.

Binocular cues rely on both eyes. One is binocular disparity, the slightly different view of the world each eye receives. Try it: extend your arm, raise one finger and focus on it. Close your left eye, then open it and close your right eye, without moving your head. The finger seems to shift because each eye sees it from a slightly different angle. 3-D movies use the same principle: the glasses let each eye see a separate, slightly different image, and the brain combines them into the illusion of depth.

We also perceive depth in flat (2-D) paintings and photographs, using monocular cues, which need only one eye. We have more monocular cues than binocular ones, which is why walking with one eye closed does not make you bump into things.

Dig deeper: stereoblindness

Bruce Bridgeman was born with an extreme case of lazy eye that left him stereoblind, unable to respond to binocular depth cues. He relied on monocular cues and never truly appreciated the 3-D world. In 2012, at a 3-D movie with his wife, he put on the glasses and saw real depth for the first time, and the ability persisted outside the theater. Cells that respond to binocular cues normally need activation early in development to survive, so experts assume he must have had at least a fleeting moment of binocular vision as a child. Why it took nearly 70 years for those cells to be activated remains a mystery (Peck, 2012).

Unit 5 · Section 5.4

Hearing

The auditory system turns pressure waves into meaningful sound: nature, music and speech. Tiny bones and fluid carry the vibration inward, hair cells convert it, and the brain works out pitch and direction.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the basic anatomy and function of the auditory system
  • Explain how we encode and perceive pitch
  • Discuss how we localize sound

Anatomy of the ear

The ear has three divisions:

  • Outer ear: the pinna (the visible part that sticks out from the head), the auditory canal, and the tympanic membrane, or eardrum.
  • Middle ear: three tiny bones called the ossicles: the malleus (hammer), incus (anvil) and stapes (stirrup).
  • Inner ear: the semicircular canals, involved in balance and movement (the vestibular sense), and the cochlea, a fluid-filled, snail-shaped structure that holds the auditory receptor cells (hair cells).

From vibration to the brain

Sound waves strike the eardrum, the ossicles pass on the vibration, and the stapes presses into the oval window, a thin membrane of the cochlea. The fluid inside the cochlea moves and stimulates hair cells, the auditory receptor cells of the inner ear, which are embedded in the basilar membrane, a thin strip of tissue within the cochlea. Hair-cell activation is a mechanical process.

Like the visual system, there is evidence that auditory recognition and localization are processed in parallel streams (Rauschecker & Tian, 2000; Renier et al., 2009).

Pitch perception

Low-frequency sounds are heard as lower pitched and high-frequency sounds as higher pitched. How does the auditory system tell pitches apart? Two theories:

Timing

Temporal theory

The temporal theory of pitch perception says frequency is coded by a sensory neuron’s activity level: a hair cell fires action potentials in step with the sound wave’s frequency. Intuitive, but it cannot cover the whole 20–20,000 Hz range, because sodium-channel properties set a limit on how fast a cell can fire (Shamma, 2001).

Location

Place theory

The place theory of pitch perception says different portions of the basilar membrane respond to different frequencies. The base responds best to high frequencies and the tip to low frequencies, so hair cells near the base act as high-pitch receptors and those at the tip as low-pitch receptors (Shamma, 2001).

In reality both theories explain part of pitch perception. Up to about 4000 Hz, both the rate of action potentials and place contribute. Much higher frequencies can only be encoded by place (Shamma, 2001).

Sound localization

Locating a sound is an important part of hearing, much like perceiving depth in vision. Just as vision has monocular and binocular cues, hearing uses monaural cues (one ear) and binaural cues (two ears) (Grothe, Pecka, & McAlpine, 2010).

  • Monaural cue: each pinna interacts with incoming sound differently depending on where the sound comes from. This helps locate sounds above or below and in front or behind us. Sounds from directly above, below, in front or behind reach both ears identically, so monaural cues are essential for them.
  • Binaural cues locate a sound along the horizontal axis from differences in how the two eardrums vibrate. An off-center sound creates two of them:
    • Interaural level difference: a sound from the right is more intense at the right ear, because the sound wave is attenuated as it passes through the head.
    • Interaural timing difference: the small difference in the time a sound wave arrives at each ear.

Hearing loss

Deafness is the partial or complete inability to hear. People born without hearing have congenital deafness. Because sound is passed mechanically from the eardrum through the ossicles to the oval window, some degree of hearing loss is inevitable.

Regular exposure to loud music or construction equipment puts musical performers and construction workers at risk of noise-induced sensorineural hearing loss (OpenStax Figure 5.20). One disease that causes sensorineural loss is Ménière’s disease. Not well understood, it degenerates inner-ear structures and can lead to hearing loss, tinnitus (constant ringing or buzzing), vertigo (a sense of spinning) and increased pressure within the inner ear (Semaan & Megerian, 2011).

A cochlear implant is an electronic device with a microphone, a speech processor and an electrode array. It picks up sound and directly stimulates the auditory nerve to carry information to the brain.

What do you think? Deaf culture

In the United States and elsewhere, deaf people have their own language, schools and customs, known as deaf culture. In the United States deaf people often communicate in American Sign Language (ASL), which has no verbal component and is based entirely on visual signs and gestures; signing is the primary mode of communication. One value of deaf culture is continuing traditions such as sign language rather than teaching deaf children to speak, read lips or have cochlear implant surgery.

Questions to consider

When a child is diagnosed as deaf, parents face hard choices. Should the child attend a mainstream school and learn to speak and lip-read, or attend a school for deaf children, learn ASL and be immersed in deaf culture? Might parents who are deaf themselves approach the decision differently from hearing parents?

Unit 5 · Section 5.5

The Other Senses

Beyond seeing and hearing: the chemical senses of taste and smell, and the body senses of touch, temperature, pain, balance and body position.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the basic functions of the chemical senses
  • Explain the basic functions of the somatosensory, nociceptive, and thermoceptive sensory systems
  • Describe the basic functions of the vestibular, proprioceptive, and kinesthetic sensory systems

The chemical senses

Vision and hearing have received far more research attention than the other senses, so we understand them better. Taste (gustation) and smell (olfaction) are called chemical senses because their receptors respond to molecules in the food we eat or the air we breathe. The two interact strongly: what we call a food’s flavor is really its taste and smell working together.

Taste (gustation)

The traditional four taste groupings are sweet, salty, sour and bitter, but research shows we have at least six:

  • Sweet
  • Salty
  • Sour
  • Bitter
  • Umami
  • Fat (growing evidence)

Umami, the fifth taste, is a Japanese word that roughly means “yummy”; it is the taste for monosodium glutamate (Kinnamon & Vandenbeuch, 2009). There is also growing evidence for a taste for the fatty content of food (Mizushige, Inoue, & Fushiki, 2007).

Molecules from food and drink dissolve in saliva and meet taste receptors on the tongue and in the mouth and throat. Taste buds are groupings of taste receptor cells whose hair-like extensions stick into the central pore of the bud. Taste molecules bind to receptors on these extensions, causing chemical changes in the cell that send neural impulses to the brain.

Taste buds have a life cycle of ten days to two weeks, so burning your tongue does no long-term damage: the buds grow right back. Depending on where the receptor is, signals travel by different nerves to the medulla, thalamus and limbic system, and to the gustatory cortex, tucked under the overlap of the frontal and temporal lobes (Maffei, Haley, & Fontanini, 2012; Roper, 2013).

Smell (olfaction)

Olfactory receptors sit in a mucous membrane at the top of the nose. Odor molecules dissolved in the mucus bind to chemical receptors on the receptors’ hair-like extensions. This causes chemical changes in the cell that send signals to the olfactory bulb, a bulb-like structure at the tip of the frontal lobe where the olfactory nerves begin. From there, information goes to regions of the limbic system and to the primary olfactory cortex, very near the gustatory cortex (Lodovichi & Belluscio, 2012; Spors et al., 2013).

Species differences

The canine nose

There is some evidence that dogs can “smell” dangerous drops in blood glucose and cancerous tumors (Wells, 2010). Their ability may come from having 800–1200 functional genes for olfactory receptors, versus fewer than 400 in humans and other primates (Niimura & Nei, 2007).

Chemical messages

Pheromones

A pheromone is a chemical message sent by another individual, often about reproductive status (Wysocki & Preti, 2004). A female rat ready to mate secretes pheromones that draw nearby males, and pheromone activation is important in eliciting male rats’ sexual behavior. Pheromones in humans have been much researched and are controversial.

Touch, thermoception and nociception

Several receptors in the skin respond to different touch-related stimuli (Abraira & Ginty, 2013). The skin also has free nerve endings, which respond to various touch-related stimuli and serve as receptors for thermoception (temperature perception) and nociception (a signal of potential harm, and maybe pain) (Garland, 2012; Petho & Reeh, 2012; Spray, 1986).

Information from these receptors and free nerve endings travels up the spinal cord to the medulla and thalamus, and ultimately to the somatosensory cortex, in the postcentral gyrus of the parietal lobe.

Pain perception

Pain is an unpleasant experience with both physical and psychological components. It is adaptive: it makes us aware of an injury, motivates us to get away from its cause, and makes us gentler with injured body parts so we are less likely to be hurt again. Pain is generally either inflammatory or neuropathic:

Table 5.5Two kinds of pain, from Section 5.5
KindWhat it signals or comes from
Inflammatory painSignals that some type of tissue damage has occurred
Neuropathic painDamage to neurons of the peripheral or central nervous system, so pain signals sent to the brain are exaggerated

Pain treatments range from relaxation therapy to analgesic medications to deep brain stimulation. The best option for a person depends on the severity and persistence of the pain and any medical or psychological conditions.

Congenital insensitivity to pain

Some people are born unable to feel pain, a very rare genetic disorder called congenital insensitivity to pain (congenital analgesia). They can detect differences in temperature and pressure but cannot experience pain, so they often suffer significant injuries; young children may repeatedly bite their mouths and tongues. Because of injuries and secondary infections, people with the disorder have much shorter life expectancies (U.S. National Library of Medicine, 2013).

Balance and body position

The vestibular sense helps us maintain balance and body posture. Its major organs, the utricle, saccule and three semicircular canals (posterior, superior and horizontal), sit next to the cochlea in the inner ear (see Figure 5.21). They are fluid-filled and have hair cells, like those of the auditory system, that respond to head movement and gravity. Stimulated hair cells signal the brain through the vestibular nerve. We are rarely aware of this system until something goes wrong, as in motion sickness or dizziness from inner-ear infections (Khan & Chang, 2013).

Gestalt Principles of Perception

The brain does not just add up sensations; it organizes them in predictable ways. Gestalt psychologists turned those ways into principles.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the figure-ground relationship
  • Define Gestalt principles of grouping
  • Describe how perceptual set is influenced by an individual’s characteristics and mental state

Gestalt psychology

In the early 20th century, Max Wertheimer published a paper showing that people perceive motion in rapidly flickering static images, an insight that came to him while using a child’s toy tachistoscope. With his assistants, later partners, Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka, he argued that perception is more than simply combining sensory stimuli.

This belief launched Gestalt psychology. Gestalt literally means form or pattern, but its use reflects the idea that the whole is different from the sum of its parts: the brain creates a perception that is more than the available sensory inputs, and it does so in predictable ways. Gestalt psychologists turned those ways into principles for organizing sensory information, and the movement has been extremely influential in the study of sensation and perception (Rock & Palmer, 1990).

Figure and ground

According to the figure-ground relationship, we segment the visual world into figure, the object or person that is the focus of the visual field, and ground, the background. What we see can change dramatically depending on which is which.

Presumably our ability to interpret sensory information depends on what we label as figure and as ground, although this assumption has been questioned (Peterson & Gibson, 1994; Vecera & O’Reilly, 1998).

Grouping principles

Four more Gestalt principles describe how we group sensory stimuli into meaningful perceptions:

  • Proximity: things close to one another tend to be grouped together.
  • Similarity: things that are alike tend to be grouped together, as when we sort football players into two teams by uniform color.
  • Good continuation (the law of continuity): we are more likely to perceive continuous, smooth-flowing lines than jagged, broken ones.
  • Closure (the principle of closure): we organize perceptions into complete objects rather than a series of parts.

Pattern perception and perceptual set

According to Gestalt theorists, pattern perception, our ability to discriminate among different figures and shapes, follows the principles above. Yet perception does not always match the real world. Our perceptions rest on perceptual hypotheses: educated guesses we make while interpreting sensory information. These hypotheses are shaped by our personalities, experiences and expectations, and we use them to form our perceptual set. For example, people given verbal priming produce a biased interpretation of complex ambiguous figures (Goolkasian & Woodberry, 2010).

Dig deeper: bias, prejudice and cultural factors

Perception is built from sensations but colored by our experiences, biases, prejudices and cultures, so it can differ greatly between people. Research suggests that implicit racial prejudice and stereotypes affect perception:

  • In several studies, non-Black participants identified weapons faster, and were more likely to misidentify non-weapons as weapons, when the image was paired with the image of a Black person (Payne, 2001; Payne, Shimizu, & Jacoby, 2005).
  • White individuals decided to shoot an armed target in a video game more quickly when the target was Black (Correll, Park, Judd, & Wittenbrink, 2002; Correll, Urland, & Ito, 2006).

The textbook notes that this research matters given the many high-profile cases in recent decades in which unarmed Black people were killed by people who claimed to believe they were armed or posed a threat.
