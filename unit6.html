Unit 6 · Overview

Learning

How behavior changes with experience: by linking two events (classical conditioning), by linking a behavior to its consequence (operant conditioning), and by watching what other people do (observational learning).

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening idea

The chapter opens on a beach. Minutes-old loggerhead sea turtle hatchlings dig out of the sand and head for the ocean. Gulls and ghost crabs pick some off, but the rest keep going, driven to leave the nest and find the water. Nobody shows them the way (OpenStax Figure 6.1).

Further down the beach, Ben and his son Julian paddle out on surfboards. Julian rides a wave for a few seconds, falls, and comes up in time to watch his father ride the face of the wave. Unlike the turtles, we are not born knowing how to swim or surf. We have to learn, and humans have even built whole institutions devoted to learning. This chapter asks how that learning actually happens.

Unit 6 · Section 6.1

What Is Learning?

Some behavior comes built in. Learning is what changes behavior through experience, and most of it starts with the mind linking events that happen together.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how learned behaviors are different from instincts and reflexes
  • Define learning
  • Recognize and define three basic forms of learning: classical conditioning, operant conditioning, and observational learning

Unlearned behavior: reflexes and instincts

Birds build nests and migrate, infants suckle, dogs shake water off wet fur, salmon swim upstream to spawn and spiders spin webs. All of these are unlearned behaviors. Both reflexes and instincts are innate: organisms are born with them, and they help the organism adapt to its environment without being learned.

  • A reflex is a motor or neural reaction to a specific stimulus in the environment, such as the knee-jerk reflex or the pupil contracting in bright light. Every healthy baby has a sucking reflex at birth; nobody teaches it.
  • An instinct is an innate behavior triggered by a broader range of events, such as maturation or the change of seasons. Migration is an example, and so is a sea turtle hatchling heading for the ocean.

Defining learning

Like reflexes and instincts, learning helps an organism adapt. Unlike them, it involves change and experience: learning is a relatively permanent change in behavior or knowledge that results from experience. Julian will need much more time on his board before he rides waves like his father.

Complex learning, such as surfing or learning psychology itself, mixes conscious and unconscious processes. Traditionally, learning has been studied through its simplest parts: the associations our minds make automatically between events that occur close together or in sequence. Associative learning happens when an organism connects stimuli or events that occur together in the environment. It is central to all three forms of learning in this chapter.

Three forms of learning

  • Classical conditioning (also called Pavlovian conditioning): organisms learn to associate events, or stimuli, that repeatedly happen together. Researchers ask: if one stimulus triggers a reflex, can a different stimulus be trained to trigger the same reflex? Tends to involve unconscious processes.
  • Operant conditioning: organisms learn to associate a behavior and its consequence (reinforcement or punishment). A pleasant consequence encourages more of the behavior; a punishment deters it. Tends to involve conscious processes.
  • Observational learning: watching others and then imitating what they do. It extends the effective range of both kinds of conditioning, which work only through direct experience, and adds social and cognitive layers on top of them.

All three approaches belong to a tradition called behaviorism (Section 6.2). They are not the whole study of learning: memory and cognition, covered in other chapters, have their own traditions, and over time the traditions tend to converge. In this chapter, cognition gradually takes a bigger role in behaviorism, whose more extreme followers once insisted that the environment triggers behavior with no thought in between.

Unit 6 · Section 6.2

Classical Conditioning

A neutral signal, paired again and again with something that triggers a reflex, comes to trigger a response by itself. Five terms describe the whole process.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how classical conditioning occurs
  • Summarize the processes of acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalization, and discrimination

Pavlov’s accidental discovery

Ivan Pavlov (1849–1936) was a Russian physiologist, not a psychologist: physiologists study the life processes of organisms, from molecules up to whole organisms. His interest was the digestive system, and he measured how much dogs salivated in response to different foods (Hunt, 2007). Classical conditioning is a process by which we learn to associate stimuli and, consequently, to anticipate events.

Over time Pavlov (1927) saw the dogs salivate not only at the taste of food but at the sight of food, the sight of an empty bowl and even the sound of the lab assistants’ footsteps. Salivating to food in the mouth is a reflex, with no learning involved, but dogs don’t naturally salivate at footsteps. Pavlov called these responses “psychic secretions” and set up carefully controlled experiments to study them objectively. He trained dogs to salivate to stimuli that had nothing to do with food: a bell, a light, a touch on the leg.

He concluded that organisms have two types of responses to their environment: (1) unconditioned (unlearned) responses, or reflexes, and (2) conditioned (learned) responses.

The five terms: UCS, UCR, NS, CS and CR

  • The unconditioned stimulus (UCS) elicits a reflexive response. For Pavlov’s dogs: meat powder.
  • The unconditioned response (UCR) is a natural, unlearned reaction to that stimulus: salivation at the meat powder.
  • A neutral stimulus (NS) does not naturally elicit a response. Before conditioning, the dogs did not salivate to a tone.
  • In classical conditioning, the neutral stimulus is presented immediately before the unconditioned stimulus. After many tone-then-food pairings, the tone becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS): one that elicits a response after repeatedly being paired with a UCS.
  • The behavior caused by the CS is the conditioned response (CR): the dogs salivate to the tone alone, in anticipation of food.

Real-world examples

Moisha vomited after each chemotherapy injection. Her cancer went into remission, but on her 6-monthly check-ups the oncologist’s office now makes her nauseous. The drugs are the UCS, vomiting the UCR, the office the CS and nausea the CR.

Tiger, a spoiled cat, has a special electric can opener used only for her cat food. For every meal she hears its distinctive “zzhzhz” and then gets her food, and she quickly learns that the sound means she is about to be fed. Food is what she reacts to naturally: the book’s diagram of this example shows her salivating at her dish (OpenStax Figure 6.5). Now, when she hears the can opener, she gets excited and runs to where her food is being prepared.

Everyday connection: classical conditioning at Stingray City

About 40 years ago, people began cleaning fish and conch (UCS) at a sandbar in the Cayman Islands, and stingrays swam in to eat (UCR). By the late 1980s, scuba divers were feeding them by hand. Now the normally solitary southern stingrays respond to the sound of a boat engine (once neutral, now a CS) as a signal that food is coming, and swarm the tour boats (OpenStax Figure 6.6).

Humans, even babies, are conditioned too. Elan buys formula for six-month-old Angelina in blue canisters. The canister is not food itself, but it always comes out just before she is fed. Now, whenever Elan takes out a canister, Angelina gets excited, reaches toward the food and most likely salivates. And conditioning goes beyond food: with an invisible electric dog fence, a small shock (UCS) causes discomfort (UCR); paired with the edge of the yard (CS), the edge itself comes to cause fear and anxiety (CR), so the dog stays inside.

How to decode an example

The book leaves two of these examples for you to work out. Use the definitions from earlier in this section, in this order:

  1. UCS: what triggers a reaction with no learning at all? (In this section: food, chemotherapy drugs, a shock or a loud noise.)
  2. UCR: what is that natural reaction?
  3. CS: what meant nothing at first, but kept coming just before the UCS?
  4. CR: what reaction does that signal now trigger on its own?

Check your answer: the UCR and the CR are often the same behavior. For Pavlov’s dogs both are salivation (Figure 6.2). What changes is what triggers it.

Higher-order conditioning

Suppose Tiger’s food cabinet starts to squeak. Now she hears “squeak” (the cabinet), then “zzhzhz” (the can opener), then gets fed, and soon the squeak alone excites her. Pairing a new neutral stimulus with an existing conditioned stimulus is higher-order conditioning, or second-order conditioning. Moisha shows it too: she sees a syringe in the office before each injection, so syringes come to make her nauseous.

Acquisition, timing and taste aversion

Acquisition is the initial period of learning, when the organism connects a neutral stimulus with an unconditioned stimulus. The NS begins to elicit the conditioned response and eventually becomes a CS that can elicit it by itself. Timing matters: there should usually be only a brief interval between the CS and the UCS, sometimes as little as five seconds (Chance, 2009), though for other types of conditioning it can be up to several hours.

Taste aversion is conditioning in which several hours may pass between the CS (something eaten) and the UCS. Harry ate a lot of cotton candy at the carnival and threw up that night. The next day a piece of candy made him feel sick. UCS: too much cotton candy. UCR: getting sick. CS: the sugary flavor. CR: nausea at the taste of sugar. It can happen after a single pairing with a long delay, which suggests an evolutionary adaptation to avoid harmful foods (Garcia & Rusiniak, 1980; Garcia & Koelling, 1966). It may also help in designing strategies for patients facing nausea from cancer treatments.

Robert Rescorla showed how well an organism learns to predict the UCS from the CS. Ari’s dad serves dinner at 6:00 every day, so Ari starts feeling hungry just before 6:00, even after a late snack. Soraya’s mom serves dinner at 5:00, 6:00 or 7:00, so 6:00 predicts dinner less well and Soraya is less likely to link the two. Rescorla and Allan Wagner at Yale built a mathematical formula for the probability that an association is learned, given how well the CS predicts the UCS and other factors: the Rescorla-Wagner model (Rescorla & Wagner, 1972).

Extinction and spontaneous recovery

To break the connection, present the CS without the UCS. Pavlov sounded the tone without the meat powder, and the dogs soon stopped responding. Extinction is the decrease in the conditioned response when the unconditioned stimulus is no longer presented with the conditioned stimulus: the response gets weaker and weaker until it disappears.

Extinguished learning is not erased. After a couple of hours’ rest from extinction training, Pavlov’s dogs salivated to the bell again. Pavlov called this spontaneous recovery: the return of a previously extinguished conditioned response after a rest period. Tiger would do the same if the can opener broke for months and was then fixed.

The same thing happens on your walk to campus. An ice cream truck’s music (NS) comes before the taste of a chocolate bar (UCS), and your mouth waters (UCR). Within days, the jingle alone makes your mouth water (acquisition). The truck runs out of ice cream and you stop buying, and by the end of the week the music no longer has an effect (extinction). After a weekend away, Monday’s jingle makes your mouth water again (spontaneous recovery).

Stimulus generalization and discrimination

Acquisition and extinction strengthen and weaken an association. Two more processes decide which stimuli trigger the learned response:

  • Stimulus discrimination: learning to respond differently to similar stimuli, giving the CR only to the CS. Pavlov’s dogs ignored other tones such as the doorbell; Tiger ignores the electric mixer; Moisha stopped feeling ill at other doctors’ appointments, such as her annual physical.
  • Stimulus generalization, the opposite: giving the CR to stimuli similar to the CS. The more similar a stimulus is to the CS, the more likely the CR. Moisha felt ill visiting other oncologists, or other doctors in her oncologist’s building.

Behaviorism and Little Albert

John B. Watson is considered the founder of behaviorism, a school of thought from the first part of the 20th century that built on Pavlov’s classical conditioning (Hunt, 2007). In stark contrast with Freud, who placed the causes of behavior in the unconscious, Watson held that all behavior can be studied as a simple stimulus-response reaction. For psychology to be a legitimate science, it had to drop internal mental processes, which cannot be seen or measured, and study outward, observable behavior. Pavlov had conditioned reflexes; Watson believed the same principles could condition human emotions (Watson, 1919).

In 1920, Watson, then chair of psychology at Johns Hopkins University, and his graduate student Rosalie Rayner showed that fear can be classically conditioned in a baby nicknamed Little Albert.

Watson wanted to produce a phobia (a persistent, excessive fear of a specific object or situation) through conditioning alone, to counter Freud’s view that phobias come from deep, hidden conflicts. There is no evidence that Little Albert had phobias in later years, and the study would be considered unethical by today’s standards.

Everyday connection: advertising and associative learning

Car commercials often pair the car with an attractive model, so viewers come to see the car as desirable (Cialdini, 2008). In Cialdini’s account, men who saw an ad with the model later rated the same car as faster, more appealing and better designed than men who saw it without the model. It also explains why advertisers drop athletes after a scandal: an athlete no longer linked to positive feelings can’t serve as the UCS that attaches those feelings (the UCR) to the product (the CS).

Unit 6 · Section 6.3

Operant Conditioning

Behavior is shaped by what follows it. Reinforcement makes a behavior more likely, punishment makes it less likely, and the timing of rewards decides how steady and how stubborn the behavior becomes.

Learning objectives

  • Define operant conditioning
  • Explain the difference between reinforcement and punishment
  • Distinguish between reinforcement schedules

The law of effect and the Skinner box

In operant conditioning, organisms learn to associate a behavior and its consequence; a pleasant consequence makes the behavior more likely to be repeated. Spirit, a dolphin at the National Aquarium in Baltimore, does a flip when her trainer blows a whistle, and gets a fish.

B. F. Skinner saw that classical conditioning only covers existing, reflexively elicited behaviors, and can’t explain new behaviors such as riding a bike. He proposed that behavior is motivated by its consequences, building on Edward Thorndike’s law of effect: behaviors followed by satisfying consequences are more likely to be repeated, and behaviors followed by unpleasant ones are less likely (Thorndike, 1911). We show up for work largely because we are paid; stop the pay and we will likely stop showing up, even if we love the job.

Skinner tested this on animals, mainly rats and pigeons (Skinner, 1938), inside an operant conditioning chamber, now known as a “Skinner box”.

Four words with special meanings

In operant conditioning, positive and negative do not mean good and bad: positive means something is added, negative means something is taken away. Reinforcement increases a behavior; punishment decreases it. All reinforcers, positive or negative, increase the likelihood of a response; all punishers decrease it.

Reinforcement

Reinforcement means applying a consequence to increase a behavior. The most effective way to teach a new behavior is positive reinforcement: adding a desirable stimulus to increase a behavior. Five-year-old Jerome cleans his room quickly because he’s promised a new art set. Paychecks, high grades, praise and passing a driver’s test are all rewards we receive constantly.

  • Paying children to read: second graders in Dallas earned $2 for each book they read and passed a short quiz on, and reading comprehension rose significantly (Fryer, 2010).
  • Skinner’s teaching machine: it rewarded small steps in learning (Skinner, 1961), an early forerunner of computer-assisted learning. Correct answers brought immediate positive reinforcement and let students continue; wrong answers brought none, so students would study more to be reinforced next time.

In negative reinforcement, an undesirable stimulus is removed to increase a behavior. A car’s “beep, beep, beep” stops when you fasten your seatbelt, so you are more likely to buckle up in future. Horse riders apply pressure on the reins or with their legs and release it when the horse turns or speeds up.

Punishment

Many people confuse negative reinforcement with punishment, but reinforcement, even negative, always increases a behavior, while punishment always decreases it.

  • Positive punishment: add an undesirable stimulus to decrease a behavior, such as scolding a student for texting in class.
  • Negative punishment: remove a pleasant stimulus to decrease a behavior, such as taking away a favorite toy when a child misbehaves.

Punishment, especially when immediate, can decrease unwanted behavior: if Brandon runs into the street after a ball and has to write “I will not run into the street” 100 times, he probably won’t do it again. Physical punishment such as spanking has drawbacks:

  1. It may teach fear, of the street but also of the person who punished. Children punished by teachers may fear the teacher and avoid school (Gershoff et al., 2010), and most U.S. schools have banned corporal punishment.
  2. It may increase aggression and antisocial behavior and delinquency (Gershoff, 2002). A child spanked by an angry parent may hit friends who won’t share.

Skinner suggested weighing punishment against its possible harms. Today’s psychologists and parenting experts favor reinforcement: catch the child doing something good and reward it.

Shaping

Reinforcement only works once the behavior appears, and an organism is unlikely to produce anything but the simplest behaviors on its own. In shaping, we reward successive approximations of a target behavior, breaking it into small, achievable steps. Skinner used shaping to teach pigeons to peck a disk, turn in circles, walk in figure eights and even play ping pong, and animal trainers still use it. Stimulus discrimination is an important part of shaping, just as it was for Pavlov’s dogs.

A primary reinforcer has innate reinforcing qualities, and organisms do not lose their drive for these things. A secondary reinforcer has no inherent value and reinforces only when linked with a primary reinforcer. Tokens are secondary reinforcers that can be traded for rewards and prizes; whole behavior-management systems built on them are called token economies, and they work well in schools, prisons and mental hospitals. Adibsereshki and Abkenar (2014) found a token economy increased appropriate social behavior and reduced inappropriate behavior in eighth graders. In studies with younger children, not hitting or pinching earned a “quiet hands” token, hitting or pinching lost one, and tokens were exchanged for minutes of playtime.

Everyday connection: behavior modification in children

Behavior modification uses operant conditioning to swap undesirable behaviors for more acceptable ones. A sticker chart is a token economy: each listed behavior earns a sticker, and enough stickers earn a prize (OpenStax Figure 6.11). For it to work, the reinforcement must be connected to the behavior, must matter to the child, and must be given consistently.

Time-out works by negative punishment: the child is removed from a desirable activity. When Sophia throws blocks at her brother Mario after a warning, she leaves the room for a few minutes. Guidelines from the text:

  • Remove the child from a desirable activity to a less desirable place; otherwise time-out is a reward and backfires.
  • Rule of thumb: one minute per year of age. Sophia is five, so five minutes; a timer helps.
  • Stay calm, ignore the child during time-out (attention may reinforce the misbehavior), and give a hug or kind word when it ends.

Reinforcement schedules

With continuous reinforcement, every instance of the behavior is rewarded, as when each lever press drops a food pellet. It is the quickest way to teach a new behavior, and works best when the reward comes immediately so the learner links behavior and consequence.

Once a behavior is learned, trainers often switch to partial reinforcement (also called intermittent reinforcement), where the behavior is not rewarded every time. Partial schedules are described along two dimensions:

  • Fixed (set, unchanging) or variable (changing) number of responses or amount of time between reinforcements.
  • Interval (based on time) or ratio (based on the number of responses).

  • Fixed interval reinforcement schedule: rewarded after a set amount of time. June presses a button for pain medication, but the doctor allows one dose an hour, so pressing early is pointless.
  • Variable interval reinforcement schedule: rewarded after unpredictable amounts of time. Manuel never knows when quality control will visit, so he keeps the restaurant clean and the service fast all the time.
  • Fixed ratio reinforcement schedule: a set number of responses before a reward. Carla earns per pair sold, so she pushes extra pairs whether or not customers need them. Fixed ratios favor quantity of output; fixed intervals, not based on quantity, can lead to higher quality.
  • Variable ratio reinforcement schedule: the number of responses needed varies. It is the most powerful partial schedule. Sarah, not a gambler, keeps feeding quarters into a slot machine after a win, even once she is $10 down, because the next quarter might pay. This is one reason gambling is so addictive.

In operant conditioning, extinction follows at some point after reinforcement stops, and how fast depends on the schedule. Variable ratio is the most productive and the most resistant to extinction; fixed interval is the least productive and the easiest to extinguish. If June’s button stops delivering at the expected time, she quickly stops pressing.

Connect the concepts: gambling and the brain

Skinner (1953) argued that a gambling establishment can keep patrons paying by returning part of their money on a variable-ratio schedule, and even claimed he could turn a pigeon into a pathological gambler. Variable-ratio schedules do keep behavior persistent: give in to a child’s tantrum even once, and the occasional reward makes the tantrums very hard to stop.

  • Recent rat research has not supported the idea that variable-ratio training alone causes pathological gambling (Laskowski et al., 2019).
  • Gambling activates the brain’s dopamine reward centers (Murch & Clark, 2016). Even near misses increase activity in the ventral striatum and other reward areas (Chase & Clark, 2010), effects almost identical to those of drugs like cocaine and heroin. On that evidence the DSM-5 now classes gambling as an addiction; earlier editions called it an impulse control disorder.
  • Norepinephrine (released during stress, arousal or thrill) and serotonin are also involved; pathological gamblers may gamble to raise norepinephrine, and serotonin deficiencies may add to compulsive behavior (Potenza, 2013).
  • Cause and effect are unclear. A true experiment would be unethical, so gambling might change brain chemistry rather than the reverse, or a confounding variable might drive both.

Cognition and latent learning

Strict behaviorists such as Watson and Skinner studied behavior, not cognition (thoughts and expectations). Skinner’s view was so strong it was called radical behaviorism; he treated the mind as a “black box”, unknowable and not worth studying. Another behaviorist, Edward C. Tolman, disagreed. His rat experiments showed that organisms can learn without immediate reinforcement (Tolman & Honzik, 1930; Tolman, Ritchie, & Kalish, 1946), against the prevailing idea that reinforcement had to be immediate. That pointed to a cognitive side of learning.

Tolman put hungry rats in a maze with no reward, and compared them with a group rewarded with food at the end. The unrewarded rats built a cognitive map, a mental picture of the maze’s layout. After 10 sessions without reward, food was placed in the goal box. As soon as the rats knew about it, they ran the maze as quickly as the group rewarded all along. This is latent learning: learning that occurs but is not shown in behavior until there is a reason to show it.

Latent learning happens in people too. Ravi’s dad drives him to school every day, so Ravi learns the route but never has to show it. One day his dad leaves early, and Ravi bikes the same route, revealing what he had learned.

Everyday connection: this place is like a maze

Whenever we enter a new place we build a cognitive map of it. Buildings with many look-alike areas or short lines of sight make it hard to predict what’s around a corner. Psychologist Laura Carlson (2010) suggests noting specific features as you enter, such as a picture on the wall, a fountain, a statue or an escalator, to add landmarks to your map for finding your way out later. Unit 6 · Section 6.4

Observational Learning (Modeling)

We learn by watching others and copying them, but only when we pay attention, remember, can do it ourselves and want to. What happens to the model decides much of the last part.

Learning objectives

  • Define observational learning
  • Discuss the steps in the modeling process
  • Explain the prosocial and antisocial effects of observational learning

Learning by watching

Classical and operant conditioning are forms of associative learning. In observational learning, we learn by watching others and then imitating, or modeling, what they do or say, as when you look up a how-to video. The people performing the behavior are models. Research suggests this imitative learning involves a specific kind of neuron, the mirror neuron (Hickock, 2010; Rizzolatti et al., 2002, 2006).

Other animals learn this way too. Researchers gave juice boxes with straws to two groups of captive chimpanzees. One group, the “dippers,” dipped the straw and sucked the little juice off its end; the other, the “suckers,” drank through the straw directly and got much more. After watching the suckers, all of the dippers switched (Yamamoto, Humle, & Tanaka, 2013). A spider monkey even learned to drink from a plastic bottle by watching a human (OpenStax Figure 6.16).

Imitation can also copy what we would rather it didn’t. Claire, at her wit’s end with her defiant nine-year-old son Jay, beat him with a belt. That night she saw her four-year-old daughter Anna whip her teddy bear with a belt. Realizing Anna was imitating her, Claire decided to discipline her children differently.

Bandura’s social learning theory

Like Tolman, Albert Bandura departed from strict behaviorism. He and others proposed social learning theory, a brand of behaviorism that takes cognitive processes into account. Pure behaviorism could not explain learning without external reinforcement, so internal mental states must play a role, and observational learning is much more than imitation. In imitation you simply copy the model; according to Lefrançois (2012), observational learning can also mean that:

  • You learn a new response. After seeing a coworker chewed out for being late, you leave home 10 minutes earlier.
  • You choose whether to imitate depending on what happened to the model. Julian copies his father’s successful pop-up on the surfboard, but learns not to touch a hot stove after seeing his father get burned.
  • You learn a general rule that applies to other situations.

The four steps of modeling

Observing a model is not enough. Bandura described four steps that must happen for observational learning to succeed: attention, retention, reproduction and motivation. Motivation depends on what happened to the model. Seeing the model rewarded is vicarious reinforcement and makes you more likely to copy; seeing the model punished is vicarious punishment and makes you less likely. Once you actually perform the behavior, the reinforcement you receive helps decide whether you repeat it.

The Bobo doll study

Bandura studied how children model adults’ aggressive and violent behavior (Bandura, Ross, & Ross, 1961). A child watched a teacher act aggressively toward a five-foot inflatable “Bobo doll”: hitting, throwing and even punching it. What the children did next depended on what happened to the teacher.

Prosocial and antisocial effects

Bandura concluded that we watch and learn, and that the learning can have both prosocial (positive) and antisocial effects.

Some studies link viewing violence with aggression in children (Anderson & Gentile, 2008; Kirsch, 2010; Miller et al., 2012). By high school graduation a child has been exposed to around 200,000 violent acts through media (Huston et al., 1992). Media violence might teach aggressive behavior directly, and repeated exposure may also desensitize people to violence.

What do you think? Violent media and aggression

Bandura’s early studies suggested TV violence increased aggression in children. Craig Anderson and colleagues found extensive evidence for a causal link between hours of violent media exposure and aggressive thoughts and behavior (Anderson et al., 2010, 2015; Bushman et al., 2016). Christopher Ferguson and others argue that while there may be a link, research so far has not accounted for other risk factors such as mental health and family life (Ferguson, 2011; Gentile, 2016). Key terms 40 terms

6.1 What Is Learning?

associative learning
connecting stimuli or events that occur together in the environment (classical and operant conditioning)
instinct
unlearned, complex pattern of behavior; thought to be more prevalent in lower animals than in humans
learning
change in behavior or knowledge that results from experience
reflex
unlearned, automatic response to a stimulus in the environment

6.2 Classical Conditioning

acquisition
initial learning period in which a neutral stimulus is connected with an unconditioned stimulus until it elicits the conditioned response
classical conditioning
learning in which a stimulus or experience comes before the behavior and gets paired with it
conditioned response (CR)
response caused by the conditioned stimulus
conditioned stimulus (CS)
stimulus that elicits a response because it has been paired with an unconditioned stimulus
extinction
decrease in the conditioned response when the unconditioned stimulus no longer follows the conditioned stimulus
higher-order conditioning
(also, second-order conditioning) using a conditioned stimulus to condition a neutral stimulus
neutral stimulus (NS)
stimulus that does not initially elicit a response
spontaneous recovery
return of a previously extinguished conditioned response
stimulus discrimination
responding differently to similar stimuli
stimulus generalization
giving the conditioned response to stimuli similar to the conditioned stimulus
unconditioned response (UCR)
natural, unlearned reaction to a given stimulus
unconditioned stimulus (UCS)
stimulus that elicits a reflexive response

6.3 Operant Conditioning

cognitive map
mental picture of the layout of the environment
continuous reinforcement
rewarding a behavior every time it occurs
fixed interval reinforcement schedule
behavior is rewarded after a set amount of time
fixed ratio reinforcement schedule
a set number of responses must occur before a behavior is rewarded
latent learning
learning that occurs but may not show until there is a reason to demonstrate it
law of effect
behavior followed by satisfying consequences is repeated; behavior followed by unpleasant consequences is discouraged
negative punishment
taking away a pleasant stimulus to decrease or stop a behavior
negative reinforcement
taking away an undesirable stimulus to increase a behavior
operant conditioning
learning in which the stimulus or experience comes after the behavior
partial reinforcement
rewarding a behavior only some of the time
positive punishment
adding an undesirable stimulus to stop or decrease a behavior
positive reinforcement
adding a desirable stimulus to increase a behavior
primary reinforcer
has innate reinforcing qualities (e.g., food, water, shelter, sex)
punishment
a consequence applied to decrease a behavior
radical behaviorism
Skinner’s staunch behaviorism: even complex mental functions like language are only stimulus-outcome associations
reinforcement
a consequence applied to increase a behavior
secondary reinforcer
no value in itself; reinforces only when linked with something else (e.g., money, gold stars, poker chips)
shaping
rewarding successive approximations toward a target behavior
variable interval reinforcement schedule
behavior is rewarded after unpredictable amounts of time
variable ratio reinforcement schedule
the number of responses needed before a reward varies

6.4 Observational Learning (Modeling)

model
person who performs a behavior that serves as an example (in observational learning)
observational learning
learning by watching others
vicarious punishment
seeing the model punished makes the observer less likely to imitate
vicarious reinforcement
seeing the model rewarded makes the observer more likely to imitate
