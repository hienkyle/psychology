Unit 7 · Section 7.1

What Is Cognition?

Cognition is thinking. To think efficiently, the brain sorts the world into concepts and bundles related concepts into schemata.

Learning objectives

  • Describe cognition
  • Distinguish concepts and prototypes
  • Explain the difference between natural and artificial concepts
  • Describe how schemata are organized and constructed

Cognition

Your brain is always active, even while you sleep, though you are aware of only a small part of that activity. Simply put, cognition is thinking: it covers the processes of perception, knowledge, problem solving, judgment, language and memory, some of which happen without conscious awareness. Planning the order in which to run your errands is an everyday example.

Cognitive psychology is the field that studies all aspects of how people think, including intelligence, problem solving and the role of emotion in thought.

Concepts and prototypes

The brain combines sensory input with emotions and memories to form thoughts. To organize all this, it uses concepts: categories or groupings of linguistic information, objects, ideas or life experiences. Concepts can be abstract (justice, freedom) or concrete (types of birds, breeds of dog). They let us generalize: we draw on past experience to understand new things.

A prototype is the best example or representation of a concept. If your first pet was a dog, that dog may be your prototype for “dog,” the image that comes to mind first when you hear the word.

Natural and artificial concepts

  • A natural concept forms through direct or indirect experience. You understand snow because you have seen and touched it, or because you have read about it and seen pictures.
  • An artificial concept is defined by a specific, fixed set of characteristics. Squares and triangles are artificial concepts, and so is the area of a square, defined by the formula length × width. Understanding the area of other shapes builds on that original concept.

Schemata

A schema (plural schemata) is a mental construct made of a cluster of related concepts (Bartlett, 1932). Schemata let the brain work efficiently: in a new situation, you can draw on what you already know about similar situations. There are many types; two are covered here.

Table 7.1Two kinds of schemata, compiled from Section 7.1
Role schemaEvent schema (cognitive script)
What it isAssumptions about how people in a particular role behaveA set of behaviors that feels like a routine
ExampleExpecting a firefighter to be brave and to run into a burning buildingUsing an elevator: let people get off, step in, turn to face the doors, find the right button (OpenStax Figure 7.4)
PitfallCan be wrong: not everyone in a role acts as expectedHard to break, even when it is unsafe

Event schemata are performed almost automatically, which helps us adapt quickly to the situation we are in. People rarely face the back of an elevator and feel uncomfortable when a crowd keeps them from facing the front. Event schemata vary across cultures: a greeting is a handshake in the United States, sticking out the tongue in Tibet and a fist bump in Belize. Because they are automatic, they can be very hard to change. Many people reach for their phone when it signals a message, even while driving, a habit that is extremely dangerous (OpenStax Figure 7.5).

Unit 7 · Section 7.2

Language

A finite vocabulary plus a set of rules lets us say an unlimited number of things. Children pick it up without being taught, and researchers still debate how much it shapes the way we think.

Learning objectives

  • Define language and demonstrate familiarity with the components of language
  • Understand the development of language
  • Explain the relationship between language and thinking

Components of language

Language is a communication system that uses words and systematic rules for organizing them to transmit information. Not all communication is language: many species signal to one another with posture, movement, scent or sounds. Many researchers argue that language is what most separates humans from other species.

  • The lexicon is a language’s vocabulary.
  • Grammar is the set of rules used to convey meaning through the lexicon. In English, for example, most verbs take -ed to show the past tense.
  • Phonemes are the basic sound units of a language. Each language has its own set.
  • Morphemes are the smallest units of language that convey meaning. Some, such as I, are both a phoneme and a morpheme.
  • Semantics (part of grammar) is the process by which we derive meaning from morphemes and words.
  • Syntax (also part of grammar) is the way words are organized into sentences.

Grammar lets us combine a limited set of words in endless new ways. We can talk about concrete things and abstract ideas, the past and the future, and pass on plans and instructions.

Language development

B. F. Skinner argued that children learn language through reinforcement, as they learn other behaviors. Noam Chomsky rejected this, arguing that humans are biologically predisposed to acquire language. Several observations support a biological basis:

  • Children learn language without formal teaching, and development follows a similar pattern across cultures.
  • There seems to be a critical period: language is acquired most easily early in life and becomes harder with age, especially a second language.
  • Learning starts before birth. Newborns prefer their mother’s voice, can tell her language apart from others, and prefer faces whose lip movements match the speech they hear.

  • Babbling. After the first few months, infants babble repeated syllables. Babbling is not clearly communicative. Infants raised with sign language babble with their hands in a similar way.
  • One-word stage. First words usually appear between 12 and 18 months. They are mostly nouns, and one word can stand for a whole sentence: “cookie” might mean “I see a cookie” or “I want a cookie.”
  • Overgeneralization. Children apply a rule where it doesn’t fit, saying “gooses” or “mouses.” Overgeneralization shows that the child has learned the rule, even before learning its exceptions.

The case of Genie

Dig deeper: Genie and the critical period

In 1970 a social worker in the Los Angeles area found a 13-year-old girl, later called Genie, who had been severely neglected and kept in isolation for most of her life. She could not stand, chew solid food or speak. After her rescue she built a large vocabulary quickly, but she never mastered grammar. Her case is often cited as evidence for a critical period: exposure to language that came so late may have limited what she could learn.

Language and thought

Starting in the 1940s, Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf proposed that the language we speak shapes the way we think. English, for instance, uses one word, love, for many different kinds of love. Later researchers judged the strong form of this idea too absolute and short on evidence, and psychologists still debate how language and thought relate.

What do you think? Saudade

Do bilingual people think differently in each language? The Portuguese word saudade mixes loss, longing, nostalgia and hope in a way no single English word does. OpenStax Figure 7.6 shows two paintings of it: Saudade de Nápoles (“missing Naples”) by Bertha Worms (1895) and Saudade by Almeida Júnior (1899).

Unit 7 · Section 7.3

Problem Solving

First identify the problem, then pick a strategy. Strategies range from guessing to exact formulas, and familiar habits and biases can steer us wrong.

Learning objectives

  • Describe problem solving strategies
  • Define algorithm and heuristic
  • Explain some common roadblocks to effective problem solving and decision making

Problem-solving strategies

Problems range from simple (doubling a pizza dough recipe) to complex (a broken printer the day a report is due). A problem-solving strategy is a plan of action used to find a solution, and the problem has to be clearly identified first.

  • Trial and error: keep trying different solutions until one works. For the printer: check the ink, then for a paper jam, then the connection. It is common, but rarely the fastest.
  • An algorithm is a problem-solving formula that gives step-by-step instructions to reach a desired outcome (Kahneman, 2011). Like a detailed recipe, it produces the same result every time. Search engines use algorithms to rank results, and Facebook uses them to choose which posts appear in your newsfeed.
  • A heuristic is a general problem-solving framework (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974): a mental shortcut, such as a rule of thumb. Heuristics save time and effort but don’t always produce the most rational decision.
Table 7.3Problem-solving strategies, after OpenStax Table 7.2

People tend to use heuristics in one of five situations (Pratkanis, 1989): when there is too much information; when time is limited; when the decision is unimportant; when there is very little information; or when an appropriate heuristic happens to come to mind.

Two heuristics in action

Working backwards starts from the end result and plans back to the beginning. The book’s example: a wedding in Philadelphia starts at 4:00 PM Saturday. You want to arrive by 3:30 PM, the drive takes 2.5 hours without traffic, and Interstate 95 can back up around Washington, D.C. Work back from the arrival time to find when to leave.

Breaking a task into smaller steps makes a large goal manageable. A research paper, for example, becomes: brainstorm, develop a thesis, research, outline, write a rough draft, revise and edit, write the final draft, organize the references, and proofread.

Everyday connection: solving puzzles

Problem solving improves with practice, and puzzles such as sudoku are a common way to practice. The book offers three: a 4 × 4 sudoku (OpenStax Figure 7.7), a nine-dot puzzle (Figure 7.8) and Sam Loyd’s “Puzzling Scales” (Figure 7.9), which asks how many marbles balance a top. Its answer is nine marbles; see the original figure for the setup.

Pitfalls: mental set and functional fixedness

A mental set is persisting with an approach that worked before even though it clearly isn’t working now. Picture a room with four doorways. One has always been open, but today it is locked; the other three are open. Someone with a mental set keeps trying the locked door.

Functional fixedness is a type of mental set: being unable to see an object as useful for anything other than its intended purpose. In Duncker’s (1945) candle problem, people were given a candle, matches and a box of thumbtacks and asked to fix the candle to the wall so that no wax dripped onto the table below.

  • Apollo 13. After an explosion damaged the spacecraft, carbon dioxide rose to dangerous levels because of filter problems. NASA engineers beat functional fixedness by building a makeshift air filter from spare plastic bags, tape and air hoses.
  • Across cultures. German and Barrett (2005) told the Shuar of Ecuador a story about a bear and a rabbit separated by a river, and asked which object could help them cross. A spoon was the only suitable item, but people chose it more slowly when it was first shown being used as a spoon. Functional fixedness appeared in industrialized and nonindustrialized cultures alike.

Biases in decision making

Our knowledge and reasoning are usually sound, but biases, or other people exploiting them, can push decisions off course. Four friends with a $1,600 rent budget are shown only run-down houses at that price, then a much nicer one for $2,000. The realtor may be using anchoring bias to make the pricier house look like the only good option.

Unit 7 · Section 7.4

What Are Intelligence and Creativity?

Is intelligence one general ability or many? Each theory below answers differently. Creativity, the ability to come up with something new, is closely tied to intelligence.

Learning objectives

  • Define intelligence
  • Explain the triarchic theory of intelligence
  • Identify the difference between intelligence theories
  • Explain emotional intelligence
  • Define creativity

Classifying intelligence: g, fluid and crystallized

The section opens with a father who hears his four-and-a-half-year-old son read “Go, Pig! Go!” from a storybook, though nobody has been teaching him to read. The boy then reads a list the father writes out (mom, dad, dog, bird, bed, truck, car, tree), slowing only on bird and truck. Is this exceptional intelligence, or normal language development? Definitions of intelligence have changed over time.

  • Charles Spearman proposed a single general intelligence factor, g, that can be measured and compared between people. He focused on what intellectual abilities have in common. Ancient philosophers, Aristotle among them, held a similar view (Cianciolo & Sternberg, 2004).
  • In the 1940s Raymond Cattell split general intelligence into two kinds (Cattell, 1963).
Table 7.5Cattell’s two kinds of intelligence, compiled from Section 7.4
Crystallized intelligenceFluid intelligence
What it isAcquired knowledge and the ability to retrieve itSeeing complex relationships and solving problems
HandlesConcrete, straightforward problemsComplex, abstract challenges
ExampleLearning, remembering and recalling course materialFinding your way home after construction forces a detour onto unfamiliar roads

Other theorists define intelligence by what helps a person succeed in life. Reciting the U.S. presidents in order is a good party trick, but does that knowledge make your life better?

Sternberg’s triarchic theory

Robert Sternberg (1988) proposed the triarchic theory of intelligence: intelligence has three facets.

  • Practical intelligence: “street smarts,” finding solutions that work in everyday life by applying knowledge from experience. It may be separate from traditional IQ: someone can be high in practical intelligence but not in analytical or creative intelligence.
  • Analytical intelligence: academic problem solving and computation, which involves analyzing, evaluating, judging, comparing and contrasting.
  • Creative intelligence: inventing or imagining a solution to a problem or situation, including novel solutions to unexpected problems and artistic or literary work.

Gardner’s Multiple Intelligences Theory

Howard Gardner, a Harvard psychologist and former student of Erik Erikson, proposed the Multiple Intelligences Theory: each person has at least eight intelligences.

Cognitive psychologists have criticized the theory heavily for its lack of empirical evidence. Educators still study and use it, and some colleges build it into their classrooms. Gottfredson (2004) suggests one reason for its appeal: it implies that everyone can be smart in some way, an attractive idea in democratic societies.

Emotional intelligence

Emotional intelligence combines Gardner’s interpersonal and intrapersonal intelligences. It includes understanding your own and other people’s emotions, showing empathy, understanding social relationships and cues, regulating your own emotions, and responding in culturally appropriate ways (Parker, Saklofske, & Stough, 2009). People high in emotional intelligence typically have well-developed social skills.

Daniel Goleman, author of Emotional Intelligence: Why It Can Matter More than IQ (1995), argues that it predicts success better than traditional intelligence does. The concept is widely debated: critics point to inconsistent definitions and how hard it is to study empirically (Locke, 2005; Mayer, Salovey, & Caruso, 2004).

The Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) model

The CHC theory is described as the most comprehensive theory of intelligence to date (Schneider & McGrew, 2018). It is a hierarchy with three levels. Only the narrow abilities can be measured directly, but they are integrated within the broader levels above them.

Cultural intelligence

What counts as intelligent varies by culture. On a small island where people fish from boats, an exceptional angler and a skilled boat repairer would be seen as intelligent. Irish culture values hospitality and entertaining stories, so a gifted storyteller is seen as intelligent. In collectivist cultures, the group’s success matters more than individual achievement. How well someone relates to such values is cultural intelligence, also called cultural competence.

Creativity

Creativity is the ability to generate, create or discover new ideas, solutions and possibilities. It is often linked with the arts, but it is a vital form of intelligence in every field, from decorating a home to discovering how a cell works. Very creative people tend to have intense knowledge of a field, years of sustained work on a topic, a drive toward novel solutions, a habit of seeking advice from other experts, and a willingness to take risks.

Everyday connection: creativity is social

Dr. Tom Steitz, who was a Sterling Professor of Biochemistry and Biophysics at Yale, studied the structure of RNA and how RNA interactions could help produce antibiotics and fight disease. He won the 2009 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Steitz stressed good mentorship and constant discussion with colleagues: outstanding discoveries, he wrote, do not happen in a vacuum (Steitz, 2010). Creativity is an individual strength, but it grows through interaction with others.

Unit 7 · Section 7.5

Measures of Intelligence

IQ tests compare a person with a large sample of same-age peers. Scores fall on a bell curve with a mean of 100, and the tests have been both useful and badly misused.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how intelligence tests are developed
  • Describe the history of the use of IQ tests
  • Describe the purposes and benefits of intelligence testing

From Galton to Terman

An intelligence quotient (IQ) is a score on a test designed to measure intelligence. IQ tests have been debated throughout their history. They were used to support eugenics: the U.S. Supreme Court’s Buck v. Bell decision allowed the forced sterilization of people judged “feeble-minded,” and about 65,000 sterilizations followed. Today only trained psychology professionals may give IQ tests, and most tests require an advanced degree in psychology; social workers and psychiatrists cannot administer them.

Terman’s test was given the same way to everyone and scored against an average for each age. Two terms describe this:

  • Standardization: the test is administered, scored and interpreted in a consistent way.
  • Norming: giving the test to a large population so that results can be compared with the group (for example, by age). The resulting norms are reference scores. They describe what a group does know, not what it should know.

Wechsler and the Flynn effect

Wechsler combined subtests from tests used between 1880 and World War I, covering both verbal and nonverbal skills. He defined intelligence as the global capacity of a person to act purposefully, think rationally and deal effectively with the environment. The WISC-V has 14 subtests grouped into five indices, and gives a score for each index plus an overall Full Scale IQ:

  • Verbal Comprehension
  • Visual Spatial
  • Fluid Reasoning
  • Working Memory
  • Processing Speed
  • Full Scale IQ

This scoring reflects the view that intelligence is made of several abilities, and it looks at the mental processes a child used to reach an answer. Because the tests are renormed on thousands of children, today’s children are compared with their same-age peers, not with children from decades ago.

The Flynn effect, named after James Flynn, is the finding that each generation scores significantly higher on IQ tests than the one before. Flynn himself argues that this does not necessarily mean younger generations are more intelligent. Questions about validity remain open: which skills should be tested, how well any test captures intelligence, and how the results should be used (Gresham & Witt, 1997; Flynn et al., 2012; Richardson, 2002; Schlinger, 2003).

The bell curve

IQ scores follow a bell curve, a graph of the normal distribution of a trait in a population. Height is the book’s example: most U.S. women are close to the average height of 5’4″–5’6″, and fewer are near the extremes of 4’11″ or 5’11″ (OpenStax Figure 7.14). Measuring this properly needs a representative sample, a subset of the population that accurately represents it. The women on your school’s basketball team would not be representative; all the women at your school might be.

On most IQ tests the mean is 100, and one standard deviation (a measure of how spread out scores are around the mean) is 15 points. About 68% of people score within one standard deviation of the mean, between 85 and 115 (OpenStax Figure 7.15).

Intellectual disability and giftedness

A score of 70 is about two standard deviations below the mean; only about 2.2% of people score below 70 (APA, 2013). An intellectual disability requires three things: an IQ about two standard deviations below the mean (around 70), major deficits in adaptive functioning, and onset before age 18. It was formerly called “mental retardation.” The DSM-5 lists four subtypes.

Dig deeper: what’s in a name?

“Mental retardation” once replaced harsher terms, but people with intellectual disabilities, their families and professionals came to see it as demeaning and inaccurate. The DSM-5 now uses “intellectual disability,” and many state departments were renamed. Rosa’s Law (2010) moved most U.S. federal agencies to the new term, though some parents objected in the Federal Register that it was imprecise. Does what we call something change how we regard people?

At the other end, about 2% of people score 130 or higher. Such a score, or superior ability in one area, may lead a school to classify a student as gifted. People once believed that very intelligent people were maladjusted. Terman disproved this: his longitudinal study, begun in 1921, followed over 1,500 children with IQs above 135, who grew up to be well-educated, successful, well-adjusted adults, and were even above average in physical build and attractiveness (Terman, 1925; Terman & Oden, 1947). Mensa admits people who score in the top 2%, and some members must pass further exams.

What do you think? Atkins v. Virginia

On August 16, 1996, Daryl Atkins and William Jones robbed, kidnapped and shot Eric Nesbitt, a U.S. Air Force airman. A clinical psychologist testified that Atkins had an IQ of 59 and an intellectual disability; the jury found him guilty and sentenced him to death. In June 2002 the Supreme Court ruled that executing people with intellectual disabilities is cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment, citing clinical definitions that require both subaverage intellectual functioning and significant limits in adaptive skills. States were left to set their own definitions. Atkins was later given an execution date, then a stay after it emerged that co-defendant Jones’s lawyers had coached his testimony; he was resentenced to life in prison. In Hall v. Florida (May 2014), the Court ruled that an IQ score alone cannot decide eligibility for the death penalty. Where should the line be drawn?

Why measure intelligence?

Testing helps identify children with learning or behavioral difficulties, and whether an IQ well above or below their age group’s mean plays a part. Without it, children and adults who need support might go unnoticed. Courts use IQ testing to decide whether a defendant can take part in a trial, and people use results when applying for Social Security disability benefits.

Case study: Candace

Candace, 14, from Connecticut, was referred for a court-ordered evaluation. In ninth grade, in regular classes, she was failing every subject; she had been passed along despite never being a strong student. She cursed at teachers who called on her, fought with other students and occasionally shoplifted. She said she hated school and that teachers called on her only when she didn’t know the answer, which embarrassed her. Her parents felt she was picked on because her ethnicity differed from most teachers’ and students’. Her IQ score was 68. What does that say about her chances in regular classes without help, and why were her difficulties never addressed?

Researchers such as Jack A. Naglieri (2020) continue to make testing more accurate, equitable and useful, and some argue that tests should be tailored to age group, ability and context.

Unit 7 · Section 7.6

The Source of Intelligence

Genes set a range; the environment, especially in childhood, decides where in that range a person ends up. Poverty, biased tests and learning disabilities complicate what an IQ score means.

Learning objectives

  • Describe how genetics and environment affect intelligence
  • Explain the relationship between IQ scores and socioeconomic status
  • Describe the difference between a learning disability and a developmental disorder

High intelligence: nature or nurture?

The section opens with a girl born to teenage parents and raised by her grandmother in a low-income household in rural Mississippi. She could read by age 3. At 6 she moved to Wisconsin to live with her mother, and as a child she was sexually abused by relatives; as a young teen she struggled. She was then sent to her father in Nashville, whose strict expectations helped her settle down and succeed in school. At 19 she became the youngest and first African American female news anchor. She is Oprah Winfrey, known for her intelligence and empathy. Where did that intelligence come from?

  • Genes. Twin studies test heritability. In the Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart, identical twins, whether raised together or apart, had more similar IQs than siblings or fraternal twins raised together (Bouchard et al., 1990).
  • Environment. Others argue that the environment a child grows up in shapes intelligence, and that intellectual stimulation, even before birth, may raise it.
  • Both. Bartels et al. (2002) found that genetics seem to control the level of intelligence, while the environment provides the stability and change that trigger cognitive abilities. Behaviors that support intelligence matter, but the genetic part should not be ignored.

Range of reaction

Range of reaction is the idea that each person responds to the environment in a unique way, based on their genetic makeup. Your genetic potential is fixed, but whether you reach your full intellectual potential depends on the stimulation your environment provides, especially in childhood. An adopted child with average genetic potential, raised in a highly stimulating home, would likely gain intellectually; a child with strong potential in an unstimulating home may not reach it.

In a longitudinal study of highly gifted people, Csikszentmihalyi and Csikszentmihalyi (1993) found that both extremes of upbringing, optimal and pathological, were overrepresented in the backgrounds of creative people; those from supportive families were more likely to report being happy.

Confounding social factors and test bias

Some ethnic groups score higher on IQ tests than others, but the book says this likely reflects factors other than the quality of each group’s intellect. Children living in poverty face constant stress about safety, shelter and food, and that stress can affect brain functioning and development, lowering IQ scores. Kishiyama et al. (2009) found reduced prefrontal brain functioning in children living in poverty, comparable to that of children with damage to the lateral prefrontal cortex.

In 1969 Arthur Jensen published “How Much Can We Boost I.Q. and Achievement” in the Harvard Educational Review. He attributed IQ to genetics and proposed two levels of ability: Level I (rote memorization), which he saw as consistent across humanity, and Level II (conceptual and analytical ability), on which he claimed ethnic groups differ. Robert Williams (1970) challenged the racial bias in Jensen’s results. The book notes that Jensen’s work is not viewed as objective, and that his later statements, including an interview with the White nationalist publication American Renaissance, revealed racist views. His conclusions rest on his belief that intelligence is inherited and that IQ tests are its truest measure.

Larry P. v. Riles (1979)

Parents of African American students sued California, arguing that the tests used to place students in classes for learning disabilities were culturally unfair because they were normed on White children. The tests disproportionately labeled African American children “mentally retarded.” The court found the tests and placement methods biased, with Black children heavily overrepresented in special classes, in violation of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 and the Education for All Handicapped Children Act of 1975. The case exposed the limits of intelligence testing.

What are learning disabilities?

Learning disabilities are cognitive disorders that affect specific areas such as language or reading. They are not intellectual disabilities: they are specific neurological impairments, not global intellectual or developmental disabilities. They are often not recognized until a child reaches school, and they most often affect children of average to above-average intelligence. They frequently co-occur with ADHD: Riccio et al. (1994) found that 30–70% of people diagnosed with ADHD also have a learning disability.

Key terms

All 52 Chapter 7 key terms in one place, grouped by the section that introduces them.

Key terms 52 terms

7.1 What Is Cognition?

artificial concept
concept defined by a very specific set of characteristics
cognition
thinking, including perception, learning, problem solving, judgment and memory
cognitive psychology
field of psychology dedicated to studying every aspect of how people think
cognitive script
set of behaviors performed the same way each time; also called an event schema
concept
category or grouping of linguistic information, objects, ideas or life experiences
event schema
set of behaviors performed the same way each time; also called a cognitive script
natural concept
mental grouping created “naturally” through your experiences
prototype
best representation of a concept
role schema
set of expectations that define the behaviors of a person occupying a particular role
schema
(plural: schemata) mental construct consisting of a cluster or collection of related concepts

7.2 Language

grammar
set of rules used to convey meaning through the use of a lexicon
language
communication system that involves using words to transmit information from one individual to another
lexicon
the words of a given language
morpheme
smallest unit of language that conveys some type of meaning
overgeneralization
extension of a rule that exists in a given language to an exception to the rule
phoneme
basic unit of sound in a given language
semantics
process by which we derive meaning from morphemes and words
syntax
manner by which words are organized into sentences

7.3 Problem Solving

algorithm
problem-solving strategy of step-by-step instructions that lead to a solution
anchoring bias
faulty heuristic in which you fixate on a single aspect of a problem to find a solution
availability heuristic
faulty heuristic in which you decide based on information readily available to you
confirmation bias
faulty heuristic in which you focus on information that confirms your beliefs
functional fixedness
inability to see an object as useful for any other use than the one for which it was intended
heuristic
mental shortcut that saves time when solving a problem
hindsight bias
belief that the event just experienced was predictable, even though it really wasn’t
mental set
continually using an old solution to a problem without results
problem-solving strategy
method for solving problems
representative bias
faulty heuristic in which you stereotype someone or something without a valid basis for your judgment
trial and error
problem-solving strategy in which multiple solutions are attempted until the correct one is found
working backwards
heuristic in which you begin to solve a problem by focusing on the end result

7.4 What Are Intelligence and Creativity?

analytical intelligence
aligned with academic problem solving and computations
convergent thinking
providing correct or established answers to problems
creative intelligence
ability to produce new products, ideas or novel solutions
creativity
ability to generate, create or discover new ideas, solutions and possibilities
crystallized intelligence
characterized by acquired knowledge and the ability to retrieve it
cultural intelligence
ability to relate to and understand people from another culture
divergent thinking
ability to think “outside the box” to arrive at novel solutions to a problem
emotional intelligence
ability to understand emotions and motivations in yourself and others
fluid intelligence
ability to see complex relationships and solve problems
Multiple Intelligences Theory
Gardner’s theory that each person has at least eight types of intelligence
practical intelligence
aka “street smarts”
triarchic theory of intelligence
Sternberg’s theory that intelligence has three facets: practical, creative and analytical

7.5 Measures of Intelligence

Flynn effect
observation that each generation has a significantly higher IQ than the previous one
intelligence quotient (IQ)
score on a test designed to measure intelligence
norming
administering a test to a large population so data can be collected and normal scores set for the population and its groups
representative sample
subset of the population that accurately represents the general population
standard deviation
measure of variability that describes how far scores are spread from their mean
standardization
method of testing in which administration, scoring and interpretation are consistent

7.6 The Source of Intelligence

dyscalculia
learning disability that causes difficulty in learning or comprehending mathematics
dysgraphia
learning disability that causes extreme difficulty in writing legibly
dyslexia
common learning disability in which letters are not processed properly by the brain
range of reaction
each person’s response to the environment is unique, based on their genetic makeup
