Unit 8 · Overview

Memory

How information gets into memory, how it is stored and pulled back out, which parts of the brain are involved, why memory fails, and what you can do to remember better.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening questions

The chapter opens with a photograph of a camera and a pile of prints: photos can trigger memories and bring past experiences back to life (OpenStax Figure 8.1). Being a good learner is of little use without a way to store what you learn.

Imagine a day with no memory at all. You would not know how to get dressed, brush your teeth or tie your shoes, you would not recognize the people in your house, and you would not know where the food is kept or how to cook it. The chapter asks: How do we process and store information? Are there different kinds of memory? How do we retrieve memories, and why do we forget?

Unit 8 · Section 8.1

How Memory Functions

Memory works like an information-processing system: information is put in, kept, and taken back out.

Learning objectives

  • Discuss the three basic functions of memory
  • Describe the three stages of memory storage
  • Describe and distinguish between procedural and declarative memory and semantic and episodic memory
Read Section 8.1 on OpenStax ↗

Three functions of memory

Because memory processes information, it is often compared to a computer. Memory is the set of processes used to encode, store and retrieve information over different periods of time.

Encoding

Encoding is the input of information into the memory system. The brain labels incoming sensory information, groups it with similar information and links new concepts to existing ones. It happens in two ways:

  • Automatic processing encodes details such as time, space, frequency and the meaning of words, usually without conscious awareness. You can easily say what you ate for lunch, or when you last studied for a test.
  • Effortful processing takes work and attention, like encoding the material you studied for that test. Learning to drive starts as effortful processing (how to start the car, brake and turn); once the skill is learned, new details about it are encoded automatically (OpenStax Figure 8.3).

Meaning makes encoding stronger

Even a simple sentence is easier to recall when it is meaningful (Anderson, 1984). Try the demonstration the chapter adapts from Bransford and McCarrell (1974): read the sentences, look away, count backwards from 30 by threes, and write them down. Then switch on the context and repeat.

Which type works best for verbal information? Craik and Tulving (1975) gave participants words with questions that forced processing at one of three levels: the font of the letters (visual), the sound or rhyme (acoustic) or the meaning (semantic). On a surprise recall or recognition test, semantically encoded words were remembered best. Semantic encoding is a deeper level of processing than the shallower visual or acoustic encoding.

Semantic encoding is strongest when you use the self-reference effect: people remember information that relates to themselves better than material with less personal relevance (Rogers, Kuiper, & Kirker, 1977).

Storage: two models

Storage is the creation of a permanent record of information. In the Atkinson-Shiffrin model (1968), a memory must pass through three stages to reach long-term storage: sensory memory, short-term memory and long-term memory. The model assumes we process memories the way a computer processes information. Select a stage to see its details.

A second model, the working memory model of Baddeley and Hitch (1974), says short-term memory has different forms. Storing information there is like opening files on a computer: each file holds a limited amount, and the kind of file depends on the kind of information.

Sensory and short-term memory

Sensory memory is very brief storage, up to a couple of seconds. Short-term memory (STM) is a temporary storage system that processes incoming sensory memory and sometimes links it to something already in long-term memory. It lasts 15 to 30 seconds. STM and working memory are sometimes used as synonyms, but STM is more accurately one component of working memory.

Rehearsal

Rehearsal moves information from STM to long-term memory.

  • Active rehearsal is repeating (practicing) the information, the way children learn their ABCs by singing the alphabet song.
  • Elaborative rehearsal links new information to what you already know. To remember a new friend’s number that starts with area code 203, you link it to your uncle in Connecticut, who has the same area code.

Craik and Lockhart (1972) proposed the levels of processing hypothesis: the more deeply you think about something, the better you remember it.

How much can STM hold?

Test yourself with the digit strings from OpenStax Figure 8.5. In the book a partner reads each string aloud; here the string flashes on screen instead. Note the longest string you recall correctly.

In 1956, George Miller reviewed research on STM capacity and found people retain between 5 and 9 items, the “magic number” 7 plus or minus 2. More recent research puts working memory capacity at 4 plus or minus 1 (Cowan, 2010). Recall is somewhat better for random numbers than random letters (Jacobs, 1887), and often slightly better for information we hear than information we see (Anderson, 1969).

Decay and interference

Two factors limit STM retention. Peterson and Peterson (1959) asked people to recall three-letter trigrams (such as CLS) after delays between 3 and 18 seconds.

  • Memory trace decay: the trace becomes less activated over time and the information is forgotten. The Petersons concluded STM decays in 18 seconds.
  • Proactive interference: earlier learning gets in the way of new learning. Keppel and Underwood (1962) looked only at the first trials of the trigram task and found that interference also affected retention.

Once information reaches long-term memory it must be consolidated, both at the synapse (a few hours) and into the memory system (weeks or longer).

Long-term memory and semantic networks

Long-term memory (LTM) is the continuous storage of information; its capacity is believed to be unlimited. LTM is generally thought to be organized in semantic (associative) networks (Collins & Loftus, 1975). A network is made of concepts, arranged hierarchically, and related concepts are linked. The more often two concepts are associated, the stronger the link: hear “peanut butter” and you probably think “jelly.”

Activating one part of a network also partly activates the concepts linked to it, which makes them easier to recall. This is spreading activation. Activation spreads in many directions, so the more links you have to a concept, the more routes you have to it.

Explicit and implicit memory

LTM has two parts. The difference matters because aging, some brain injuries and some disorders affect them differently.

Explicit memory

Explicit memory is what we consciously try to remember, recall and report, like the material you study for a chemistry exam. In the computer analogy it is a file on the hard drive: not on the desktop, but usually easy to open. Some explicit memories need a prompt: you might not recall a restaurant’s name until told it was named after its owner. Because it can be put into words, explicit memory is also called declarative memory. It has two kinds:

  • Episodic memory is information about events you personally experienced, such as your last birthday. It is usually told as a story. Tulving (1972) first proposed it; it is now seen as memory for the what, where and when of an event (Tulving, 2002), involving visual imagery and a feeling of familiarity (Hassabis & Maguire, 2007).
  • Semantic memory is knowledge of words, concepts and language-based facts, usually reported as facts: the definition of psychology, or who the first African American president of the United States was.

Everyday connection: remembering everything

Episodic memories are also called autobiographical memories. Few people could say what they wore five years ago today. Actress Marilu Henner, known for the TV show Taxi, can: she has highly superior autobiographical memory, called hyperthymesia (OpenStax Figure 8.7). Fewer than 20 people have been identified with it and only a few studied (Parker, Cahill, & McGaugh, 2006). It normally appears in adolescence, though two U.S. children seem to have such memories from well before age 10.

Implicit memory

Implicit memory is long-term memory that is not part of our consciousness. It is learned outside awareness and cannot be consciously recalled, but it shows in performance (Roediger, 1990; Schacter, 1987), like a program running in the background. It has three kinds:

  • Procedural memory stores how to do things: skilled actions like brushing your teeth, riding a bike or driving. You stay balanced on a bike without being able to explain exactly how.
  • Priming: exposure to one stimulus affects the response to a later one (Schacter, 1992). Try it below.
  • Emotional conditioning: classically conditioned emotional responses (Olson & Fazio, 2001), such as a smell or a song that makes you feel nostalgic without your knowing why.

Retrieval

Retrieval is getting information out of memory storage and back into conscious awareness, like reopening a saved paper. Everything from brushing your hair to doing your job depends on it. There are three ways to retrieve from LTM:

Parts of the Brain Involved with Memory

Is memory stored in one place or spread across the brain? A century of research points to a few key structures working together.

  • Explain the brain functions involved in memory
  • Recognize the roles of the hippocampus, amygdala, and cerebellum

The search for the engram

About 100 years ago, Karl Lashley went looking for the engram: the group of neurons that serves as the physical representation of a memory (Josselyn, 2010). He trained rats to run a maze, then used a soldering iron to make lesions in their cerebral cortex, trying to erase the memory trace (Lashley, 1950).

The rats still found their way, whatever the size or place of the lesion. Lashley proposed the equipotentiality hypothesis: if part of a brain area involved in memory is damaged, another part of the same area can take over. He never found the engram, but modern researchers are getting closer. Eric Kandel, for example, has spent decades studying how synapses control the flow of information through the circuits that store memories (Mayford, Siegelbaum, & Kandel, 2012).

Four key structures

Many scientists think the whole brain is involved in memory, but others argue that memory is located in specific parts, with specific neurons involved in forming memories. The main parts are the amygdala, hippocampus, cerebellum and prefrontal cortex.

The amygdala

In one study, rats heard a neutral tone paired with a foot shock (Pavlovian conditioning) and learned to freeze at the tone. When researchers killed neurons in the lateral amygdala, the area responsible for fear memories, the fear memory faded (Josselyn, 2010). Because it processes emotion, the amygdala also takes part in memory consolidation, the transfer of new learning into long-term memory.

The hippocampus

Rats with hippocampal lesions showed impaired object recognition and maze running, so the hippocampus supports normal recognition memory and spatial memory (Clark, Zola, & Squire, 2000). The famous patient H. M. had both temporal lobes (hippocampi) removed to control severe seizures (Corkin et al., 1997). Afterwards he could remember events from before the surgery but could no longer form new declarative memories or new semantic knowledge.

The cerebellum and prefrontal cortex

Even without a hippocampus you can still form implicit memories (procedural memory, motor learning and classical conditioning) thanks to the cerebellum. Rabbits with a damaged cerebellum could not learn to blink to a conditioned cue that predicted a puff of air (Steinmetz, 1999; Green & Woodruff-Pak, 2000).

PET studies point to the prefrontal cortex. When people either looked for the letter a in words (perceptual task) or sorted nouns as living or non-living (semantic task), recall was much better after the semantic task, which activated the left inferior prefrontal cortex far more (Kapur et al., 1994). Another study linked encoding to left frontal activity and retrieval to the right frontal region (Craik et al., 1999).

Neurotransmitters and arousal

Several neurotransmitters are involved in memory, including epinephrine, dopamine, serotonin, glutamate and acetylcholine (Myhrer, 2003). Researchers still debate which plays which role, but communication between neurons is critical for new memories: repeated activity raises neurotransmitter levels in the synapses and builds more, and more efficient, connections. This is how consolidation occurs.

Arousal theory holds that strong emotions form strong memories and weaker emotional experiences form weaker ones (Christianson, 1992). Strong emotion releases neurotransmitters and hormones that strengthen memory; under stress the brain releases more glutamate, which helps us remember the stressful event (McGaugh, 2003).

Flashbulb memories and false memories

A flashbulb memory is an exceptionally clear recollection of an important event. A Pew Research Center (2011) survey found that 97% of Americans who were at least 8 years old on 9/11 could recall the moment they learned of the attacks, a decade later (OpenStax Figure 8.9). The event does not have to be shared widely (Brown & Kulik, 1977; Hirst & Phelps, 2016): a family or a school can hold flashbulb memories of its own events, and positive events can produce them too.

Dig deeper: inaccurate and false memories

Flashbulb memories lose accuracy over time. On at least three occasions President George W. Bush described inaccurately how he heard about 9/11; in January 2002 he said he had seen the first plane hit on TV before entering a classroom (Greenberg, 2004). In fact no live broadcast showed the first plane hitting.

Memory is not a video recording. The parts of a memory (time, visual details, smells) are stored in different places and must be put back together, which is memory reconstruction, and each part is a chance for error. A false memory is remembering something that did not happen: participants have recalled hearing words they never heard (Roediger & McDermott, 2000).

Amnesia

Amnesia is the loss of long-term memory caused by disease, physical trauma or psychological trauma. Endel Tulving (2002) studied K. C., who had severe amnesia after a motorcycle accident: he could not remember any event from his own life, from birth onward, apart from the last minute or two.

Anterograde amnesia suggests the damage blocks the transfer of information from short-term to long-term memory, that is, it blocks consolidation. Hollywood has used the retrograde-amnesia plot for about a century, from Garden of Lies (1915) to the Jason Bourne films.

Construction, reconstruction and suggestibility

Forming new memories is sometimes called construction; bringing up old memories is reconstruction. A memory pulled from long-term into short-term memory is flexible: new events can be added and details changed, producing distortions even when no one intends them (Roediger & DeSoto, 2015).

Suggestibility is the effect of misinformation from outside sources that leads to false memories. During the 2002 sniper attacks around Washington, D.C., ten people were killed over three weeks. Police received over 140,000 tips (Newseum, n.d.). After a white van was seen at one shooting and the police chief showed a picture of it on national TV, more eyewitnesses reported a white van, and police and public focused on white vans (there were over 70,000 in the area). The suspects were caught driving a blue sedan.

Eyewitness misidentification

Police, prosecutors and courts still rely heavily on eyewitness identification, but faulty eyewitness testimony can lead to wrongful convictions.

The Ronald Cotton case

In 1984, Jennifer Thompson, a 22-year-old college student in North Carolina, was raped at knifepoint and tried to memorize her attacker’s face. Shown six photos, she hesitated before choosing Ronald Cotton, saying she thought he was the man; the detective then told her she had done great. In a later lineup of seven men she wavered between two before saying Cotton looked most like him. By the trial she had no doubt, and her testimony helped convict him.

Gary Wells and Deah Quinlivan (2009) blame suggestive police identification procedures: lineups stacked so the suspect stands out, telling witnesses whom to pick, and confirming their choice afterwards. Cotton was sentenced to life plus 50 years, then to two life sentences at a retrial. After 11 years in prison, DNA evidence showed he was innocent. The Innocence Project, a non-profit, works to exonerate people wrongly convicted, including through eyewitness testimony.

Dig deeper: preserving eyewitness memory

When 14-year-old Elizabeth Smart was abducted from her bed, her 9-year-old sister Mary Katherine was the only witness. Salt Lake City police and the FBI deliberately avoided lineups and composite sketches so they would not implant false memories. About 4 months later Mary Katherine recalled that she had heard the abductor’s voice before: he had worked one day as a handyman at their home. She named him, the press spread his picture, and after nine months in total he was caught and Elizabeth returned home.

The misinformation effect

Cognitive psychologist Elizabeth Loftus developed the misinformation effect paradigm: after exposure to additional, possibly inaccurate information, a person may misremember the original event. Loftus and Palmer (1974) showed 45 U.S. college students films of car accidents and asked how fast the cars were going when they “smashed,” “collided,” “bumped,” “hit” or “contacted” each other.

A week later, participants were asked whether they had seen broken glass. There was none in the film, but people from the “smashed” group were more than twice as likely to say they had. A leading question changed both the speed they remembered and what they remembered seeing.

Repressed and recovered memories

Whole events, not just details, can be falsely recalled. The idea that traumatic memories can be repressed goes back to Sigmund Freud and is still debated. Recall of false autobiographical memories is called false memory syndrome; it draws attention especially for abuse with no independent witnesses.

One side

Memories can be repressed and recovered

Some researchers argue that very traumatic childhood experiences are locked away and can be recovered intact decades later, through hypnosis and guided imagery (Devilly, 2007). In Briere and Conte (1993), 59% of 450 adults in treatment for abuse before age 18 had forgotten it. Cheit (2007) linked repression to distress in adulthood; the Recovered Memory Project was set up to help victims recall.

Other side

Recovered memories may be false

Loftus does not deny that abuse happens, but questions whether recovered memories are accurate, given how easily suggestion during therapy (hypnosis, guided visualization, age regression) creates misinformation effects. Ceci and Bruck (1993, 1995) found 55% of three-year-olds pointed to genital or anal areas on a doll when asked where a doctor had touched them, though none had had a genital exam.

Since Loftus’s work in the 1970s, practice has changed: interviewers use neutral, non-leading language, which helps children recall more accurately (Goodman, 2006; Pipe et al., 2004); blind photo lineups are recommended, so the officer running the lineup does not know which photo is the suspect; some judges warn jurors about misidentification; and judges can suppress unreliable eyewitness testimony.

Forgetting and encoding failure

Forgetting is the loss of information from long-term memory. Sometimes the loss happens before memory even begins: this is encoding failure. You cannot remember what you never stored, like looking for an e-book you never downloaded. Nickerson and Adams (1979) found most Americans cannot pick out the correct drawing of a U.S. penny: we encode only enough to tell it from other coins. OpenStax Figure 8.13 tries the same test with four drawings of a nickel (the correct one is c).

Forgetting errors

Transience means memories fade with time. Nathan’s mother loved To Kill a Mockingbird in high school but now recalls only that Scout is a main character and her father is an attorney. This is storage decay: unused information fades.

In 1885, Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of nonsense syllables, then measured how much he retained when relearning them, from 20 minutes to 30 days later. His forgetting curve shows that memory for new information drops fast and then levels off: on average about 50% is lost after 20 minutes and 70% after 24 hours (Ebbinghaus, 1885/1964).

Absentmindedness is a lapse in memory caused by a break in attention, like losing your phone or walking into a room and forgetting why. A psychologist rushing to collect her sick daughter could not later remember whether she had returned her court ID card (she had).

Blocking means you cannot access stored information even though it feels close. It is also called the tip-of-the-tongue (TOT) phenomenon: you can picture the actor from Oblivion and The Shawshank Redemption and hear his voice, but the name Morgan Freeman will not come (OpenStax Figure 8.15).

Distortion errors

  • Misattribution: confusing the source of a memory. Alejandra saw the second Hobbit film with someone else but “remembers” seeing it with Lucia. Eyewitness expert Donald Thomson was identified by a rape victim who had been watching his live TV interview during the attack; she confused the attacker’s face with his (Baddeley, 2004).
  • Suggestibility: also a false memory, but planted by someone else, such as a therapist or police officer asking leading questions. With misattribution you create the false memory yourself.
  • Bias: feelings and world view distort memories of the past (Schacter, 2001). Stereotypical bias involves racial and gender bias: participants misremembered typical African American names as basketball players and typical White names as politicians (Payne, Jacoby, & Lambert, 2004). Egocentric bias enhances our own past (did you score the goal, or assist?). Hindsight bias is the “I knew it all along” feeling that an outcome was inevitable (Carli, 1999).

Intrusion

Persistence is the involuntary recall of unwanted, often unpleasant, memories that interfere with concentration: a song stuck in your head, a horrific car accident you witnessed on the way to work, or the unwanted memories many military veterans live with (OpenStax Figure 8.16).

Practice: name the sin

Interference

Sometimes information is stored but cannot be reached because other information gets in the way. This is interference, and its direction gives its name.

  • Proactive interference: old information hinders recall of newly learned information. You give out your old phone number or address, or write last year’s date in January.
  • Retroactive interference: recently learned information hinders recall of older information. After studying Erikson’s theory of psychosocial development next week, you struggle to recall this week’s Ebbinghaus forgetting curve.

Memory-enhancing strategies

A memory-enhancing strategy is a technique that helps move information from short-term to long-term memory.

  • Rehearsal: conscious repetition of the information (Craik & Watkins, 1973), as when you memorized 6 × 6 = 36, 6 × 7 = 42 and 6 × 8 = 48.
  • Chunking: organizing information into manageable bits or chunks (Bodie, Powers, & Fitch-Hauser, 2006), useful for dates and phone numbers.
  • Elaborative rehearsal: thinking about the meaning of new information and how it relates to what you already know, while also repeating it (Tigner, 1999). If the person you met is from Arizona, remembering that 520 is an Arizona area code helps you keep the number.

Mnemonic devices

Mnemonic devices are memory aids that help organize information for encoding. They are especially useful for larger sets of information such as steps, stages, phases and parts of a system (Bellezza, 1981). The more vivid or unusual the mnemonic, the easier it is to remember, and the key is finding one that works for you.

Expressive writing and saying words aloud

  • Expressive writing: Yogo and Fujihara (2008) had people write for 20-minute sessions several times a month about a traumatic experience, their best possible future self, or a trivial topic. After five weeks, short-term memory capacity rose, but only for those who wrote about trauma. Psychologists cannot yet explain why.
  • Saying words aloud: saying a word out loud makes it more distinctive and easier to remember (MacLeod, Gopie, Hourihan, Neary, & Ozubko, 2010). Just mouthing the words works equally well. These techniques raised memory for the words by more than 10%.

How to study effectively

The chapter turns its findings into nine study strategies (OpenStax Figure 8.19 shows students studying). Each one links back to an idea from earlier in this unit.

Levels of processing

Use elaborative rehearsal

Think about material deeply and link it to other memories (Craik & Lockhart, 1972). Picture a hippopotamus with an excellent memory to remember the hippocampus.

Self-reference effect

Make it personal

Rewrite definitions in your own words and relate concepts to your life or other classes. This builds a web of retrieval cues.

Consolidation

Use distributed practice

Study in short sessions across time instead of cramming. Consolidation takes time, and cramming can leave you stuck in one over-active link.

Rehearsal

Rehearse, rehearse, rehearse

Review in spaced, organized sessions, take practice quizzes, and link new material to what you already know well.

Efficiency

Study efficiently

Swap highlighting for index cards: question on one side, answer on the other. Keep re-studying the pile you got wrong until every card is right.

Interference

Be aware of interference

Study at a quiet time without interruptions or distractions such as TV or music.

Neurogenesis

Keep moving

Regular aerobic exercise benefits memory (van Praag, 2008); it promotes the growth of new brain cells in the hippocampus.

Sleep

Get enough sleep

During sleep the brain organizes and consolidates information for long-term storage (Abel & Bäuml, 2013).

Mnemonics

Use mnemonic devices

Acronyms, acrostics and jingles (Table 8.5) help you recall lists and sequences.
