Unit 9 · Overview

Lifespan Development

How people change, and stay the same, from conception to death: the questions developmental psychologists ask, the theories they use, what happens at each stage of life, and how we face its end.

Read the chapter introduction on OpenStax ↗

The opening questions

The chapter opens with a photograph of an infant’s tiny hand wrapped around an adult’s finger (OpenStax Figure 9.1) and a set of questions: How have you changed since childhood? How are you the same? What will your life be like 25 or 50 years from now?

Growth is most obvious in infancy and childhood, but development is happening right now and continues for the rest of your life. Who you are, and who you will become, depends on a blend of genetics, environment, culture and relationships. The chapter follows physical, cognitive and psychosocial development “from the womb to the tomb.”

What Is Lifespan Development?

Developmental psychologists study how humans change and grow from conception through childhood, adolescence, adulthood and death.

Learning objectives

  • Define and distinguish between the three domains of development: physical, cognitive and psychosocial
  • Discuss the normative approach to development
  • Understand the three major issues in development: continuity and discontinuity, one common course of development or many unique courses of development, and nature versus nurture

Three domains of development

The section opens with William Wordsworth’s line “the child is father of the man” (1802): the adult you are depends largely on the experiences you had as a child. How much is the adult shaped by the child, and how different is a child from the adult they become? Developmental psychologists treat development as a lifelong process that can be studied scientifically across three domains.

Research methods in developmental psychology

The normative approach

The normative approach asks, “What is normal development?” In the early 20th century, normative psychologists studied large numbers of children to find norms, the average ages at which most children reach specific developmental milestones: crawling, walking, writing, dressing, naming colors, speaking in sentences and starting puberty (Gesell, 1933, 1939, 1940; Gesell & Ilg, 1946; Hall, 1904). Children develop at slightly different rates, so milestones are guidelines for comparing a child with same-age peers. The terms “normal” and “normative” are still used, but they deserve careful thought, and some researchers avoid them.

  • Universal milestones, mostly biological, are experienced by everyone: puberty.
  • Social milestones are not universal; they affect most people within one culture. Some countries start school around age 5 or 6, others later, and UNESCO estimates that nearly 244 million school-aged children worldwide still have no access to school (Antoninis, 2022).

Three big issues in development

Every theory in this chapter answers three questions about change.

1. Continuous or discontinuous?

Continuous development is a cumulative process, gradually improving on existing skills, like a child adding inches of height year by year. Discontinuous development happens in unique stages at specific times or ages; the change is more sudden, like an infant suddenly grasping object permanence.

2. One course of development or many?

Is development the same for every child, or does each child follow a different course depending on genes and environment? Stage theories say the sequence is universal. In cross-cultural studies of language, children everywhere coo before they babble, start babbling at about the same age and say their first word around 12 months (Gleitman & Newport, 1995). But child-care practices vary by culture and can speed up or slow down motor milestones such as sitting, crawling and walking (Karasik, Adolph, Tamis-LeMonda, & Bornstein, 2010).

3. Nature or nurture?

Are we who we are because of nature (genes and biology) or nurture (environment and culture)? We inherit traits such as eye color, height and some personality traits, but genes and environment interact: our experiences influence whether and how traits are expressed, and our genes influence how we interact with the environment (Diamond, 2009; Lobo, 2008). The chapter’s answer is a reciprocal interaction, though the debate about each one’s relative contribution continues.

Dig deeper: the achievement gap

The achievement gap is the persistent difference in grades, test scores and graduation rates between students of different ethnicities, races and, in some subjects, genders (Winerman, 2011). Research links it strongly to socioeconomic differences between families (Duncan & Magnuson, 2005). Children from low-income households have lower test scores, graduation rates and college entrance rates, and higher dropout rates. But the problem may start before school.

All the parents in Hart and Risley’s longitudinal study engaged with their children, but middle- and high-income parents talked to them more, starting in infancy. Solutions exist: University of Chicago experts visit low-income families at home to encourage more talk, and preschools that mix children from different economic backgrounds have produced significant language gains for children with lower socioeconomic status (Schechter & Byeb, 2007).

Freud: psychosexual theory

Sigmund Freud (1856–1939) believed personality develops during early childhood, and that childhood experiences shape adult personality and behavior. He saw development as discontinuous: in psychosexual development, pleasure-seeking urges focus on a different erogenous zone at each of five stages. A child who lacks proper nurturance during a stage may become stuck, or fixated, there.

  1. 1 · Oral
  2. 2 · Anal
  3. 3 · Phallic
  4. 4 · Latency
  5. 5 · Genital

Most of Freud’s ideas have not found support in modern research, and psychologists dispute the stages as an explanation of personality. What remains is the idea that personality is shaped, in part, by childhood experience. The stages are covered in detail in the personality chapter (Unit 11).

Erikson: psychosocial theory

Erik Erikson (1902–1994), another stage theorist (OpenStax Figure 9.4), modified Freud’s theory to stress the social rather than sexual nature of development, and he extended it across the whole lifespan. How we interact with others shapes our sense of self, our ego identity. In psychosocial development, each of eight stages poses a conflict, or task. Resolving it brings a sense of competence and a healthy personality; failing leads to feelings of inadequacy. Select a stage.

Piaget: cognitive theory

Jean Piaget (1896–1980) spent over 50 years studying how children’s minds develop (OpenStax Figure 9.5). He believed thinking is central to development, that children are naturally inquisitive, and that they do not think and reason like adults (Piaget, 1930, 1932). His stages are another example of discontinuous development: each new stage brings a distinct shift in thinking.

Schemata, assimilation and accommodation

Children build schemata (singular schema): concepts, or mental models, used to categorize and interpret information. New information adjusts them in two ways. Assimilation takes in information similar to what is already known. Accommodation changes the schema to fit new information that is different.

Four stages

The ages are approximate. The text gives the preoperational stage as about 2 to 7 years and the formal operational stage as about 11 to adulthood, slightly different from the table.

  1. Sensorimotor (birth to about 2). Infants learn through their senses and motor behavior: mouthing objects, shaking and banging them. Between 5 and 8 months they develop object permanence, knowing that something out of sight still exists (Bogartz, Shinskey, & Schilling, 2000). In Piaget’s test, a toy is hidden under a blanket: infants with object permanence reach for it; those without seem confused. Around the same time comes stranger anxiety, a fear of unfamiliar people, because the baby cannot assimilate the stranger into an existing schema.
  2. Preoperational (about 2 to 7). Children use symbols, so they engage in pretend play (arms become airplane wings, a stick becomes a sword) and begin to use language, but they cannot mentally manipulate information (“operational” means logical manipulation). They lack conservation: 3-year-old Kenny thinks his pizza slice, cut into five pieces, is more than his sister Keiko’s. They also show egocentrism, difficulty taking others’ perspective: Kenny picks an Iron Man figure as Keiko’s present because he likes it. Piaget tested egocentrism with the Three-Mountain Task.
  3. Concrete operational (about 7 to 11). Children think logically about real, concrete events, use numbers and memory strategies, and understand transformations (addition undoes subtraction). They master conservation and reversibility: something can be changed and then returned to its original form.
  4. Formal operational (about 11 to adulthood). Teens can handle abstract ideas and hypothetical situations, test alternative solutions, and show a renewed egocentrism: a 15-year-old is sure everyone notices her very small pimple.

Beyond formal operational thought

Later research has challenged parts of Piaget’s theory:

  • Several studies support a more continuous model than his discrete stages (Courage & Howe, 2002; Siegler, 2005, 2006).
  • Many find that children reach cognitive milestones earlier than he described (Baillargeon, 2004; de Hevia & Spelke, 2010). See Baillargeon’s truck study in Section 9.3.
  • Many developmental psychologists propose a fifth, postformal stage (Basseches, 1984; Commons & Bresette, 2006; Sinnott, 1998). Decisions depend on situations and circumstances, and logic is integrated with emotion. Adults draw on past experience and think more deeply about relationships, work and politics (Labouvie-Vief & Diehl, 1999). The best way to settle a disagreement with a colleague may not be the best way with a significant other.

Connect the concepts: neuroconstructivism

More recent theories build on the two-way influence of genes and environment (Newcombe, 2011). Neuroconstructivism says neural development shapes cognitive development, and experience changes how neural pathways develop. Neural and cognitive networks interact at and between five levels: genes, neurons, brain, body and social environment (Westermann et al., 2007).

Example: a child predisposed to a difficult temperament has parents who encourage healthy self-expression. The brain forms connections enhanced by that environment, which can soften temperament (genes), make the body less prone to high blood pressure, and lead the child to seek similar people (social environment).

Vygotsky: sociocultural theory

Russian psychologist Lev Vygotsky proposed that development is rooted in culture. A child’s social world forms the basis of language and thought, and the language one speaks and the way one thinks depend on cultural background. He also saw historical influences as key, and was interested in the process of development and the individual’s interactions with the environment (John-Steiner & Mahn, 1996).

Kohlberg: moral theory

Lawrence Kohlberg (1927–1987) extended Piaget’s work: moral development, like cognitive development, follows stages (stages of moral reasoning). He posed moral dilemmas to people of all ages and analyzed their answers. Try his best-known one before reading on.

In Europe, a woman was near death from a special kind of cancer. A druggist in her town had discovered a form of radium that might save her. It cost him $200 to make, but he charged $2,000 for a small dose. Her husband, Heinz, could borrow only about $1,000. The druggist refused to sell it cheaper or let him pay later. So Heinz broke into the store and stole the drug for his wife. Should he have done that?

Condensed from Kohlberg (1969, p. 379), as quoted in Section 9.2

Kohlberg cared about the reasoning, not the yes or no. He placed in the highest stage the answer that Heinz should steal the drug because a human life is worth more than the druggist’s profit. Select a stage.

Two cautions

  • Reasoning is not behavior. Even people with post-conventional reasons for some choices make others for the simplest pre-conventional reasons. We might “talk the talk” but not “walk the walk.”
  • Gilligan’s critique. Kohlberg (1969) felt more men than women moved past stage 4 and that women seemed deficient in moral reasoning. His former research assistant Carol Gilligan, in In a Different Voice (1982), argued his theory was based only on upper-class White men and boys. Women are not deficient; genders reason differently, and girls and women focus more on staying connected and on relationships. Many say Heinz should not steal: if he is jailed, he and his wife are separated, and she could die while he is in prison.

Prenatal development

From a one-cell structure to birth, prenatal development follows an orderly, delicate sequence in three stages: germinal, embryonic and fetal.

  • Germinal stage (weeks 1–2). At conception a sperm fertilizes an egg to form a zygote (OpenStax Figure 9.7); its genetic makeup and sex are set at this point. The zygote divides by mitosis: 1 cell, 2, 4, 8, about 100 cells after 5 days, billions after 9 months. Mitosis is fragile: fewer than half of all zygotes survive the first two weeks (Hall, 2004).
  • Embryonic stage (weeks 3–8). After about 7–10 days of division (about 150 cells), the mass travels down the fallopian tubes and implants in the lining of the uterus, becoming an embryo. Blood vessels form the placenta, which provides nourishment and oxygen through the umbilical cord. The heart begins to beat, organs form, and the neural tube develops into the brain and spinal cord.
  • Fetal stage (weeks 9–40). At about nine weeks the embryo becomes a fetus, about the size of a kidney bean. The brain keeps growing, nearly doubling in size from weeks 16 to 28.

Prenatal influences

The developing fetus depends entirely on the person carrying it. Almost everything that person ingests, including food, liquid and medication, passes through the placenta (hence “eating for two”). Prenatal care, medical care during pregnancy that monitors the health of both the pregnant person and the fetus, reduces the risk of complications (NIH, 2013; OpenStax Figure 9.9). People planning a pregnancy may be advised to take a vitamin with folic acid, which helps prevent certain birth defects. Prenatal care is not provided equitably across populations or locations (Backes & Scrimshaw, 2020).

A teratogen is any environmental agent, biological, chemical or physical, that damages the developing embryo or fetus.

Alcohol

Not safe in any amount during pregnancy. The leading preventable cause of intellectual disabilities in U.S. children (Maier & West, 2001). Heavy drinking can cause fetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD): small head size and abnormal facial features; poor judgment and impulse control, higher rates of ADHD, learning issues and lower IQ, persisting into adulthood (Streissguth et al., 2004).

Smoking

Nicotine crosses the placenta and lowers the baby’s blood oxygen. Can cause premature birth, low birth weight, stillbirth and SIDS (CDC, 2013).

Drugs and medicines

Heroin, cocaine, methamphetamine, almost all prescription medicines and most over-the-counter medications. A baby born addicted to heroin must be weaned under medical supervision or may have seizures and die.

Other agents

Radiation; viruses such as HIV and herpes; rubella (German measles), much less common in people raised in the U.S. because most were vaccinated as children.

What do you think? Should people who use drugs during pregnancy be jailed?

In the 1990s, South Carolina’s “fetal protection” policy at the MUSC obstetrics clinic, which served mainly low-income and Medicaid patients, warned that drug use could involve the police. It deterred people from prenatal care and other services, applied only to low-income patients, and led to lawsuits. It was canceled after 5 years and 42 arrests, and a federal agency found it involved human experimentation without IRB approval. Under today’s “mandatory reporting” laws in states such as Massachusetts, Florida, California, Illinois and Utah, prosecutions are rare, yet pregnant people still avoid care and hide their substance use (Work, 2023). What were the flaws, and what are the ethics of charging pregnant people with child abuse?

Newborns: reflexes and senses

The average newborn weighs about 7.5 pounds. Newborns are not helpless: newborn reflexes, inborn automatic responses to particular stimulation, help them survive until more complex behavior develops. They appear in babies whose brains are developing normally and usually disappear around 4–5 months.

Rooting

Stroke the cheek and the baby turns toward the touch and begins to suck.

Sucking

Automatic, unlearned sucking motions of the mouth.

Grasping

The baby automatically grasps anything that touches the palm.

Moro

A response to the sensation of falling: arms spread, pull back in, and the baby (usually) cries.

Senses are not fully developed, but many built-in preferences draw infants toward people. Vision is the least developed sense, yet newborns prefer faces. Babies a few days old listen longer to voices than to non-speech sounds (Vouloumanos & Werker, 2004); 3-week-olds sucked harder on a pacifier that played their mother’s voice than a stranger’s (Mills & Melhuish, 1974). Smell is strong: more than two-thirds of breastfed 1-week-olds turned toward a pad carrying their own mother’s scent rather than a stranger’s (MacFarlane, 1978).

Infancy through childhood: physical development

Growth is rapid in infancy, toddlerhood and early childhood (OpenStax Figure 9.10), but not steady (Carel et al., 2004).

  • Body. Newborns weigh 5–10 pounds, so a 2-year-old should weigh 20–40 pounds. Average length is 19.5 inches at birth, 29.5 at 12 months and 34.4 at 2 years (WHO, 2006). Growth slows between 4 and 6 (5–7 pounds and 2–3 inches a year). At 8–9, girls outpace boys because of a pubertal growth spurt that lasts until around 12. At 10, the average girl weighs 88 pounds and the average boy 85.
  • Brain. Neurogenesis can continue into adulthood, but most neural connections form in the first few years: rapid growth called blooming. It is followed by pruning, which reduces connections and is thought to make the brain more efficient (Hutchinson, 2011). Pruning continues into adolescence. The frontal lobes (planning, reasoning, memory, impulse control) grow rapidly at 3–6, so school-age children can control attention and behavior. Brain growth spurts tend to follow Piaget’s sequence (Kolb & Whishaw, 2009).
  • Motor skills, our ability to move the body and manipulate objects, develop in order: reflexes, then holding up the head, sitting with help, sitting alone, crawling, walking. Fine motor skills use the muscles of the fingers, toes and eyes for small actions (grasping a toy, writing, using a spoon). Gross motor skills use large muscle groups for big movements (balancing, running, jumping).

Each milestone has an average age and a range. Most babies sit alone at 7 months (90% between 5 and 9 months) and hold up their head at 6 weeks (90% between 3 weeks and 4 months; not by 4 months is a delay). Delays on several milestones are a reason to talk to the pediatrician, and some can be addressed through early intervention.

Infancy through childhood: cognitive development

Piaget thought understanding objects develops slowly through experience. Today developmental psychologists think he was wrong: very young children understand objects before they have experience with them (Baillargeon, 1987; Baillargeon, Li, Gertner, & Wu, 2011).

  • Infants and toddlers. Babies shake their head “no” around 6–9 months and respond to requests like “wave bye-bye” around 9–12 months. They grasp object permanence by around 8 months, so toddlers (12–24 months) enjoy hide and seek, know people who leave will come back, point to pictures and look in the right places for objects (Loop, 2013).
  • Preschoolers (3–5). They count, name colors, give their name and age, choose an outfit, understand before and after, predict what happens next in a story, enjoy humor and pretend play, and constantly ask “Why?”
  • Theory of mind (TOM). Piaget described 2–3-year-olds as egocentric. Between 3 and 5, children come to understand that others have thoughts, feelings and beliefs different from their own, including false beliefs (Dennett, 1987; Callaghan et al., 2005). They can now tease, persuade a parent to buy candy, or see why a sibling is angry. False-belief tasks, such as the crayon-box video in the chapter’s Link to Learning, test it.
  • Middle and late childhood (6–11). Thinking becomes logical and organized for concrete information. Children understand past, present and future, so they can plan; they handle addition, subtraction and cause and effect; and they can follow game rules because they understand luck and fairness (OpenStax Figure 9.12). Attention spans stay limited until about 11, then improve through adulthood.

Language acquisition

Children everywhere learn language structures in the same order (Hatch, 1983). At birth, babies recognize their mother’s voice, tell her language from foreign ones, and prefer faces moving in sync with speech. They gesture long before they speak, and gesture use may predict later language (Iverson & Goldin-Meadow, 2005).

Nurture · B. F. Skinner

Language is learned through reinforcement and feedback: a toddler says “me juice” and gets apple juice.

Nature · Noam Chomsky (1957)

We are born with an innate capacity for language, a language acquisition device (LAD).

The verdict

Both are right: language is partly inborn and partly learned from the linguistic environment (Gleitman & Newport, 1995).

Attachment

Forming healthy attachment, a long-standing connection or bond with others, is the major social milestone of infancy.

Harlow: comfort over food

In the 1950s Harry Harlow separated newborn monkeys from their mothers and gave them two surrogates. Scientists had thought babies attach to whoever feeds them, but the monkeys clung to the soft cloth mother. Harlow (1958) concluded that comfort and security are the critical components of the mother-infant bond. His studies were done before modern ethics guidelines and are now widely considered unethical and even cruel.

Bowlby: attachment theory

John Bowlby defined attachment as the affectional bond an infant forms with the mother (1969). An infant must form it with a primary caregiver for normal social and emotional development, and the bond lasts throughout life. A secure base is a parental presence that gives the child a sense of safety while exploring (1988). Healthy attachment needs two things: a caregiver who is responsive to the child’s physical, social and emotional needs, and mutually enjoyable interactions (OpenStax Figure 9.13).

Ainsworth: the Strange Situation

Bowlby saw attachment as all-or-nothing; Mary Ainsworth (1970) showed children differ in how they bond.

Criticism: a child’s temperament may strongly influence attachment (Gervai, 2009; Harris, 2009), and attachment varies across cultures, which Ainsworth did not account for (Rothbaum et al., 2000; van IJzendoorn & Sagi-Schwartz, 2008).

Self-concept and parenting

Just as attachment is the main psychosocial milestone of infancy, a positive self-concept, an understanding of who you are, is the main one of childhood.

  1. InfantNo self-concept: reaches out to the baby in the mirror
  2. 18 moPasses the mirror test: with a red dot painted on the nose, touches their own nose (Amsterdam, 1972)
  3. 24–36 moNames or points to themself in pictures
  4. 2–4 yrMore social; enjoys playing with others but finds sharing hard; explores gender roles through play
  5. 4 yrCooperates, shares when asked, separates from parents with little anxiety; shows autonomy and carries out plans
  6. 6 yr+Describes self by group membership (“I’m a first grader!”); compares self with peers and names own traits (“I’m kind of shy”)

Children with a positive self-concept are more confident, do better in school, act more independently and try new activities (Maccoby, 1980; Ferrer & Fugate, 2003). Self-concept begins with autonomy in toddlerhood, grows with favorable comparisons in elementary school, and is re-evaluated as teens form an identity (Erikson, 1968).

Dig deeper: race, identity and PVEST

Kenneth and Mamie Clark found that African American children were more likely to choose a White doll than a Black doll, which they took as a sign of unhealthy self-concept (Clark & Clark, 1950); it was the first psychological study used in a Supreme Court case (Brown v. Board of Education). Inez Beverly Prosser found more positive self-concepts in Cincinnati’s segregated schools than in integrated ones, partly because of teachers’ low expectations (Benjamin, Henry, & McMahon, 2005). Later work found gender identity mattered more than race to children around 10 (Rogers & Meltzoff, 2017), while for minority teens racial and ethnic identity can be paramount, shaped by racial socialization from caregivers (Neblett et al., 2009).

Margaret Beale Spencer’s Phenomenological Variant of Ecological Systems Theory (PVEST) merges phenomenology (how a person makes meaning of experience) with Bronfenbrenner’s (1977) ecological systems theory (development through interactions among school, family and community). With PVEST, Spencer, Dupree and Hartmann (1997) explained how African American adolescents’ attitudes to learning related to others’ perceptions of them.

Parenting styles

Diana Baumrind (1971, 1991) described four parenting styles. Select one.

Influence runs both ways. Temperament is the set of innate traits that influence how one thinks, behaves and reacts to the environment. Easy children show positive emotions, adapt well and regulate their emotions; difficult children show negative emotions and struggle to adapt and regulate, and challenge caregivers more (Thomas, 1984). Easy children may elicit warm, responsive parenting, while irritable, withdrawn ones may irritate parents or make them withdraw (Sanson & Rothbart, 1995).

Everyday connection: play and recess

Unstructured play builds creativity, problem solving, social relationships and theory of mind (American Academy of Pediatrics, 2007). Outdoor play lets children sense the world directly, start lifelong hobbies and enjoy exercise, supporting a healthy heart and brain. Children now play outdoors less (Clements, 2004); with calorie-dense food, this contributes to childhood obesity (Karnik & Kanekar, 2012). Some schools cut recess for test preparation or remove it as punishment. Do you agree?

Adolescence

Adolescence is socially constructed. In pre-industrial societies children became adults at physical maturity; today there is an extended period, typically 12 to 18, from puberty to emerging adulthood. In the U.S. it is a time to develop independence while staying connected to parents (OpenStax Figure 9.15).

Physical development

The sequence of puberty is predictable, but its onset and pace vary widely. Adrenarche (maturing adrenal glands) and gonadarche (maturing sex glands) occur. Primary sexual characteristics are organs needed for reproduction (uterus, ovaries, testes); secondary sexual characteristics are signs of maturation that do not directly involve sex organs (breasts and hips; facial hair and a deeper voice).

Menarche is the beginning of menstrual periods; spermarche is the first ejaculation. Height depends on nature (genes) and nurture (nutrition, medications, medical conditions).

Research on transgender and gender-nonconforming youth is recent and should not be over-generalized, but youth who are supported in their identity do not show significantly more depression or anxiety than control groups, while those who are not supported show more symptoms and risk factors (Olson, Durwood, & McLaughlin, 2016).

The frontal lobes, responsible for judgment, impulse control and planning, are still maturing into early adulthood, which may explain teens’ risk-taking and emotional outbursts (Casey et al., 2005; OpenStax Figure 9.16). As neuroscientist Jay Giedd put it, it is “sort of unfair to expect [teens] to have adult levels of organizational skills or decision-making before their brains are finished being built.”

Cognitive development

More complex thinking may come from faster, more efficient processing rather than more capacity (Bjorkland, 1987; Case, 1985). Teens reach Piaget’s formal operational thought: abstract thinking, multiple points of view, hypothetical situations, debating politics, religion and justice, and questioning authority (OpenStax Figure 9.17). Cognitive empathy, the ability to take others’ perspective and feel concern for them, rises from about 13 in girls and 15 in boys (Van der Graaff et al., 2013), and more so in teens with supportive fathers (Miklikowska, Duriez, & Soenens, 2011).

Psychosocial development

Erikson’s task is identity versus role confusion: “Who am I?” and “Who do I want to be?” Some teens adopt their parents’ values; others form identities in opposition to parents but aligned with a peer group. Teens pull away and peers matter more (Shanahan et al., 2007), yet most feel positive toward their parents, and warm relationships predict better grades and fewer school problems.

G. Stanley Hall’s famous adolescent “storm and stress” is overstated. Only small numbers of teens have major conflicts with parents (Steinberg & Morris, 2001); in a study of over 1,800 parents, conflicts were about homework, money, curfews, clothing, chores and friends (Barber, 1994), and they decline as teens develop. Using fMRI, Galvan et al. (2007) found reward-center activity correlated with risk taking but not with impulsivity or risk perception, challenging the idea that adolescents are more impulsive than children or adults.

Emerging adulthood

Emerging adulthood is a newly defined period from 18 to the mid-20s, an in-between time of identity exploration focused on work and love. When does someone become an adult? Legally at 18 in the U.S.; in sociology, when self-supporting, choosing a career, marrying or starting a family. In Malawi, Njemile married at 14 and had her first child at 15, and is considered an adult; children there take on adult work as early as 10. In Western cultures, independence is taking longer and longer.

Longer lives

People in higher-income, industrialized countries live longer, freeing an extra decade to start a career and family (Arnett, 2000).

Work

A high school diploma no longer opens the economic ladder; degrees are needed even for entry-level jobs, and many take 5–6 years to finish while working.

Boomerang Generation

Graduates without adequate jobs return to the parental home (Davidson, 2014).

Culture (perhaps most important)

Young people spend more time exploring options, changing majors and jobs and delaying marriage and work.

Adulthood

Adulthood begins around 20 and has three stages, each with its own rewards and challenges.

StageAgesPhysical changes

Exercise, nutrition and an active lifestyle can minimize these declines (OpenStax Figure 9.18); the chapter’s example is Neil Unger, who took up skateboarding at 60.

Cognitive development

Adult cognitive development may be even more active than in infancy and early childhood (Fischer, Yan, & Stewart, 2003). Unlike physical abilities, which peak in the mid-20s and slowly decline, cognitive abilities stay steady through early and middle adulthood.

  • Crystallized intelligence (information, skills and strategies gathered through experience) holds steady and may even improve; intelligence-test scores are stable to rising until the mid-30s to mid-50s (Bayley & Oden, 1955).
  • Fluid intelligence (information processing, reasoning and memory) begins to decline and slow in late adulthood.
  • Delaying decline: mentally and physically stimulating activities (mahjong, chess, reading, crosswords) are linked to less cognitive decline and less mild cognitive impairment and dementia (Hertzog et al., 2009; Larson et al., 2006; Podewils et al., 2005; OpenStax Figure 9.19).
  • The aging brain: older adults judging the direction of moving dots were slower and less accurate because of degenerated corticostriatal connections (Forstmann et al., 2011). Children aged 6–7 and adults over 80 show similar spatial representations, from immature neurology and neurodegeneration respectively (Ruggiero, D’Errico, & Iachini, 2016).

Dementia is a set of brain changes that harm cognition. Alzheimer’s disease, one type, was initially studied by Solomon Carter Fuller and has a genetic basis: plaques from cell death cause severe forgetfulness, until a person may forget how to walk, talk and eat. Risk rises with exposure to lead, iron and zinc and falls with a Mediterranean diet (Arora, Mittal, & Kakkar, 2015). There is no cure, but cognitive rehabilitation can offset mild cognitive impairment, which can evolve into dementia; virtual reality built around daily living, memory and language is one proposed method (Garcia-Betances et al., 2015).

Psychosocial development

Healthy aging involves activity, social connection and culture. George Vaillant (2002), analyzing over 50 years of data, argued that we need to keep finding meaning.

  • Work (Erikson’s generativity): earnings peak in adulthood, but job satisfaction depends more on contact with people, interesting work, chances for advancement and some independence (Mohr & Zoghi, 2006) than on salary (Iyengar, Wells, & Schwartz, 2006).
  • Family (Erikson’s intimacy): positive relationships contribute to well-being (Ryff & Singer, 2009). Most U.S. adults define themselves through spouses, children and parents; adult children tend to boost parents’ well-being (Umberson et al., 2010), and a stable marriage contributes too (Vaillant, 2002).
  • Social support: socioemotional selectivity theory says that as we age our friendships dwindle in number but stay as close, or closer, than before (Carstensen, 1992; OpenStax Figure 9.20).

Death and Dying

Every story has an ending. Culture and personal background shape how we view death, how we grieve, and how we plan for the end of life.

Learning objectives

  • Discuss hospice care
  • Describe the five stages of grief
  • Critique issues regarding living wills, Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) orders, and hospice care
Read Section 9.4 on OpenStax ↗

Hospice

Some cultures accept death as a natural part of life (OpenStax Figure 9.21 shows burial, cremation and burial at sea, and a Hindu cremation in Bali). In the U.S., until about 50 years ago, a doctor might not tell someone they were dying, and most deaths happened in hospitals. Hospice changed that: its aim is a death with dignity, with pain management in a humane, comfortable setting, usually outside a hospital.

For the patient

High satisfaction, because they can stay at home and are not completely dependent on strangers (Brumley et al., 2007). Hospice patients tend to live longer than non-hospice patients (Connor et al., 2007; Temel et al., 2010).

For the family

Emotional support, regular updates on treatment and condition, and a lighter burden of care (McMillan et al., 2006). Patients and families both report more family and social support and better coping (Godkin, Krant, & Doster, 1984).

Kübler-Ross’s five stages of grief

Elizabeth Kübler-Ross (1969), who worked with hospice’s founders, described how a person comes to accept their own death in five stages.

Coping and meaning

Not everyone facing death, or the loss of a loved one, feels the negative emotions in the Kübler-Ross model (Nolen-Hoeksema & Larson, 1999). People with religious or spiritual beliefs often cope better, through hope in an afterlife and support from religious or spiritual communities (Hood et al., 1996; McIntosh, Silver, & Wortman, 1993; Wortman & Park, 2008).

Randy Pausch, a Carnegie Mellon professor, was diagnosed with terminal pancreatic cancer in his mid-40s and given 3–6 months to live. Instead of anger and depression, he gave his famous last lecture, “Really Achieving Your Childhood Dreams,” on seeing the good in others, overcoming obstacles and experiencing zero gravity. He lived his final year with joy and hope: plans for the future still matter, even when we know we are dying.

Living wills, DNR orders and health care proxies

Some people want their wishes known in advance, in case they become incapacitated or can no longer speak for themselves. The desire for these documents is often shaped by religion, culture and upbringing.
